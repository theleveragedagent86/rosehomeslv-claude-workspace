## BUILD SHAPE THIS ROUND (new, applies to whichever one you pick)

Ryan's spec: **a new image every 3 to 7 seconds, motion graphics only in the very first shot**, same as *He Died 1 Minute Before Peace*.

What that means in build terms for a 12 minute episode:

| | Reference episode (final-minutes-ww1) | This round |
|---|---|---|
| Scenes | 150 | ~130 to 160 |
| Unique stills | 36 (heavily reused via Ken Burns) | **~130 to 160, one per scene, no reuse** |
| Kling motion clips | 9 (90 credits) | **1** (cold open only, 10 credits) |
| Everything else | KENBURNS on repeated stills | KENBURNS / static on unique stills |

Net effect: **Kling spend drops from 90 credits to 10**, and image generation goes up roughly 4x. That is the cheap direction, since stills cost a fraction of a 5 second Kling clip. The real constraint moves from credits to **whether the story has 150 genuinely different things to draw**, which is why every pitch below carries a still-supply rating. Thin-supply stories get repetitive fast in this format and were cut.

The cold open animated shot is now the only motion in the entire video, so it has to be **one clear single action**. Each pitch names its shot.

## 1. The Man Who Invented The Battle Of Midway | Codename History

- **Hook:** For fifty years, the most famous five minutes in naval history came from one man who was not even flying that day, and who made most of it up.
- **The known battle:** The Battle of Midway, 4 June 1942.
- **The fresh angle:** Nearly everything the public knows about Midway traces to Mitsuo Fuchida, the Pearl Harbor strike leader, whose 1955 account gave us the image of Japanese flight decks packed with fueled, armed aircraft five minutes from launching when the American dive bombers arrived. Fuchida was not flying at Midway. He had an emergency appendectomy days earlier and watched from Akagi's bridge. Japanese carrier operating records show no full strike was ever spotted on those decks, because the carriers were cycling combat air patrol and the aircraft were still in the hangars. The "fateful five minutes" is a literary invention that got laundered into US Navy institutional memory, into textbooks, and into the movies. The episode is about how the losing navy's most charming storyteller wrote the winner's version of events.
- **War / tier / runtime:** WW2 / Standard / ~13 min.
- **Sources (verified real, tier-labeled):** Jonathan Parshall, "Reflecting on Fuchida, or 'A Tale of Three Whoppers'", Naval War College Review 63:2, Spring 2010 [Tier 1] (https://digital-commons.usnwc.edu/nwc-review/vol63/iss2/10/); Parshall and Tully, *Shattered Sword: The Untold Story of the Battle of Midway*, Potomac Books [Tier 1] (https://www.nebraskapress.unl.edu/potomac-books/9781574889239/shattered-sword/); Naval History and Heritage Command, "Japanese Story of the Battle of Midway" (ONI translation of the Nagumo Report) [Tier 1] (https://www.history.navy.mil/research/library/online-reading-room/title-list-alphabetically/j/japanese-story-of-the-battle-of-midway.html); Fuchida and Okumiya, "Five Fateful Minutes at Midway", USNI Proceedings, June 1955 [cited as the object of study, not as authority] (https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1955/june/five-fateful-minutes-midway).
- **Accuracy caveat:** Do not overclaim. Nagumo genuinely was rearming aircraft in the hangars, which is exactly why the bomb hits were so lethal. The correction is specifically that no strike was spotted and ready on the flight decks and that a launch was not minutes away. Parshall's article drew a published rebuttal, so present this as the strong scholarly consensus and say it was argued, not that it is uncontested. Avoid implying his later religious life is evidence about his sourcing.
- **Straight-tone beats:** Torpedo Squadron 8 and the other torpedo squadrons annihilated. Japanese sailors trapped in burning hangar decks. The carriers scuttled with men aboard. The execution of captured American airmen after the battle, if included, fully straight.
- **Still-image supply: STRONG.** Fuchida on a hospital cot aboard Akagi; the 1955 Proceedings article as a physical magazine page; Nagumo's four carriers from above; an Akagi cutaway separating hangar deck from flight deck; deck crews wheeling torpedoes back to the racks; scout plane Tone No. 4 launching late; the near-empty flight deck with only CAP Zeros, which is the visual thesis of the episode; Torpedo Squadron 8 going in low and alone; the dive bombers rolling in; the Hollywood myth frame next to the records frame.
- **Thumbnail + cold open:** Thumbnail: split frame, left is the burning packed carrier deck everyone pictures with a red X, right is a nearly empty deck with a green check, text "THIS NEVER HAPPENED". Cold open (the one animated shot): a deck crewman rolls a torpedo across Akagi's hangar deck and it clangs into a stack of unsecured bombs. Camera holds.

## 2. The Biggest Tank Battle In History Did Not Happen | Codename History

- **Hook:** Everyone has heard that 1,500 tanks smashed into each other at Prokhorovka. The Germans wrote off about twenty, and most of the Soviet losses came from driving into a ditch the Soviets dug themselves.
- **The known battle:** The Battle of Kursk, July 1943, specifically Prokhorovka on 12 July.
- **The fresh angle:** The Prokhorovka legend, roughly 800 tanks a side and 400 German tanks destroyed, comes from General Pavel Rotmistrov, who needed to explain to Stalin why his 5th Guards Tank Army had been gutted in a single morning. Archival work puts the real engagement near 616 Soviet and 294 German tanks, with 5th Guards losing about 359 against German losses of roughly 193 damaged or lost and only around twenty written off. A large share of the Soviet loss came from tanks attacking downhill at speed into a Soviet-built anti-tank ditch roughly 4.5 metres deep that their own crews had not been told about, then queuing for one crossing under fire. Stalin did not fully buy the report and sent Malenkov to investigate. Then in 2019 historian Ben Wheatley found Luftwaffe reconnaissance photographs in the US National Archives, taken days later while the Germans still held the ground, showing the wrecks, the ditch, and Hill 252.2. The episode is a battlefield report that became a national myth, and the aerial photographs that finally called it.
- **War / tier / runtime:** WW2 / Standard / ~13 min.
- **Sources (verified real, tier-labeled):** The Tank Museum, "Prokhorovka and the Myth of the Largest Tank Battle" [Tier 1] (https://tankmuseum.org/article/prokhorovka-myth-largest-tank-battle); Ben Wheatley, "A visual examination of the battle of Prokhorovka", Journal of Intelligence History 18:2, 2019, peer reviewed [Tier 1] (https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/16161262.2019.1606545); Valeriy Zamulin, "Soviet Troop Losses in the Battle of Prokhorovka, 10-16 July 1943", Journal of Slavic Military Studies 32:1, 2019 [Tier 1] (https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13518046.2019.1552735); Zamulin, *Demolishing the Myth: The Tank Battle at Prokhorovka* [Tier 1] (https://archive.org/details/demolishingmytht0000zamu); Karl-Heinz Frieser et al., *Germany and the Second World War, Vol. VIII*, Oxford University Press [Tier 1].
- **Accuracy caveat:** Two traps. First, "twenty" is German write-offs, meaning total losses, not damaged tanks. German damaged counts were far higher, and the Germans held the field so they could recover and repair, which is part of why the ratio looks lopsided. Loss figures vary between Wheatley, Frieser and Zamulin, so say "roughly" and give the range. Second, and say it out loud in the script, **the corrected numbers do not make Kursk a German victory.** The pincers never closed, Germany lost the campaign and lost the initiative in the east permanently. This fact gets misused and the episode has to close that door. Zamulin, a Russian historian, is himself on record that the ditch failure is incomprehensible, so this is not a Western-versus-Russian argument.
- **Straight-tone beats:** Soviet crews burning inside tanks stopped dead in the ditch. Over 3,000 Soviet personnel lost in the day against roughly 560 German. The wider Kursk casualty total. Rotmistrov's motive for lying was that his army was gutted, and what that implies about the men is not funny.
- **Still-image supply: STRONG, but it needs deliberate work** to avoid a monotonous field. It carries on the non-battlefield material: the Kursk salient map with both pincers; Hitler postponing Citadel to wait for Panthers; the Panther, Ferdinand and Tiger as promised wonder-weapons; the recon aircraft camera bay; the grainy Luftwaffe frames with annotation callouts; Hill 252.2 and the Oktyabrsky state farm; an anti-tank ditch cross-section with a T-34 nose-down in it; tanks stacked behind a single crossing; Rotmistrov writing the report; Stalin reading it; Malenkov's commission arriving; the enormous Soviet monument built on the legend; the archive box at College Park.
- **Thumbnail + cold open:** Thumbnail: a huge painted wall of clashing tanks fading out to a nearly empty field with a handful of wrecks, text "20 TANKS." Cold open (the one animated shot): a T-34 crests a low rise at full speed and the camera holds still as it drops nose-first out of frame into the ditch, dust rolling over it.

## 3. The Allies Bombed An Empty Monastery, Then Fought The Fortress They Made | Codename History

- **Hook:** They flattened a 1,400 year old abbey to kill Germans who were not in it, and by lunchtime the rubble was the best defensive position in Italy.
- **The known battle:** Monte Cassino, and the bombing of the abbey on 15 February 1944.
- **The fresh angle:** Three things most viewers do not know. The abbey was effectively empty of German combat troops when it was bombed, and the German forces camped on the mountain below were not harmed by the raid at all, while American newspapers kept printing that the Nazis had been using it. Four months earlier, two officers of the Hermann Goring Division, Lieutenant Colonel Julius Schlegel and Captain Maximilian Becker, had talked the abbot into letting them evacuate the abbey's library and art, roughly 700 crates in about 100 truckloads, to Vatican property. So the Germans emptied it and the Allies bombed the empty box. And the raid achieved nothing because it was not synchronised with the ground attack: the bombers went in on 15 February, the infantry assault did not go in until the night of 17 February, and by then German paratroopers of the 1st Parachute Division had moved into the ruins and fortified them. The Allies then spent three more months taking a position they had built.
- **War / tier / runtime:** WW2 / Standard / ~13 min.
- **Sources (verified real, tier-labeled):** The National WWII Museum, "The Destruction of Monte Cassino" [Tier 1] (https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/destruction-of-monte-cassino-1944); UK Ministry of Defence official battlefield guide, "The Battles For Monte Cassino, 12 January to 5 June 1944" [Tier 1] (https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/30053/ww2_montecass.pdf); US Army Center of Military History, *Cassino to the Alps*, CMH Pub 6-4 [Tier 1] (https://www.history.army.mil/html/books/006/6-4-1/CMH_Pub_6-4-1.pdf); Matthew Parker, *Monte Cassino: The Hardest-Fought Battle of World War II*, and Peter Caddick-Adams, *Monte Cassino: Ten Armies in Hell* [Tier 1 credentialed authors].
- **Accuracy caveat:** Do not use the popular "mistranslation" story as fact, the one where an intercept saying the abbot is with the monks was misread because Abt looks like the abbreviation for Abteilung, meaning battalion. It traces to Colonel David Hunt's memoir and is contested by Field Marshal Lord Carver among others. Present it as disputed or leave it out. Civilian deaths in the abbey are given anywhere from roughly 115 to over 200, and bomb tonnage differs across accounts, so give ranges. Do not launder Schlegel into a clean hero without noting he was held seven months postwar as a suspected looter before Cassino's own monks testified for him.
- **Straight-tone beats:** The Italian refugees sheltering in the abbey who were killed by the raid. The 11,000 Allied dead already spent on the sector before the bombing. The Polish II Corps assault in May and its cemetery on the hill. The destruction of the Benedictine mother house itself, played as loss, never as a gag.
- **Still-image supply: STRONG.** The abbey intact above the Liri valley; the Gustav Line across the peninsula; monks and refugees in the cloisters; Schlegel's trucks loading crates by torchlight; the crates stacked in Castel Sant'Angelo; troops in freezing rock sangars unable to dig into stone; B-17s opening bomb bays; the abbey mid-collapse in a column of white dust; Fallschirmjager moving into the ruins with an MG42 in a broken arch; the Royal Sussex attacking Point 593 alone; the flooded cratered valley that stopped the tanks; the Polish flag on the ruins on 18 May; the rebuilt abbey.
- **Thumbnail + cold open:** Thumbnail: intact abbey on one side, smoking rubble on the other, a German paratrooper's helmet rising out of the rubble half, text "IT WAS EMPTY." Cold open (the one animated shot): a locked-off wide of the abbey as the first bombs land and the whole structure disappears into a rising wall of white dust.

## 4. America Would Not Turn Off Its Lights, So U-Boats Sank Ships In Sight Of The Beach | Codename History

- **Hook:** For six months in 1942, the easiest way to find an American tanker was to look for the Ferris wheel.
- **The known battle:** The Battle of the Atlantic, specifically the U-boat campaign in American coastal waters in 1942, Operation Paukenschlag, known to the U-boat crews as the Second Happy Time.
- **The fresh angle:** The Battle of the Atlantic is always told as mid-ocean convoy versus wolfpack. Its worst stretch happened within sight of American beaches and was largely self-inflicted. Donitz opened with only five long-range boats. Coastal cities kept their lights burning well into 1942, so merchant ships were backlit against the glow and reduced to silhouettes, and there was no interlocking coastal convoy system until spring. Off North Carolina alone roughly 90 ships went down and about 1,600 people died. The stopgap defence was genuinely absurd: the Civil Air Patrol flew privately owned light planes with bombs strapped under the wings, and the Coast Guard's Coastal Picket Patrol, nicknamed the Hooligan Navy, put civilian yachts and fishing boats out on the water, Hemingway among them, carrying grenades and a plan to lure a U-boat to the surface. Losses collapsed the moment convoys and a dim-out arrived, which is the whole argument in one graph.
- **War / tier / runtime:** WW2 / Standard / ~12 to 13 min.
- **Sources (verified real, tier-labeled):** US Naval History and Heritage Command, H-Gram 008-5 [Tier 1] (https://www.history.navy.mil/about-us/leadership/director/directors-corner/h-grams/h-gram-008/h-008-5.html); NOAA Monitor National Marine Sanctuary, Battle of the Atlantic shipwrecks including U-352 and U-701 [Tier 1] (https://monitor.noaa.gov/shipwrecks/ and https://monitor.noaa.gov/shipwrecks/u-701.html); NOAA, "A first look at two WWII Battle of the Atlantic shipwrecks" [Tier 1] (https://www.noaa.gov/media-release/first-look-at-two-wwii-battle-of-atlantic-shipwrecks); Civil Air Patrol Coastal Patrol Operations 1942-1943, DTIC official report [Tier 1] (https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/trecms/pdf/AD1145587.pdf); Imperial War Museums, "Battle of the Atlantic Explained" [Tier 1] (https://www.iwm.org.uk/history/the-battle-of-the-atlantic-explained).
- **Accuracy caveat:** Do not run the popular version where Admiral Ernest King refused convoys out of spite and anglophobia. NHHC's own account is that King, Ingersoll and Andrews argued badly escorted convoys would concentrate targets and make sinkings easier, and that the escorts genuinely did not exist yet. The reasoning was wrong and the record shows it was wrong, but it was reasoning, not malice, and correcting that is the better beat. The blackout was a dim-out that came in stages, not one national switch. Wider ship and casualty totals vary by how you define the theatre and date range, so anchor to the North Carolina numbers, which are firm, and label the rest approximate.
- **Straight-tone beats:** Merchant mariners burning to death in oil fires within swimming distance of shore. The roughly 1,200 merchant mariner deaths off North Carolina. Bodies and wreckage washing onto tourist beaches. Merchant sailors were civilians. The absurdity lives entirely in the policy room and the Hooligan Navy, never in the water.
- **Still-image supply: STRONG.** A neon beach hotel strip glowing over black water; a tanker silhouetted in a periscope reticle; U-123's conning tower; a chart of the five Drumbeat boats fanning out; the burning Dixie Arrow; a Piper Cub with a depth charge under the wing over the Outer Banks; a Coast Guard picket sailboat with a machine gun bolted to the cabin; Hemingway's Pilar with the coffin-shaped bomb; the wooden ops board at Eastern Sea Frontier headquarters; the bucket-brigade daylight-hop convoy diagram; a modern NOAA sonar image of U-576 and Bluefields lying a few hundred yards apart on the seabed.
- **Thumbnail + cold open:** Thumbnail: a burning tanker on the horizon, a lit boardwalk Ferris wheel in the foreground, text "THE LIGHTS STAYED ON." Cold open (the one animated shot): a periscope rises out of calm black water until the lens breaks the surface and a far shoreline of city lights snaps into focus.

## 5. Britain Surrendered 130,000 Men To An Army That Was Running Out Of Bullets | Codename History

- **Hook:** The largest surrender in British military history was handed to a smaller, hungrier army one good counterattack from collapse, and everyone has spent eighty years blaming the wrong thing.
- **The known battle:** The Fall of Singapore, 8 to 15 February 1942.
- **The fresh angle:** Two corrections carry it. First, the thing everyone repeats, that Singapore's big guns pointed the wrong way and could only fire out to sea, is false. Several of the heavy guns had full traverse and did fire north at the Japanese. The real failure was ammunition: the magazines held armour-piercing shells built to punch through warship armour, which bury themselves in soft ground and do almost nothing to infantry, and there were very few high explosive shells. Second, Yamashita's 25th Army was outnumbered, overextended and low on ammunition by the time it reached the city. At the surrender table his main worry was that Percival would realise how weak he was, so he pounded the table and demanded an immediate yes. Percival said yes. Over 130,000 troops, about 15,000 of them Australian, went into captivity. A fortress lost to a bluff, and a myth invented afterward to explain it.
- **War / tier / runtime:** WW2 / Standard / ~13 min.
- **Sources (verified real, tier-labeled):** Imperial War Museums, "Why did Singapore fall?" [Tier 1] (https://www.iwm.org.uk/history/why-did-singapore-fall); Australian War Memorial, "Remembering 1942: The Fall of Singapore" [Tier 1] (https://www.awm.gov.au/articles/blog/1942-fall-of-singapore); Australian Army Journal, "'Out-generalled, Outwitted, and Outfought': Generals Percival and Bennett in Malaya, 1941-42" [Tier 1] (https://researchcentre.army.gov.au/library/australian-army-journal-aaj/volume-2-number-1-2004/out-generalled-outwitted-and-outfought-generals-percival-and-bennett-malaya-1941-42); Lionel Wigmore, *The Japanese Thrust*, Australian official history vol. IV [Tier 1] (https://www.awm.gov.au/collection/RCDIG1070203); National Library Board Singapore, "Operation Sook Ching" [Tier 1] (https://www.nlb.gov.sg/main/article-detail?cmsuuid=cc4da337-3bcd-4f96-bdc6-5210646bdd90).
- **Accuracy caveat:** Do not overclaim the guns correction. Not all guns could traverse fully, and even with HE shells there was no forward fire direction, so they would likely have been of limited use. The honest framing is "the guns were not the reason, and here is what actually was." Yamashita's troop numbers and ammunition state vary between the Japanese and Australian official histories, so give a range and attribute it. Criticise Percival for real documented decisions, such as refusing to build northern defences on morale grounds, not for cowardice.
- **Straight-tone beats:** Everything after the surrender. The Alexandra Hospital massacre. The Sook Ching purge of Singapore's Chinese population from 18 February, with estimates from tens of thousands upward and no agreed figure. The POW camps, the Burma Railway, and the roughly 7,000 Australian prisoners who died in captivity. Zero comedy in the final third of the episode. **This one has the heaviest straight-tone load of the five.**
- **Still-image supply: STRONG.** A 15 inch gun turret rotating to face north; a cutaway of an armour-piercing shell burying itself in mud next to an HE shell bursting; bicycle infantry moving down the peninsula; the causeway blown and then repaired; the reservoirs; the Ford Factory surrender room with both delegations at a table; Yamashita's fist on the table; the column of prisoners marching to Changi; Churchill reading the cable in London; Australian newspaper front pages. Jungle, city, sea, boardroom and archive give it enormous variety.
- **Thumbnail + cold open:** Thumbnail: a giant coastal gun swinging inland with the sea behind it, text "THE GUNS WORKED FINE." Cold open (the one animated shot): at the surrender table, Yamashita slams his fist down and the water glasses jump.

## Manager's pick

**#1, Midway / Fuchida.** Biggest built-in search audience of the five, the title promise reads without any prior knowledge, the payoff is a person rather than a statistic which suits the OverSimplified voice, and it is the cleanest sourcing job in the set (peer-reviewed Naval War College Review plus a university press book that argue the correction explicitly). Still supply is strong without needing rescue.

**Close second: #2 Prokhorovka.** The most total, most provable reversal on the list and the strongest accuracy-moat episode. Slight ding only because the battlefield itself is visually repetitive under a stills-only build, so its frame count leans on maps, recon photos and the cover-up sequence.

**Best pure comedy-to-stakes ratio: #4, the Atlantic lights.** Ferris wheels, Hemingway in a fishing boat with grenades, and a policy room that is the whole joke, with the water kept completely straight.

## Runners-up (verified, available on request)

- **Weeks After Winning The Battle Of Britain, Britain Fired The Men Who Won It** (the Dowding System was the real weapon, radar was only the front door, and Dowding and Park were both removed within weeks). Standard, ~12 min. Tier 1 IWM and RAF Museum. Cut only because the pitch needed theatre spread. Caveat: Dowding's removal had roughly seven documented causes, so the Big Wing row was the final push, not the sole cause.
- **Stalingrad Was Not Lost In Stalingrad** (Operation Uranus hit the Romanian, Italian and Hungarian flanks, and Goring's airlift promise averaged around 105 tons a day against a requirement in the hundreds). Standard, ~13 min. Tier 1 IWM, Joel Hayward in Airpower Journal, Army University Press. Caveat: do not run the cheap "the Romanians ran" beat, they repelled the first attacks and had requested anti-tank guns they never got.
- **Britain Had The Entire German Plan For Crete And Lost Crete Anyway** (Ultra gave Freyberg the airfields and the date, then Maleme turned on a communications blackout and a refused reinforcement). Standard, ~13 min. Tier 1 NZHistory, IWM, UK National Archives. Caveat: Freyberg's handling of Ultra is a live controversy, not a settled failure, and the Kondomari and Kandanos reprisals are a heavy straight-tone block.
- **The Island That Was Supposed To Take One Day** (Tarawa's apogean neap tide left Marines wading 800 yards, and Norman Hatch's footage of American dead went to theatres with Roosevelt's personal approval, won an Oscar, and triggered a public backlash). Standard, ~12 min. Tier 1 USMC History Division, National WWII Museum, National Archives. Caveat: the famous Shibasaki "million men, hundred years" quote is poorly attributed, drop it or flag it.
