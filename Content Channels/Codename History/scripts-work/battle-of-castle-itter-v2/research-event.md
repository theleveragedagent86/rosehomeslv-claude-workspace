# Research Report: The Battle for Castle Itter, 5 May 1945 (EVENT TRACK)

## One-paragraph summary

Schloss Itter, a rebuilt 19th-century castle above the village of Itter in the Austrian Tyrol, was requisitioned by the SS on 7 February 1943 and run from 25 April 1943 as "SS-Sonderkommando Schloss Itter," a subcamp administered by Dachau. It held French VIP "honor prisoners" (two former prime ministers, two former commanders-in-chief, a Wimbledon champion, a trade-union chief, Charles de Gaulle's sister and others), guarded by a small SS-Totenkopf detachment and served by up to 27 concentration-camp forced labourers drawn from Dachau and Flossenbürg. Between 30 April and 4 May 1945 the SS command structure collapsed: the last Dachau commandant, Eduard Weiter, shot himself at the castle on 2 May, and commandant Sebastian Wimmer fled at dawn on 4 May. Two prisoners went for help on bicycles, the Croatian electrician Zvonimir Čučković on 3 May and the Czech cook Andreas Krobot on 4 May. Krobot's message reached Wehrmacht Major Josef "Sepp" Gangl in Wörgl, who had already gone over to the Austrian resistance. Gangl drove to Kufstein under a white flag, met 1st Lt. John C. "Jack" Lee Jr. of Company B, 23rd Tank Battalion, 12th Armored Division, and the two led a mixed American and German column to the castle on the evening of 4 May. On 5 May roughly 100 to 150 Waffen-SS attacked. Americans, Wehrmacht soldiers, one Waffen-SS officer, an Austrian resistance teenager and five armed French prisoners held the castle for most of the day. Gangl was killed. Lee's Sherman "Besotten Jenny" was destroyed. Relief from Company E, 2nd Battalion, 142nd Infantry Regiment, 36th Infantry Division, with Shermans of the 753rd Tank Battalion, arrived just after 4 PM. It was the second and last documented occasion in the European war when US and Wehrmacht troops fought side by side, and the only known case of an active Waffen-SS officer fighting alongside Allied troops.

## Verified timeline

### Background

- **7 February 1943** - SS-Obergruppenführer Oswald Pohl requisitions Schloss Itter "for special SS use" on Himmler's order. [Harding, *The Last Battle*, ch. 1; German Wikipedia, *Schloss Itter*; English Wikipedia, *Itter Castle*]
- **25 April 1943** - The site opens as **SS-Sonderkommando Schloss Itter**, listed by the KZ-Gedenkstätte Dachau exhibition catalogue among the Dachau subcamps: "Itter, Österreich / SS-Sonderkommando Schloss Itter / 1943-1945 / bis 27 [Häftlinge] / SS (Bauarbeiten, Bedienung von Sonderhäftlingen)." [Haus der Bayerischen Geschichte / KZ-Gedenkstätte Dachau subcamp list PDF; German Wikipedia]
- **2 May 1943** - First French VIP prisoners arrive: Édouard Daladier, Maurice Gamelin, Léon Jouhaux. Harding says roughly 18 "honor" prisoners passed through over two years. [Harding, ch. 2 and ch. 3]
- **Summer 1944** - The forced-labour prisoners plan an escape to Switzerland that would have taken the VIPs with them. "Die Prominenten weigerten sich" (the prominent prisoners refused), and the plan collapsed. [German Wikipedia, citing Koop, *In Hitlers Hand*]

### The collapse

- **30 April 1945** - SS-Obersturmbannführer Eduard Weiter, last commandant of Dachau, arrives at Itter with staff, wives and children. [Harding, ch. 5; English Wikipedia, *Eduard Weiter*]
- **2 May 1945** - Weiter shoots himself at the castle. Harding: "near the heart, and then finished himself off with a second shot behind the ear." Reynaud's account has him drunkenly boasting about executions he had ordered at Dachau shortly before. Tom Segev has suggested he may instead have been shot by another SS man. Buried in an unmarked grave just outside the walls. [Harding, ch. 5; English Wikipedia]
- **3 May 1945, c. 1:30 PM** - Zvonimir Čučković leaves on a bicycle under cover of an errand ordered by Wimmer, carrying an English-language letter written by Christiane Mabire. He rides roughly 40 miles / 70 km toward Innsbruck through German checkpoints. That evening he reaches the outskirts of Innsbruck and finds lead elements of the 103rd Infantry Division (Maj. Gen. Anthony C. McAuliffe), specifically the 409th Infantry Regiment. [Harding, ch. 5; English and German Wikipedia; Britannica]
- **4 May 1945, dawn** - Wimmer flees with his wife, having first asked SS-Hauptsturmführer Kurt-Siegfried Schrader to look after the VIPs. The remaining guards leave. The freed VIPs break into the unguarded weapons room and arm themselves. [Harding, ch. 5; Harding, *World War II* magazine 2008 / HistoryNet]
- **4 May 1945, morning** - An American rescue party under Major John Kramers (103rd ID) sets out from Innsbruck with Čučković but is stopped by SS resistance and shelling near Jenbach and turns back. [Harding, ch. 6; English Wikipedia]
- **4 May 1945, midday (c. 11:00)** - Andreas Krobot, a Czech prisoner working as the castle cook, cycles to Wörgl with a second English-language plea written by Mabire. He is pulled off the street by Gangl, who is displaying an Austrian flag. [Harding, ch. 5; English Wikipedia]
- **4 May 1945, c. 3 PM** - Gangl and his driver, Corporal Keblitsch, drive a Kübelwagen with a white flag about seven miles to Kufstein. After roughly 45 minutes they meet four M4 Shermans. Lee reads Mabire's letter and commits to the rescue. [Harding, ch. 5]
- **4 May 1945, just before 7 PM** - The column leaves Kufstein: "Lee and *Besotten Jenny* took the lead, followed by *Boche Buster* and the five 753rd TB Shermans." At Wörgl it is cut down because a bridge will not take the weight. "When the relief column left Wörgl to continue the journey to Schloss Itter, Lee commanded two Sherman tanks, fourteen American soldiers, and a kübelwagen and small Mercedes truck carrying a total of ten Germans." *Boche Buster* is then left at the Bruggberg bridge to disarm demolition charges and hold the crossing. En route the force clears an SS roadblock. [Harding, ch. 6]
- **4 May 1945, nightfall** - The column reaches the castle. *Besotten Jenny* is parked covering the main gate. [Harding 2008 / HistoryNet; Harding, ch. 6]

### The battle, 5 May 1945

- **c. 4:00 AM** - Lee is woken by gunfire. A small SS party with grappling hooks is driven back off the slope. [Harding, ch. 7]
- **c. 6:00 AM** - A lull. [The Unravel, summarising Harding]
- **8:30 AM** - Lee sees, roughly 800 yards to the northwest, a 20mm anti-aircraft cannon and an 88mm gun being sited. Trucks from Hopfgarten unload "between 100 and 150" Waffen-SS in camouflage, including men of the 17th SS Panzergrenadier Division. [Harding, ch. 7]
- **after 10:00 AM** - An 88mm round strikes the third floor of the keep. Two more German soldiers and the Austrian resistance youth Hans Waltl arrive by Kübelwagen, the only reinforcement Gangl's phone call to Alois Mayr produces. [Harding, ch. 7; English Wikipedia]
- **c. 10:00 AM** - *Besotten Jenny* is knocked out. Harding attributes it to an antitank round, "likely 75mm Pak 40," striking the left side, killing the engine and setting the tank on fire. Crewman Rushford gets out; Pollock and Worsham survive by diving into the ravine. No one in the tank is killed. [Harding, ch. 7]
- **During the morning/midday** - Gangl is shot dead by a sniper in the front courtyard while trying to pull Reynaud out of an exposed position. Two of Gangl's Wehrmacht soldiers are seriously wounded. [Harding, ch. 7; DÖW; German Wikipedia]
- **c. noon** - Ammunition nearly gone. Harding 2008: "By noon the American-German force was almost out of ammunition." Machine-gunner McHaley has burned through all but a few belts of 1,300 rounds of .30 calibre. [Harding 2008; Harding, ch. 7]
- **early afternoon** - Jean Borotra, aged 57, volunteers, goes over the wall disguised "as an Austrian refugee, complete with ragged bedroll and gnarled walking stick," crosses a waist-deep stream and runs the SS lines to reach the Americans. He then guides and accompanies a platoon assault that kills two Waffen-SS and captures twelve without a US casualty. [Harding, ch. 7]
- **c. 1:00 PM** - The relief force leaves Wörgl: Company E of the 142nd Infantry with elements of Companies F and G, plus tanks of the 753rd Tank Battalion including *Boche Buster*. Captain Joe W. Gill commands Company E; Lt. Col. Marvin J. Coyle commands the 2nd Battalion; Col. George Lynch commands the regiment. [Harding, ch. 7; Harding 2008]
- **just after 4:00 PM** - *Boche Buster* appears at the village end of the Schlossweg. The siege ends. Lee's greeting to the relief: "What kept you?" [Harding, ch. 7; Roberts, *Daily Beast*]

### Aftermath

- **5 May 1945, c. 7 PM** - The French VIPs leave by jeep for Innsbruck. Col. Lynch sets up his 142nd Infantry regimental CP in the castle and brokers a local ceasefire through General Georg Ritter von Hengl. [Harding, ch. 8]
- **6 May** - The French are feted by Gen. McAuliffe at Innsbruck, then move to Augsburg and dine with Gen. Alexander Patch.
- **7 May** - Gen. Jean de Lattre de Tassigny arranges their return to France. Germany's unconditional surrender is signed at Reims.
- **8 May** - Most fly from Strasbourg to Paris in de Gaulle's aircraft; Daladier drives separately with his son. [Harding, ch. 8. English Wikipedia says 10 May; prefer Harding.]
- **26 May 1945** - The 12th Armored Division newspaper *Hellcat News* (Vol. 3, No. 2) runs "12th Men Free French Big-Wigs," reporting 14 French notables freed on 4 to 5 May. [Lone Sentry archive]
- **29 August 1945** - Lee receives the Distinguished Service Cross, HQ US Forces European Theater, General Orders No. 212, for actions "in the vicinity of Worgl and at Itter Castle, Austria, on 4 and 5 May 1945." [Hall of Valor / Military Times]

## Absurd / comedic beats (all real, all sourced)

1. **The castle's previous tenant was an anti-smoking league.** After the Anschluss, Schloss Itter briefly served as headquarters of the "German Alliance for Combating the Dangers of Tobacco" before Himmler took it for a VIP prison. [Harding, ch. 1]
2. **Tchaikovsky orchestrated a piece there.** Pianist Sophie Menter, a pupil of Liszt, bought the castle in 1884 and hosted Tchaikovsky, who orchestrated one of his compositions during an 1892 visit. [English Wikipedia, *Itter Castle*; Harding, ch. 1]
3. **Daladier had holidayed there.** The castle ran as a fashionable Alpine hotel in the 1920s and 30s, and Daladier stayed there in 1932. He was imprisoned in the same building eleven years later. [Harding, ch. 1]
4. **The Nazis left the armoury unlocked.** When Wimmer bolted at dawn on 4 May, the freed VIPs broke into the weapons room and helped themselves to pistols, rifles and submachine guns. [Harding, ch. 5; Harding 2008]
5. **The prisoners could not stand each other.** Reynaud and Daladier were bitter rivals; Weygand and Gamelin had succeeded and despised one another; collaborators shared a corridor with resisters. [Harding, ch. 3]
6. **A Waffen-SS captain helped defend the castle against the Waffen-SS.** Schrader is the only known case of a serving Waffen-SS officer fighting on the Allied side. [Harding; English Wikipedia]
7. **The tank was called *Besotten Jenny*.** Lee's M4A3(76)W. The second Sherman was *Boche Buster*, which spent the battle several miles away guarding a bridge and then turned up at the head of the relief column. [Harding, ch. 6 and ch. 7]
8. **A 57-year-old Wimbledon champion made the breakout.** Borotra went over the wall in a fake refugee outfit with a bedroll and a gnarled walking stick, waded a waist-deep stream, ran the SS lines, reached the Americans, then came back with a US platoon. [Harding, ch. 7]
9. **Lee's greeting to his rescuers: "What kept you?"** [Roberts, *Daily Beast*; Harding ch. 7]
10. **The castle was, for less than a day, the provisional headquarters of Allied occupation forces in Austria.** [Harding, ch. 8]
11. **The 12th Armored Division's own newspaper got the hero's name wrong**, printing "Lieutenant John C. Leo Jr." [*Hellcat News*, 26 May 1945, via Lone Sentry]
12. **Reynaud gave the whole thing four pages.** In his 680-page memoir *In the Thick of the Fight*, the Itter episode occupies about four pages. [The Unravel, citing Reynaud]

## Human-cost beats (play these completely straight)

- **Schloss Itter was a concentration-camp subcamp, not a gilded chateau.** It appears on the KZ-Gedenkstätte Dachau's own list of Dachau Außenlager: "SS-Sonderkommando Schloss Itter, 1943-1945, bis 27 [prisoners]." Up to 27 concentration-camp prisoners from Dachau and Flossenbürg built the cell blocks and then served the men imprisoned in them. **Čučković and Krobot, the two people who actually went for help, were both prisoners of that labour detail, not employees.** [KZ-Gedenkstätte Dachau / hdbg.de; Harding, ch. 1; German Wikipedia]
- **The conversion built nineteen cells across three floors**, each for at most two prisoners, with barred exteriors, double locks, floodlights and three machine-gun positions overlooking the courtyards. [Harding, ch. 1]
- **Eduard Weiter came to Itter straight from Dachau**, where he had ordered executions in the camp's final days. By Reynaud's account he was drunkenly boasting about them shortly before he shot himself on 2 May. [Harding, ch. 5; English Wikipedia]
- **Sebastian Wimmer's record.** Munich police 1923, SS 1935, Dachau, the Sudetenland 1938, Poland 1939 with Totenkopfstandarte "Oberbayern" during mass killings, then Das Reich, then Majdanek, then back to Dachau, then Itter. Harding calls him "a nasty piece of work." Released by the French in 1949, worked as a farm labourer, killed himself 10 December 1952. [Harding, ch. 1 and ch. 8]
- **In summer 1944 the labour prisoners planned an escape to Switzerland that would have taken the VIPs out too. The VIPs refused, and the plan died.** [German Wikipedia, citing Koop]
- **Gangl was killed by a sniper in the courtyard while trying to pull Paul Reynaud out of an exposed position, three days before the war in Europe ended.** Two of his men were seriously wounded. [Harding, ch. 7; DÖW]
- **The forced labourers' liberation was not the end of their displacement.** Krobot and the surviving women prisoners were moved to a UNRRA displaced-persons camp; Harding says they cannot be traced further. [Harding, ch. 8]
- **The postwar reckoning split the prisoners.** Daladier, Gamelin and Reynaud got a hero's welcome. Weygand, Borotra and de La Rocque were taken into French custody on collaboration charges. Weygand was exonerated, Borotra's charges were dropped, de La Rocque died before his case resolved. [Harding, ch. 8]
- **Lee's later life.** He lost a sheriff's election, worked as a bartender, saw multiple marriages fail amid heavy drinking, and died 15 January 1973 at Chenango Memorial Hospital in Norwich, New York, aged 54, of asphyxiation. [Harding, ch. 8]

## Myth vs record (corrections)

**1. "The only time Americans and Germans fought together in WW2."** Wrong. **Operation Cowboy**, 28 April 1945 at Hostau, saw Col. Charles "Hank" Reed's 2nd Cavalry Group and Wehrmacht troops fight Waffen-SS together to save the Lipizzaner stud. Itter was the **second and last** such action. [Army Historical Foundation; Military.com; English Wikipedia]

**2. "An 88 killed *Besotten Jenny*."** Harding's book attributes the killing round to an antitank gun, "likely 75mm Pak 40," striking the tank's left side. The 88 was firing on the keep. The "88 killed the Sherman" version is widely repeated including on Wikipedia. Present Harding's attribution and note the disagreement.

**3. The tank's name is *Besotten Jenny*, not "Besotten Betty."** [Harding, ch. 6]

**4. "General Paul Gamelin."** It is **Maurice Gamelin**.

**5. "All the French prisoners took up arms."** They did not. Harding names **five** who fought: **Reynaud, Michel Clemenceau, Gamelin, Borotra, de La Rocque**. **Daladier sheltered in the keep.** Weygand did not fire. [Harding, ch. 7]

**6. "Bochmann led the attack."** **No source found places SS-Oberführer Georg Bochmann physically at Itter.** English Wikipedia asserts it uncited; Harding never names him. **Treat the attacking force as elements of the 17th SS Panzergrenadier Division under an unidentified local commander.** German Wikipedia's added claim of Panzergrenadier-Division "Großdeutschland" involvement is uncorroborated and may be a conflation with the Grossdeutschland troops Čučković passed on 3 May.

**7. "Roughly 100 SS were captured at the castle."** Widely repeated from Wikipedia. **Harding's account does not support it** (only twelve, in Borotra's platoon action). Do not state an attacker casualty or capture total as fact.

**8. The date: 4 May or 5 May?** The rescue column moved on the evening of **4 May**; the battle was fought **5 May**. Lee's DSC citation covers "4 and 5 May 1945," and *Hellcat News* calls it a 4 to 5 May operation.

**9. "Weiter died on 4 May."** English Wikipedia's *Itter Castle* article contradicts its own other articles, Harding, and Reynaud. Use **2 May 1945**.

**10. Relief arrival time.** Harding's 2013 book says **just after 4 PM**. His 2008 article said just before 3 PM. Use "just after 4 PM."

**11. The relief was not the 12th Armored.** It was **Company E and elements of F and G, 2nd Battalion, 142nd Infantry Regiment, 36th Infantry Division**, with Shermans of the **753rd Tank Battalion**. Lee's own 12th Armored contribution was two tanks and a handful of men. [Harding, ch. 7]

**12. René Lévesque's eyewitness claim is contested.** Wikipedia and Harding both rely on Lévesque's *Mémoires* for the scene where the future Quebec premier, then a war correspondent, recognises Borotra emerging from the woods. Journalist William Johnson argues Lévesque was never at Itter. **Flag as disputed.** If the script uses Lévesque at all, hedge it. (Recommendation: cut it.)

**13. Prisoners reached Paris on 8 May, not 10 May.** Prefer Harding.

**14. The castle is not a museum.** Privately owned since 1985, currently by Dr. Ernst Bosin, an attorney from Kufstein, and **not open to the public**. Under Austrian monument protection. Was a luxury hotel from around 1950 to the late 1980s. [English and German Wikipedia; Atlas Obscura]

**15. Gangl's memorials.** Buried in Wörgl's cemetery on a street named **Sepp-Gangl-Straße**. His memorial inscription is catalogued by **DERLA**, University of Graz (record o:derla.tir141). [DERLA; Heimat Wörgl]
