# Research Report: Castle Itter — PEOPLE

## Summary

The Battle for Castle Itter (Schloss Itter, Tyrol, 5 May 1945) is unusually well documented for a "weird history" story, because Stephen Harding's **The Last Battle** (Da Capo Press, 2013) is a properly sourced monograph built on German, American, Austrian and French archival material and prisoner memoirs. Full text is mirrored at erenow.org, and I mined it chapter by chapter and cross-checked against German Wikipedia (which cites Harding's German edition plus a 2020 Ludwigsburg military-history society monograph on Gangl and a 2018 FAZ piece), the USHMM Encyclopedia of Camps and Ghettos entry for Schloss Itter, the Military Times Hall of Valor DSC citation, the International Tennis Hall of Fame, and Norwich (NY/VT) local records.

Six findings matter most for the script:

1. **Gangl did NOT hold the Knight's Cross.** He held the **German Cross in Gold** (Deutsches Kreuz in Gold, 8 March 1945), plus Iron Cross 2nd and 1st Class. Multiple popular retellings upgrade this. He also was not at Stalingrad.
2. **Lee was a 1st Lieutenant at the battle.** The captaincy came later; the DSC citation itself lists him as Captain with "(then First Lieutenant)."
3. **Exactly five French VIPs fired weapons**, named by Harding: Reynaud, Michel Clemenceau, Gamelin, de La Rocque, Borotra. **Weygand did not fire. Daladier did not fight.**
4. **Borotra was a Vichy official** (Commissioner General for General Education and Sport, July/August 1940 to April 1942) before the Germans arrested him. This is the part popular retellings skip.
5. **Georg Bochmann's personal direction of the Itter attack is unsourced.** Harding never names him. English Wikipedia asserts it with no citation.
6. **Schloss Itter was formally a Dachau subcamp** with a separate, unnamed population of concentration-camp "number prisoners" doing the labor, including women from Ravensbrück. Their fates are largely untraceable.

## Primary figures (deep)

### Major Josef "Sepp" Gangl, Wehrmacht, Werfer-Regiment 83

**Vital statistics**
- Born **12 September 1910, Obertraubling**, Landkreis Regensburg, Bavaria. Died **5 May 1945, Schloss Itter, Tyrol**, age 34. [Harding, *The Last Battle*, ch. 4; de.wikipedia "Josef Gangl (Offizier)"; WW2Gravestone]
- Father was an official of the Royal Bavarian State Railways; mother a former shop clerk. [Harding ch. 4; de.wikipedia]
- Married **Walburga Renz**, a shop assistant from Ludwigsburg, in 1935. Daughter **Sieglinde**, born 1936. [Harding ch. 4; de.wikipedia]
  - *Conflict:* Harding's text as rendered says "two children, including daughter Sieglinde"; German Wikipedia says one daughter, Sieglinde. Use "a wife and a young daughter, Sieglinde" as the safe formulation.

**Career (this is the detailed, verified version)**
- Enlisted in the Reichswehr **1 November 1928**, age 18. Artillerie-Regiment 7 (Nürnberg), then Artillerie-Regiment 5 (Ulm), then Artillerie-Regiment 25 (Ludwigsburg) from 1935. [Harding ch. 4; de.wikipedia]
- **Hauptfeldwebel** (senior NCO / company sergeant major) by November 1938. This confirms the "senior NCO who rose to officer" framing. [Harding ch. 4; de.wikipedia]
- Commissioned second lieutenant May 1940; forward-observation team leader in the Western campaign. [Harding ch. 4; de.wikipedia]
- **Eastern Front from June 1941**: commanded a 10.5 cm howitzer battery, Artillerie-Regiment (mot.) 25, Heeresgruppe Süd / Panzergruppe 1, Ukraine, through the **Battle of Kiev**. **No Stalingrad service is documented.** [Harding ch. 4; de.wikipedia, which states explicitly the unit operated in the southern sector]
- **Normandy: verified.** Assigned **4 March 1944** to the newly raised **Werfer-Regiment 83** at Celle, part of **Werfer-Brigade 7**. Moved to France May 1944; **7 June 1944** in the defense of Caen with the brigade attached to the 12th SS-Panzer-Division "Hitlerjugend"; escaped the **Falaise pocket** in August 1944 with heavy losses. [de.wikipedia, citing Harding German ed. and the Ludwigsburg monograph; Harding ch. 7 calls him a man "who'd survived the hell of Stalingrad and the maelstrom of Normandy," so Harding's own prose overstates the Stalingrad element his research chapter does not support]
- Ardennes offensive, then the failed defense of **Saarbrücken**, February 1945.

**Decorations (the Knight's Cross question, settled)**
- Iron Cross 2nd Class, **20 August 1941**
- Iron Cross 1st Class, **12 February 1942**
- **German Cross in Gold (Deutsches Kreuz in Gold), 8 March 1945**, for the Saarbrücken defense
- **NO Knight's Cross (Ritterkreuz).** [Harding ch. 4; de.wikipedia; TracesOfWar]
- Promoted **Major** shortly after the German Cross in Gold, given command of **II. Abteilung, Werfer-Regiment 83**. Harding's shorthand is "a career soldier three times decorated for bravery in combat." [Harding ch. 6]

**Wörgl and the defection**
- Early April 1945 his battalion, down to roughly **thirty men**, was ordered into the Tyrol for the Alpine Redoubt. Mid-April he arrived in the Inn valley and was absorbed into **Kampfgruppe Giehl** under Oberstleutnant **Johann Giehl**, within Generalleutnant **Georg Ritter von Hengl's** "Alpine Front, Northwest." [Harding ch. 4; de.wikipedia]
- Shortly after arriving he made contact through an intermediary with the **Wörgl resistance cell led by Alois Mayr**, a wealthy local businessman, with deputy **Rupert Hagleitner**, 31, a local politician who commanded the cell's twenty to thirty armed men. Gangl supplied them intelligence on Wehrmacht positions, named officers who could be trusted, and released weapons from a cache hidden at **Kelchsau**. On 4 May he handed Hagleitner's men automatic weapons and hand grenades. [Harding ch. 4]
- Harding is blunt about the stakes: this was "a blatant act of treason against both führer and fatherland, one punishable by death."
- Motive: Gangl's efficiency reports called him "a dedicated National Soldier" with "correct political views," so this was not long-standing ideological opposition. Both Harding and German Wikipedia frame his decision as **protecting Wörgl's civilians from SS reprisal** (Himmler's standing order was that any house flying a white flag would see its men shot) and preventing Giehl's order to defend the town to the last. **Alois Mayr held the same position despite having lost his 53-year-old sister and 17-year-old niece in an Allied bombing raid on 22 February 1945.** [Harding ch. 4; de.wikipedia]
- When ordered to withdraw with von Hengl's forces, Gangl **stayed with ten men from Werfer-Regiment 83**. That is the number that follows him. [Harding ch. 4; de.wikipedia]
- **4 May 1945, ~11:00**, the Czech cook Andreas Krobot arrived by bicycle from Schloss Itter. Gangl drove roughly seven to eight kilometers through Waffen-SS held ground to the American lines at **Kufstein** in a kübelwagen under a white flag, accompanied only by his driver, **Corporal Keblitsch**. There he met Lee, presented Christiane Mabire's letter, and proposed, in Harding's phrasing, that "they were all going on a rescue mission." [Harding ch. 5; de.wikipedia]

**Death**
- Killed by a **sniper's bullet** in the castle's front courtyard while running to pull **Paul Reynaud** out of the line of fire. Harding, verbatim: *"In his haste to stop the Frenchman the German officer ran almost fully erect, and he'd gone barely ten feet when he suddenly dropped to the courtyard's cobblestones in an awkward heap, his visored hat rolling a few feet past him... when blood quickly began pooling around his head, it was obvious that Sepp Gangl... had been cut down by a sniper's bullet."* [Harding ch. 7]
- German Wikipedia, verbatim: *"Gangl wurde beim Versuch, den ehemaligen französischen Premierminister Paul Reynaud aus der Schusslinie zu bringen, von der Kugel eines Scharfschützen tödlich getroffen."*
- **He was the only defender killed.** Four defenders were wounded. [de.wikipedia; en.wikipedia "Battle of Castle Itter"]

**Posthumous**
- Body taken to St. Joseph's Church in Itter village, then buried **10 May 1945** in Wörgl's **Waldfriedhof** (municipal/forest cemetery). Grave marked with an iron cross plaque in the war-graves section, catalogued by **DERLA**, the Austrian academic Digital Memorial Landscape project at the University of Graz. [Harding ch. 8; de.wikipedia; DERLA tir141]
- **Sepp-Gangl-Straße in Wörgl**, running between Wildschönauerstraße and Brixentalerstraße, separating the old cemetery from the new southern municipal cemetery. [Heimat Wörgl; de.wikipedia; Harding ch. 8]
- Harding: *"Gangl is considered an Austrian national hero because of his alliance with the anti-Nazi resistance movement, his efforts to protect the civilian population of Wörgl, and his participation in the defense of the French VIPs at Schloss Itter. A major street in Wörgl is named after him."*
- **16 October 2021**: Ludwigsburg's mayor Matthias Knecht unveiled a memorial plaque to Gangl at the Karlskaserne. [de.wikipedia]
- **DISPUTED / NOT VERIFIED:** German Wikipedia lists a posthumous **US Distinguished Service Cross** for Gangl. Harding's aftermath chapter mentions **no American decoration** for him, and I could not find him in the Military Times Hall of Valor DSC database. **Do not put this in the script.**

### 1st Lieutenant John Cary "Jack" Lee Jr., Company B, 23rd Tank Battalion, 12th Armored Division

**Vital statistics**
- Born **12 March 1918 in Nebraska**; family moved to **Norwich, Chenango County, New York**, which is his hometown. **Age 27 at the battle.** [Evening Sun (Norwich NY), Norwich HS Hall of Distinction profile; Harding via HistoryNet]
- Died **15 January 1973** at **Chenango Memorial Hospital, Norwich**, age **54**. Buried **St. Paul's Cemetery, Norwich**. [Evening Sun; Harding ch. 8; Find a Grave 147686659]

**Prewar and training**
- **Norwich High School class of 1937**, lettered in football. One year at **Syracuse University**, then transferred to **Norwich University** in Northfield, Vermont, the military college. **Graduated 11 May 1942**, commissioned second lieutenant of **cavalry**. Class of 1942. [Evening Sun; Norwich University bicentennial series #54]
- Trained as an armor officer at **Fort Knox**, where an instructor noted he showed "a natural talent" for armored warfare. [Harding ch. 6]

**Rank at the battle: FIRST LIEUTENANT.** The DSC citation reads "Captain (Cavalry) [then First Lieutenant] John Carey Lee, Jr." The captaincy came after. German Wikipedia calls him "Hauptmann" at Kufstein, which is anachronistic. [Military Times Hall of Valor, HQ USFET GO No. 212, 29 Aug 1945]

**Documented personality (all of these are sourced, not invented)**
- Harding: **"five-foot-ten, 190-pound"** former high school and college football player; **"a rough-talking, hard-drinking, and hard-charging bull of a man"** with a **"devil-may-care attitude,"** a **"quick grin,"** and a "swashbuckling" reputation. His platoon in Company B was nicknamed **"Hell for Leather."** [Harding ch. 6]
- Harding: **"a man who thoroughly enjoyed a drink and a laugh."** He and his close friend 1st Lt. **Harry Basse** (the motor officer, tank "Boche Buster," later Silver Star) worked the bars and clubs of Abilene during stateside training. [Harding ch. 6]
- **The cigar is documented.** Andrew Roberts, reviewing Harding in The Daily Beast, describes Lee as a **"cigar-chewing, hard-drinking"** officer and the "quintessential warrior: smart, aggressive, innovative" with "immense cool." Task & Purpose, drawing on Harding, has Lee meeting Gangl **"chewing on an unlit cigar and ready to hear the German officer out."** [Roberts, Daily Beast, 12 May 2013; Task & Purpose]
- Battalion commander **Clow** rated Lee "one of the best tank officers in the 12th AD." [Harding ch. 6]
- **Reynaud, who did not like him, is the best source for the profanity/roughness angle**: Reynaud called Lee **"crude in both looks and manners"** while also conceding he had the **"fine figure of a football player."** [Reynaud, quoted in Harding, via HistoryNet]

**Awards**
- **Bronze Star Medal** for the Battle of **Herrlisheim**, cited for his "cool and aggressive handling of his platoon." [Evening Sun / Norwich HS Hall of Distinction]
- **Distinguished Service Cross**, HQ US Forces European Theater, **General Orders No. 212, 29 August 1945**, for "extraordinary heroism in connection with military operations against an armed enemy... in the vicinity of Worgl and at Itter Castle, Austria, on 4 and 5 May 1945." The citation describes the damaged and demolition-wired bridge, SS roadblocks, the assault by roughly 150 Waffen-SS grenadiers with an 88mm anti-tank gun, his coordination of the defense through the night, and the withdrawal into the keep until relief arrived. It concludes that his actions "exemplify the highest traditions of the military forces of the United States." Hometown of record: **Chenango, New York.** [Military Times Hall of Valor]

**Postwar (the human-cost beat)**
- Honorably discharged **late January 1946**. Played semi-pro football (Evening Sun: the **New Jersey Giants**; the Norwich University series and other retellings say "New York Giants," treat the pro-team claim as **soft**), coached and played for Norwich's "Y Giants," and coached the **Sidney Cardinals**, a now-defunct upstate New York team. [Evening Sun; Norwich University bicentennial #54]
- Ran **Hand's Inn**; tried bartending, hotel ownership, and a run for **sheriff**, which he lost. Multiple marriages, all failed. Harding attributes the collapse to alcoholism. [Harding ch. 8; Evening Sun]
- Harding, verbatim: he **"died at Chenango Memorial Hospital in Norwich on January 15, 1973, at the relatively young age of fifty-four. The cause of death was listed as 'asphyxiation,' likely as the result of acute alcohol poisoning."**
- **Inducted into the Norwich High School Hall of Distinction in 2024**, as one of the first six inductees. Norwich University runs him as entry #54 in its bicentennial "Forging the Future" series. [Evening Sun, published Feb 2025; bicentennial.norwich.edu]

**His crews (for texture)**
- "Besotten Jenny" (Lee's tank): Sgt. **William T. Rushford**, Cpl. **Edward J. Szymcyk**, Cpl. **Edward J. Seiner**, Pfc. **Herbert G. McHaley**.
- "Boche Buster" (Basse's tank): Tech. Sgt. **William E. Elliot**, Sgt. **Glenn E. Shermann**. [Harding via HistoryNet]

### Jean Borotra, "the Bounding Basque"

**Vital statistics**
- Born **13 August 1898, Biarritz**, France. Died **17 July 1994**, age 95. [International Tennis Hall of Fame; Harding ch. 3]
  - *Conflict:* Harding's aftermath chapter says "died in June 1994 at age ninety-five." The ITHF gives **17 July 1994**. Use the ITHF date.

**Tennis record (ITHF, authoritative)**
- **4 Grand Slam singles titles**: Wimbledon **1924, 1926**; Australian **1928**; French **1931**.
- **9 Grand Slam men's doubles titles**; **5 mixed doubles titles**, including Wimbledon 1925 with **Suzanne Lenglen** and the 1926 US National with Elizabeth Ryan.
- One of the **Four Musketeers** with **René Lacoste, Henri Cochet and Jacques "Toto" Brugnon**. France won the **Davis Cup six years running, 1927 to 1932**. Borotra's Davis Cup record: **36-18** (19-12 singles, 17-6 doubles), a member 1922-1937 and again in 1947.
- **Inducted into the International Tennis Hall of Fame in 1976**, together with all four Musketeers.
- Famous 1932 Davis Cup performance at age 34: beat Ellsworth Vines, then came back from two sets down against Wilmer Allison to clinch it for France.

**The Vichy chapter (do not skip this)**
- Appointed **Commissioner General for General Education and Sport** in the Vichy government, **20 July / August 1940**, serving until **April 1942**. He took control of the sporting federations via the **Sports Charter of 20 December 1940** and imposed **nine hours a week of compulsory PE** in primary and secondary schools. [Harding ch. 3; Mémorial de la Shoah "Sport under the Vichy regime"; University of Wolverhampton Football and War Network]
- Under his commissariat, **women's football was banned in France**. That is a real, documented consequence of his tenure and a useful complicating detail. [University of Wolverhampton, Football and War Network]
- He was not a compliant collaborator: in **August 1941** he **barred French national teams from competing against German teams**, and he was **fired on 19 April 1942** after refusing German pressure, when Laval returned to power. [Harding ch. 3]
- **Arrested 22 November 1942** attempting to cross into Spain to reach the Free French. Deported to Germany. Arrived at **Schloss Itter 12 May 1943**. [Harding ch. 3]
- At Itter he kept himself in extreme condition, running roughly **90 circuits of the castle grounds, about nine miles, daily**, and made **three escape attempts**, the last recaptured **29 April 1945**, punished only with house arrest. That conditioning is exactly why he was the one who could run the gauntlet on 5 May. [Harding ch. 3]
- Politically he sat with the **right-wing table** at meals, alongside the Weygands and de La Rocque. He had been a **Croix de Feu** member and joined de La Rocque's **PSF**. [Harding ch. 3]

**What he actually did at Itter, 5 May 1945**
- He **first fought**, firing from the front courtyard alongside Reynaud, Clemenceau, Gamelin and de La Rocque. [Harding ch. 7]
- Then he volunteered to break out and fetch help. He disguised himself as an Austrian refugee with a **"ragged bedroll and gnarled walking stick,"** **scaled the low parapet wall on the north side**, crossed about **forty yards of open ground**, went through woods past SS positions, and **waded a swift stream** while two Waffen-SS soldiers watched and held their fire, apparently taken in by the disguise. [Harding ch. 7]
- Reaching American lines near **Lt. Reinhard's platoon** of the 142nd Infantry, he delivered the message and proposed a tactical plan. **Lynch gave him "an American uniform and a weapon,"** and Borotra led **Company G and Reinhard's platoon** back toward the castle, personally clearing two further Waffen-SS positions as the relief force took the village. [Harding ch. 7]
- **Confirmed: he did borrow a US uniform and come back with the relief force.** This is Harding, not folklore. [Harding ch. 7; corroborated by LandmarkScout, which states he "asked for an U.S. uniform and joined the relief force which reached Itter castle around 16:00"]

**Postwar**
- **Never tried** for collaboration despite the charges being raised. Resumed his business career until **1975**; kept playing competitive and veterans' tennis internationally into his nineties. [Harding ch. 8; ITHF]

## Secondary figures

### SS-Hauptsturmführer Kurt-Siegfried Schrader
- Born **Magdeburg, August 1916** (one source gives **24 August 1916**). Hitler Youth from age 14 (1930), enlisted 1937, joined the SS the same day, attended an elite military-political school in Berlin, commissioned second lieutenant 1939. **Promoted SS-Hauptsturmführer 21 June 1943.** [Harding ch. 5; en.wikipedia "Battle of Castle Itter"; Real Life Heroes Wiki summarizing Schrader's memoir]
- Service: Anschluss and Sudetenland, then the **siege of Leningrad** (wounded 1941), then **22nd SS-Panzergrenadier Regiment** in France, where he was **severely wounded by aircraft strafing in July 1944**. [Harding ch. 5]
- **Why he was at Itter:** sent to the Wörgl military hospital in **January 1945** to convalesce. His old acquaintance **Wimmer** arranged housing for Schrader's family in Itter village. He made regular social calls at the castle and talked "politics and philosophy" with the French VIPs. [Harding ch. 5]
- **Why he switched:** Harding traces it to the **20 July 1944 bomb plot**, after which the bedridden Schrader "mentally broke" with Hitler, the Party and the Reich. His "only goal now was to protect his family and make it through the rest of the war unscathed." On 28 April 1945 he joined Kampfgruppe Giehl as supply officer, quietly worked on Wehrmacht colleagues, helped persuade **Giehl** to stop resisting, obtained a discharge document, went home to Itter village and put his uniform away. [Harding ch. 5]
- **In the battle:** he co-ran the defense of the upper keep with Lee, posting German riflemen at windows on several floors and directing fire down the slope at the advancing Waffen-SS. **His wife and children were inside the castle during the fight; his wife took minor injuries from flying stone in the initial bombardment.** [Harding ch. 7]
- **Postwar:** the French VIPs gave him a signed protective letter reading: **"On May 4 [sic], 1945, Captain S. Schrader ensured the safety of the French detainees at Itter castle."** He served only a short POW/internment term, released **1947** (other summaries say a two-year sentence, consistent). Worked as a **bricklayer**, then took a **North Rhine-Westphalia Interior Ministry** post in **1953**, retiring in **1980**. [Harding ch. 8]
- *Caution:* the South African Military History Journal article claims Schrader was "Hitler's bodyguard." **NOT SUPPORTED** by Harding or any archival source I found. Do not use.
- *Note on rank:* Harding's battle chapter sometimes refers to him loosely as "Lieutenant Schrader" / "the German officer," but his substantive biography chapter and every corroborating source give **SS-Hauptsturmführer** (captain). Use SS-Hauptsturmführer.

### Zvonimir "Zvonko" Čučković
- **Croatian.** Frequently described as a **Yugoslav communist resistance member**. Electrical technician by trade. Aged about **36** in 1943. [Harding ch. 1 and 5; en.wikipedia]
- **Arrested by the Gestapo December 1941; transferred to Dachau 26 September 1942.** Selected for the February 1943 Itter construction detail because of his technical skill, and **kept on at the castle after the other laborers went back** to finish the electrical system. He was therefore a concentration-camp prisoner, not a servant. [Harding ch. 1]
- **3 May 1945:** left the castle on a bicycle on the pretext of installing electric lights at Wimmer's farmhouse at Mühltal, carrying a letter in English addressed to the first American he met. **Wörgl, eight kilometers downhill, was held by German troops, so he went the other way**, up the Inn valley toward **Innsbruck**, roughly 40 miles / 64 km, through checkpoints, past German troops and Tiger tanks. Late that evening he reached the outskirts of Innsbruck and made contact with an advance party of the **409th Infantry Regiment, 103rd Infantry Division** (Maj. Gen. Anthony C. McAuliffe), US VI Corps. A Yugoslav-American interpreter told him to come back the next morning. [Harding ch. 5; en.wikipedia]
- **Postwar:** returned to Yugoslavia, ran an electrical contracting business in **Belgrade**, wrote up his notes as **"Zwei Jahren auf Schloss Itter"** and corresponded with the French ex-prisoners. **Died in Belgrade, 1984.** [Harding ch. 8]

### Andreas Krobot
- **Czech.** The castle **cook**, also a prisoner. [Harding ch. 5; de.wikipedia; en.wikipedia]
- **4 May 1945:** carried the **second** appeal, an English-language letter **written by Christiane Mabire**, by bicycle down to **Wörgl**, arriving about **11:00**, where he found Gangl. That letter is what Gangl carried to Lee at Kufstein. [Harding ch. 5; de.wikipedia]
- **Fate: NOT FOUND.** Harding is explicit: *"it has proven impossible to track Andres Krobot or any of the female number prisoners following their handover to the UNRRA."* [Harding ch. 8]
- *Spelling caution:* Harding's text uses both "Andreas" and "Andres." German Wikipedia uses **Andreas Krobot**. Use Andreas.

### SS-Hauptsturmführer Sebastian Wimmer, commandant
- Born **5 January 1902, Dingolfing**, Bavaria. Joined the **Munich police in 1923**, made sergeant, Harding says, through brutal interrogation. Joined the **SS 1 March 1935**; SS-Totenkopfstandarte "Oberbayern" at **Dachau** from 1936; promoted **Hauptsturmführer 20 April 1940**. [de.wikipedia "Sebastian Wimmer (SS-Mitglied)"; Harding ch. 1]
- **Poland, 1939**: participated in killings at villages including **Ciepielów** and **Rawa Mazowiecka**. Then 3rd SS Panzer Division (France and Eastern Front) and 2nd SS Panzer Division. [Harding ch. 1]
- **Majdanek**, late September 1942 to mid-February 1943: **first Schutzhaftlagerführer** (protective-custody camp leader), i.e. the officer running the prisoner compound. [de.wikipedia] Harding places him afterward at **Dachau** in a Schutzhaftlager role (de.wikipedia says deputy Schutzhaftlagerführer at Dachau to early October 1944).
- **Arrived Schloss Itter as commandant 28 April 1943** with his wife Thérèse. Harding: **"a brutal thug by both nature and training."**
- **Fled early on 4 May 1945**, but not before **asking several of the VIPs to sign a statement saying he had treated them "correctly."** He left with his wife. [Harding ch. 5]
- **Postwar:** held at Kufstein fortress. Automatically classifiable as a war criminal on the basis of his SS-TV service and Dachau/Majdanek postings, but **former Itter prisoners intervened on his behalf** and the French **released him in 1949**. He worked as a farm laborer near Wörgl, returned to Dingolfing in 1951 an alcoholic and recently divorced, and **killed himself on 10 December 1952, age 50**. [Harding ch. 8; de.wikipedia]

### SS-Oberführer Georg Bochmann, 17th SS Panzergrenadier Division "Götz von Berlichingen"
- Born **18 September 1913, Albernau, Saxony**; died **8 June 1973, Offenbach am Main**. Joined the SS 1934. Promoted **SS-Oberführer 20 April 1945**. Commanded the **17th SS Panzergrenadier Division from 27 March 1945 to 8 May 1945**, succeeding SS-Standartenführer Jakob Fick. [en.wikipedia "Georg Bochmann"]
- **THE ITTER ATTRIBUTION IS SHAKY.** English Wikipedia states "He would then lead SS troops from the Berlichingen at the Battle of Castle Itter" **with no citation at all**, and Wikipedia's Castle Itter article lists him as the opposing commander. **Stephen Harding never names Bochmann anywhere in the book.** Harding identifies the attacking force only by its unit markings: "at least two of the vehicles bore the mailed-fist insignia of the 17th SS Panzer-Grenadier Division." No individual SS commander of the Itter assault is identified in any reliable source I found.
- **Recommended handling in the script:** attribute the attack to *elements of* the 17th SS Panzergrenadier Division, roughly 100 to 150 men, and do not put Bochmann personally at Itter. If he must be mentioned, say only that he commanded the division at the time.
- Bochmann's own end: he refused suicidal orders from Generalfeldmarschall Schörner, was dismissed and faced court-martial, and surrendered to US forces 9 May 1945. The division surrendered to the 101st Airborne at Rottach-Egern on 6 May 1945.

| **Christiane Mabire** | Reynaud's secretary, later his wife | b. **17 February 1913**. Arrested 22 Nov 1942, held at **Ravensbrück**, arrived Itter **2 July 1943**. **She wrote the English-language letter Krobot carried to Wörgl.** ~35-year age gap with Reynaud. |
| **Marcel Granger** | Relative by marriage of General Henri Giraud | b. **1901, Toulon**. Estate owner in French Tunisia, resistance intelligence agent who set up arms dumps. Captured April 1943, **held at Dachau**, arrived Itter **2 July 1943**, taken as a Giraud-family hostage on Hitler's order. |
| **Mme Marie-Renée-Joséphine Weygand** | Weygand's wife | Arrived with him 5 Dec 1943. |

**NOT present in May 1945: André François-Poncet.** The former French ambassador to Berlin was arrested **27 August 1943** and brought to Schloss Itter on **3 September 1943**, but was **moved out on 23 November 1943** to the **Hotel Ifen** at Hirschegg in the Kleinwalsertal, where he was freed on 2 May 1945. Several retellings (including HistoryNet's roster) list him among the Itter prisoners without this timeline. **Do not put him in the battle.** [NZZ; Tagesspiegel; François-Poncet, *Tagebuch eines Gefangenen*]

### WHO ACTUALLY FIRED (this is the correction that matters)

Harding names **exactly five** French VIPs who armed themselves and fought:

1. **Paul Reynaud** — got a **Tommy gun out of his own trunk**, took position behind the parapet wall in the front courtyard, fired toward the inn on the Schlossweg and into the trees of the ravine.
2. **Michel Clemenceau** — had already **calmly posted himself at a loophole** in the front courtyard, to stop the SS taking the burning Sherman.
3. **Général Maurice Gamelin** — joined them at the parapet wall, firing, in Harding's phrase, **"enthusiastically, if somewhat randomly."**
4. **Colonel François de La Rocque** — also at the parapet wall; his **MP-40 eventually ran dry**.
5. **Jean Borotra** — fired from the courtyard, then made the breakout run.

**Weygand did NOT fire.** Lee got Weygand and Gamelin to agree to help shepherd defenders and VIPs into the keep; Gamelin then went and fought, Weygand did not. [Harding ch. 7]

**Daladier did NOT fight.** He positioned himself inside the keep during the heavy fighting. Two Polish-born Wehrmacht soldiers sheltered him; one of them told him **"Hitler was kaput."** [Harding ch. 7]

**The women, Jouhaux, Granger, the Cailliaus:** no evidence any of them took up arms. Do not imply it.

## The Dachau subcamp forced laborers (play straight)

This is the part every viral retelling drops, and it is the reason the castle existed.

- The German government leased Schloss Itter from its owner, the Tyrolean lawyer and deputy Landeshauptmann **Dr. Franz Grüner**, in early 1940. On **23 November 1942** Himmler got Hitler's signature on an order directing **SS-Obergruppenführer Oswald Pohl** (head of the SS-WVHA) to acquire it for "special SS use." **Formal requisition 7 February 1943**, placing the castle under **Dachau's operational control**. [Harding ch. 1]
- **8 February 1943**: SS-Untersturmführer **Petz** arrived with a work detail of **27 concentration-camp prisoners: 12 from Dachau and 15 from Flossenbürg**, chosen for construction trades (carpentry, plumbing, electrical), supervised by about **ten SS-TV guards**. They converted the castle into a prison. Čučković was one of them. [Harding ch. 1; USHMM *Encyclopedia of Camps and Ghettos*, Vol. I, entry "Schloss Itter," which gives 26 prisoners from Flossenbürg and Dachau]
- The SS designated it **"SS-Sonderkommando Schloss Itter."** It was **one of 16 Dachau subcamps on Austrian soil**. Harding: it "was planned to be arguably the most important and high-profile subcamp in all of Nazi Germany's sprawling concentration-camp system." [USHMM Encyclopedia of Camps and Ghettos; Harding ch. 2]
- **The forced-labor population held there through the war:** English Wikipedia, summarizing the same literature, states the castle held **"a number of Eastern European prisoners detached from Dachau, who were used for maintenance and other menial work."** The USHMM entry specifies that **from August 1943 there were seven or eight female prisoners, German, Austrian or Czech, transferred from Ravensbrück, plus two male prisoners from Dachau**, alongside the "prominent" prisoners. **A female SS overseer was transferred in from Ravensbrück to run them.** [USHMM Encyclopedia of Camps and Ghettos, "Schloss Itter"; en.wikipedia]
- Harding's term for them throughout the book is **"number prisoners"** — the castle's own vocabulary, distinguishing the people with tattooed or sewn-on camp numbers from the VIPs who ate at tablecloths with two liters of wine a week. **Two categories of prisoner, one building, radically different treatment. That contrast is the honest version of this story.**
- **What happened to them:** after liberation, **Major John Kramers** took the female number prisoners to a **UNRRA displaced-persons camp**. Harding: *"it has proven impossible to track Andres Krobot or any of the female number prisoners following their handover to the UNRRA."* Only **Čučković's** postwar life is documented. [Harding ch. 8]
- **NOT FOUND:** names of any of the Ravensbrück women; the total number of number prisoners present on 5 May 1945; a Dachau memorial site (kz-gedenkstaette-dachau.de) page for Schloss Itter specifically. The Dachau memorial's subcamp-network page does not list it. The USHMM *Encyclopedia of Camps and Ghettos* Vol. I entry is the authoritative source and is paywalled at Project MUSE (document 1647); the figures above come from quotations of it in search results and should be re-checked against the printed volume if you can get it.

**Script guidance:** the VIPs' wine ration, hairdresser, library and political feuding, and the number prisoners' forced labor, were happening in the same building at the same time. Say that plainly and without a joke attached.

## Documented feuds and ironies (all real)

**Reynaud vs. Daladier.** Real, documented, and older than the castle. Daladier was PM before Reynaud, Reynaud pushed him out, and Daladier had Reynaud's mistress the Comtesse de Portes as a rival influence. At Itter they **ate at separate tables and did not speak.** [Harding ch. 3]

**The three-table seating chart.** Harding documents the dining room split by faction:
- Table 1 (right): the Weygands, Borotra, de La Rocque
- Table 2 (anti-Daladier, anti-Weygand): Reynaud, Mabire, Gamelin, Clemenceau
- Table 3 (neutral): Daladier, Jouhaux, Bruchlen, the Cailliaus

Marie-Agnès Cailliau arrived hoping the French prisoners would be united by shared captivity and, in Harding's phrasing, "soon found out just how wrong she was." [Harding ch. 3]

**Reynaud vs. Weygand.** Reynaud blamed Weygand above all others for France's 1940 collapse. On seeing him, Reynaud reportedly said **"Traitor, collaborator!"** Weygand at one point chased after Reynaud shouting **"Hooligan!"** [Harding ch. 3]

**Weygand vs. Gamelin.** Real. Weygand replaced Gamelin as supreme commander in May 1940; Gamelin never forgave the professional humiliation and sided with Reynaud against both Weygand and Daladier. [Harding ch. 3]

**De La Rocque vs. Jouhaux.** Real and structural: de La Rocque's anticommunist Croix de Feu had spent the 1930s fighting Jouhaux's CGT in the streets. Harding: de La Rocque **"could not abide the leftist Jouhaux."** Then on 5 May 1945 de La Rocque fired an MP-40 from a wall a few yards from where Jouhaux was sheltering. [Harding ch. 3 and 7]

**The Gamelin irony.** In September 1939 Gangl's regiment was in the Saarpfalz when French divisions crossed the German border, and withdrew within two weeks **on the orders of the French commander-in-chief, Maurice Gamelin**. In May 1945 Gangl died defending Gamelin. [de.wikipedia, service record; Harding]

**The Vichy irony.** Two of the five prisoners who fired guns at the SS, Borotra and de La Rocque, were men of the Vichy/authoritarian right. De La Rocque was simultaneously running an intelligence network for British SIS. Neither is a simple hero or a simple collaborator.

**The Cailliau irony.** Charles de Gaulle's own sister was in the castle, ill, having come through Fresnes and a Buchenwald annex, while the men who had governed France before him argued over seating.

**The commandant's exit.** Before he ran on 4 May, Wimmer, veteran of Majdanek and Dachau and the 1939 Polish killings, asked his prisoners to **sign a paper saying he had treated them "correctly."** Some of them later intervened for him and the French released him in 1949. He drank himself into a divorce and killed himself in 1952.

**Two castles' worth of Germans.** Gangl and Schrader, a Wehrmacht major with the German Cross in Gold and a Waffen-SS captain, fought alongside American tankers against a Waffen-SS division. Harding and German Wikipedia both note this is **one of only two known WWII engagements where US and German troops fought on the same side**, the other being Operation Cowboy.

## Myth vs record (corrections)

| Popular claim | Record |
|---|---|
| Gangl held the **Knight's Cross** | **No.** German Cross in Gold, 8 March 1945, plus Iron Cross 1st and 2nd Class. [Harding ch. 4; de.wikipedia; TracesOfWar] |
| Gangl was a **Stalingrad** veteran | **No documented Stalingrad service.** He fought in Ukraine with Heeresgruppe Süd / Panzergruppe 1, through the Battle of Kiev. German Wikipedia states explicitly his unit operated in the southern sector. Harding's own narrative prose calls him a Stalingrad survivor, but his research chapter does not support it. **Normandy is solid** (Werfer-Brigade 7, Caen, Falaise pocket). |
| Gangl received a **posthumous US Distinguished Service Cross** | **Unverified.** Claimed by German Wikipedia only. Harding's aftermath chapter mentions no American award. Not in the Hall of Valor database. |
| Lee was a **Captain** at the battle | **First Lieutenant.** His own DSC citation reads "Captain (Cavalry) [then First Lieutenant]." He made captain later. |
| Lee played for the **New York Giants** | **Soft.** The Norwich Evening Sun's Hall of Distinction profile says the **New Jersey Giants** (semi-pro) and Norwich's local "Y Giants." The NFL claim appears in secondary retellings. Say "semi-pro football" and be safe. |
| **All** the French prisoners fought | **Five did:** Reynaud, Michel Clemenceau, Gamelin, de La Rocque, Borotra. **Weygand did not fire** (he helped move people to the keep). **Daladier stayed inside the keep.** The women, Jouhaux, Granger and the Cailliaus did not fight. |
| **Borotra deflected a grenade with his tennis racket** | **Myth.** Appears in the South African Military History Journal piece. Nothing in Harding. Do not use. |
| **Schrader was Hitler's bodyguard** | **Unsupported.** Same SAMHJ article. Harding's biography of Schrader has him in Leningrad and Normandy with the 22nd SS-Panzergrenadier Regiment, no Leibstandarte/bodyguard role. |
| **Georg Bochmann personally commanded the attack on Itter** | **Unsourced.** Wikipedia asserts it without a citation; Harding never names him. Attribute the attack to "elements of the 17th SS Panzergrenadier Division," roughly 100 to 150 men. |
| **André François-Poncet was among the liberated prisoners** | **No.** He was at Itter only from 3 September to 23 November 1943, then moved to the Hotel Ifen in the Kleinwalsertal, freed there 2 May 1945. |
| Čučković rode **down to Wörgl** for help | **He rode the opposite way.** Wörgl was German-held, so he went **up the Inn valley 40 miles toward Innsbruck** and found the 409th Infantry, 103rd Division. Krobot is the one who went to Wörgl, the next day. |
| Čučković was a hired **handyman/servant** | He was a **Dachau concentration-camp prisoner** on a construction detail, retained for his electrical skills. |
| Borotra was simply a heroic tennis star imprisoned by Nazis | He was **Vichy's Commissioner General for General Education and Sport, 1940 to 1942**, who banned women's football in France, before being sacked, arrested and deported for refusing German demands. Both halves are true. |
| Marie-Agnès Cailliau born **1899** | Harding's text as rendered says 1899; she was born **27 May 1889** and was Charles de Gaulle's **older** sister (he was born 1890). Use 1889. |
