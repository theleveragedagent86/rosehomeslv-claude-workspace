# EPISODE SCRIPT: THE DAY AMERICANS AND GERMANS FOUGHT SIDE BY SIDE

**Target runtime:** ~20 minutes (~3,000 narration words at ~150 wpm)
**Style:** OverSimplified format. Comedic animated history. Flat colors, simple characters, exaggerated reactions, fake country and figure dialogue.
**Story type:** Single obscure but REAL WW2 action. The Battle for Castle Itter, 5 May 1945, Schloss Itter, Austrian Tyrol.
**Title (standard tier):** `The Day Americans and Germans Fought Side by Side | Codename History`

> ACCURACY NOTE FOR PRODUCTION: Every beat here is documented. A few popular exaggerations are corrected in the fact-check log (the "strangest battle" label is popular framing, not official; not every VIP fought; Lee was a Lieutenant at the battle, promoted to Captain after). Keep the jokes on the absurdity. Keep the facts straight. The death of Major Sepp Gangl is played completely straight.
>
> VOICE NOTE: The comedy lives in the NARRATION, not just the visuals. Deadpan delivery. The narrator explains this like the least believable thing that ever actually happened. Countries and figures get short fake dialogue. Second-person "you" pulls the viewer in. When we reach the death of Sepp Gangl, the jokes stop. That beat is straight.

## ACT 1: THE FANCIEST PRISON IN THE ALPS (0:45 - 3:15)

**[VISUAL: The castle again, now with a "PRISON" sign hung crookedly on the gate.]**

**NARRATOR:** Okay. Let's back up. This place is Schloss Itter, a castle in the Tyrol region of Austria. And by 1945 it was not a home. It was a prison. A very specific prison.

**[VISUAL: A Nazi official checking a clipboard labeled "VIPs ONLY" at the gate. A regular soldier gets turned away.]**

**NARRATOR:** Nazi Germany used Itter to hold high-value French prisoners. Not soldiers. Not random people. Famous people. The kind of people you keep alive because they might be useful later.

**[VISUAL: A lineup of cartoon prisoners, each with a name tag.]**

**NARRATOR:** So let's meet the guests, because the guest list is insane. First, two former Prime Ministers of France: Édouard Daladier and Paul Reynaud.

**[VISUAL: Daladier and Reynaud standing stiffly, backs to each other, not making eye contact.]**

**NARRATOR:** And here is the first fun problem. These two men could not stand each other. They had spent the years before the war blaming each other for basically everything. And now they were roommates.

**[VISUAL: Two French generals added to the lineup, also glaring at each other.]**

**NARRATOR:** Next, two former commanders-in-chief of the French army: General Maxime Weygand and General Maurice Gamelin. Who also, wonderfully, could not stand each other.

**[VISUAL: A man in tennis whites holding a racket, looking way too relaxed for a prison.]**

**NARRATOR:** Then we have Jean Borotra. A world-famous tennis champion. One of the "Four Musketeers" who dominated Wimbledon in the 1920s. Remember him. He becomes important.

**[VISUAL: More prisoners: a labor leader, an older gentleman, a stern woman.]**

**NARRATOR:** Rounding out the roster: labor leader Léon Jouhaux. Michel Clemenceau, son of the Prime Minister who led France in the First World War. The right-wing leader François de La Rocque. And Marie-Agnès Cailliau, who happened to be the older sister of a French general named Charles de Gaulle.

**[VISUAL: A group shot of all the prisoners, arms crossed, glaring in every direction at once.]**

**NARRATOR:** So picture this castle. Left-wing politicians. Right-wing politicians. Rival generals. Old enemies. Trapped together for years, hating each other almost as much as they hated the Nazis. It is the world's tensest dinner party. And in a few days, all of them are going to have to pick up guns and fight for their lives together.

**[VISUAL: Down in the castle basement, ordinary prisoners in rough clothing doing hard labor.]**

**NARRATOR:** And it is worth remembering these famous names were not the only people here. The castle also held Eastern European prisoners moved over from the Dachau concentration camp, used for the labor that kept the place running. The VIPs got the fame. Those prisoners got the work.

**[VISUAL: The SS guards at the castle suddenly looking at their watches nervously.]**

**NARRATOR:** Because by early May, the war is collapsing. The German guards can hear the front getting closer every day. And they have a decision to make. Do you stay and fight for a country that is already finished? Do you run? Or do you do something no one expects?

## ACT 2: THE MAJOR WHO SWITCHED SIDES (3:15 - 6:00)

**[VISUAL: A map of Austria. A small town labeled "Wörgl" near the castle. A German officer standing in it, looking exhausted.]**

**NARRATOR:** A few miles away, in the town of Wörgl, meet Major Josef Gangl. Everyone called him Sepp. He was a Wehrmacht officer, a real one, a professional soldier who had been fighting for Germany for years.

**[VISUAL: Gangl looking out at the ruined countryside, thinking.]**

**NARRATOR:** And by May 1945, Sepp Gangl had done the math. Hitler was dead. The war was lost. And the orders coming down now were about fighting to the last man and destroying towns rather than surrendering them. Gangl looked at that, and decided he was done.

**[VISUAL: Gangl shaking hands in secret with a group of Austrian resistance fighters.]**

**NARRATOR:** So Sepp Gangl did something a German major absolutely was not supposed to do. He secretly contacted the local Austrian resistance, led by a man named Alois Mayr, and started handing them weapons and information. He switched sides.

**[VISUAL: Gangl's small group of loyal soldiers standing behind him, nodding.]**

**NARRATOR:** He wanted to protect the town of Wörgl from being destroyed by the SS in the last days of the war. And he had a handful of his own soldiers who agreed with him. Not many. A dozen, maybe two.

**[VISUAL: Gangl learning about the castle from a resistance messenger, eyes widening.]**

**NARRATOR:** And then Gangl learned about the castle. About the famous French prisoners inside. And about the danger they were in, because in the chaos of a collapsing regime, hostages like that tend to get shot before the enemy can free them.

**[VISUAL: Gangl deciding. He starts marching toward the castle with his tiny group.]**

**NARRATOR:** So Sepp Gangl decided to protect them. One problem. He had maybe a dozen men. And he knew the SS was coming for that castle in force. A dozen men was not going to be enough.

**[VISUAL: Gangl weighing his options on the road. A thought bubble showing an American tank.]**

**NARRATOR:** Think about the position he was in. He was a German officer. If his own side caught him, he was a traitor, and they shot traitors. If the Americans caught him, he was the enemy, and he was surrendering himself into their hands. There was no safe move here. Every direction he could walk ended somewhere dangerous. He picked the one that might save the prisoners.

**[VISUAL: Gangl reaching a road. In the distance, American Sherman tanks rumbling forward.]**

**NARRATOR:** He needed help. And it just so happened that the nearest help was the United States Army, which he had been at war with for years. So Sepp Gangl walked toward the American lines, under a white flag, to ask his enemy for a favor.

**[VISUAL: A young American officer popping out of a tank hatch, squinting at the approaching German.]**

**NARRATOR:** And the American he found was about to have the weirdest day of his life.

## ACT 3: BESOTTEN JENNY (6:00 - 8:45)

**[VISUAL: A young American officer, cocky grin, cigar, standing on a Sherman tank. Name tag: "Lt. Jack Lee."]**

**NARRATOR:** Meet Lieutenant John Lee. Everybody called him Jack. He commanded a group of Sherman tanks in the 23rd Tank Battalion, 12th Armored Division. He was young, he was aggressive, and by every account he was exactly the kind of guy you wanted in charge when a plan was insane.

**[VISUAL: Gangl explaining the situation with a hand-drawn map. Lee listening, cigar drooping.]**

**NARRATOR:** So a German major walks up and, through the language barrier and the sheer absurdity of it, explains: there is a castle, it is full of famous French prisoners, the SS is coming to kill them, and I, your enemy of five years, would like you to help me save them.

**[VISUAL: Lee shrugging, grinning.]**

**NARRATOR:** And Jack Lee, being Jack Lee, basically said, yeah, alright.

**LEE (character voice):** "A castle full of VIPs, and the SS is coming? Sure. Why not. Let's go."

**[VISUAL: Lee's Sherman tank rolling out, with the name "BESOTTEN JENNY" painted on the side. Gangl's men riding along.]**

**NARRATOR:** So Lee took his tank, a Sherman he had named Besotten Jenny, plus a small handful of American soldiers, and he linked up with Gangl's Germans. American tankers and German infantry, driving to the same castle, to fight the same enemy. Together.

**[VISUAL: A road sign. The little convoy of one American tank and a mix of American and German soldiers heading up into the mountains.]**

**NARRATOR:** Stop and picture that convoy for a second. An American tank. American GIs. German Wehrmacht soldiers. All rolling up the same mountain road, in the same direction, in a war that had spent years teaching both sides to shoot each other on sight. Nobody had a manual for this. They were making it up as they went.

**[VISUAL: The combined force arriving at Schloss Itter. Tanks and mixed soldiers at the gate.]**

**NARRATOR:** They got to the castle. And here is where the numbers get scary. Because when it came time to actually defend the place, the American force was just over a dozen men and, in the end, effectively two Sherman tanks. Gangl had his dozen or so Germans. Add the freed prisoners and a couple of others, and the whole garrison came to somewhere around three dozen people. And that was basically it.

**[VISUAL: A tiny cluster of defenders inside a huge castle, looking out at empty woods.]**

**NARRATOR:** A handful of Americans. A handful of Germans. One turncoat SS officer named Kurt-Siegfried Schrader who had also decided to protect the prisoners. And a castle full of French VIPs who mostly had not fired a weapon in years.

**[VISUAL: Lee positioning Besotten Jenny right at the main gate, barrel pointing down the approach road.]**

**NARRATOR:** Lee parked Besotten Jenny at the main entrance, pointing down the road the enemy would have to use. Then everyone waited.

**[VISUAL: The prisoners being handed rifles. Reynaud and Daladier accepting them warily, still not looking at each other.]**

**NARRATOR:** And then, because there were not nearly enough defenders, the guns got handed to the prisoners. Two former Prime Ministers. Two former generals. A tennis star. Told: you are going to have to help.

**[VISUAL: Night inside the castle. Defenders at the windows, weapons ready, watching the dark tree line.]**

**NARRATOR:** So the night before, this castle held one of the strangest garrisons ever assembled. Enemies who had become allies in a single afternoon, waiting together in the dark for the SS to come. Nobody there could have described the situation without it sounding made up.

**[VISUAL: Dawn. Tree line. Movement in the shadows.]**

**NARRATOR:** They did not have to wait long. On the morning of May 5th, 1945, the woods around the castle started to move.

## ACT 4: THE SS ARRIVES (8:45 - 11:30)

**[VISUAL: Waffen-SS soldiers emerging from the tree line in numbers, weapons ready.]**

**NARRATOR:** Out of the forest came the attackers. These were Waffen-SS troops of the 17th SS Panzergrenadier Division, a unit with the grand name "Götz von Berlichingen." And there were somewhere between 100 and 150 of them.

**[VISUAL: Side-by-side count: a huge block of 100-plus SS versus the tiny cluster of castle defenders.]**

**NARRATOR:** Let me make sure that landed. Over a hundred SS soldiers, against a couple dozen defenders. That is not a fair fight. That is a math problem with an obvious answer.

**[VISUAL: The SS wheeling up an 88mm gun and flak cannons on the road.]**

**NARRATOR:** And worse, the SS did not just bring rifles. They brought heavy guns, including the famous 88 millimeter cannon. A weapon designed to destroy tanks and knock down buildings. Pointed at a castle full of politicians.

**[VISUAL: The SS commander, Georg Bochmann, directing the assault from the tree line.]**

**NARRATOR:** The SS force was led by an experienced officer, Georg Bochmann. To him this was a straightforward job. Overwhelming numbers, heavy guns, a fixed target, and a garrison he had every reason to think was tiny. Which it was.

**[VISUAL: The first shells slamming into the castle walls. Stone flying. Defenders ducking.]**

**NARRATOR:** The attack opened up. Shells hammered the walls. Machine gun fire raked the courtyard. And the little garrison, Americans and Germans shoulder to shoulder, fired back with everything they had.

**[VISUAL: On the wall, an American GI and a Wehrmacht soldier reloading side by side, glancing at each other.]**

**NARRATOR:** Think about what that actually looked like. An American loading his rifle next to a German loading his, three weeks after they might have been trying to kill each other. Now they were covering each other.

**[VISUAL: The French prisoners in the fight. Reynaud, Clemenceau, La Rocque, and Borotra firing from windows and walls.]**

**NARRATOR:** And the VIPs did not just hide. Several of them grabbed rifles and joined the line. Former Prime Minister Paul Reynaud. Michel Clemenceau, son of a Prime Minister. François de La Rocque. And the tennis champion, Jean Borotra. All of them, firing at the SS.

**[VISUAL: Weygand and Gamelin, the two old rival generals, watching the fight from cover, giving quiet advice.]**

**NARRATOR:** Even the old generals had a job. Weygand and Gamelin had run the entire French army once. Now they were two elderly men in a castle under fire. But they had spent their whole lives around combat, and men who had never done this before were suddenly listening to them.

**[VISUAL: The 88mm gun crew re-aiming, lining up Besotten Jenny at the gate.]**

**NARRATOR:** But the SS had that 88. And the biggest thing the defenders had was Lee's tank, Besotten Jenny, sitting right at the gate. Both sides knew it. Take out the tank, and the gate was open. The 88 turned toward the Sherman.

**[VISUAL: A single massive muzzle flash from the 88.]**

**NARRATOR:** And it fired.

## ACT 5: THE LINE HOLDS (11:30 - 14:15)

**[VISUAL: The 88mm round striking Besotten Jenny. The Sherman erupting in flame, crew scrambling clear.]**

**NARRATOR:** The 88 round hit Besotten Jenny and tore it apart. The tank's fuel caught and the Sherman became a burning wreck at the gate. The single most powerful weapon the defenders had was gone in one shot.

**[VISUAL: The crew, now on foot with rifles, joining the men on the wall.]**

**NARRATOR:** The tankers bailed out and grabbed rifles and went back to the wall on foot. Because that is all there was left to do. Hold the wall, or die.

**[VISUAL: The defenders' ammo dwindling. A soldier shaking a nearly empty ammo box.]**

**NARRATOR:** And the fight dragged on for hours. The defenders were outnumbered, out-gunned, and slowly running out of ammunition. Everyone in that castle understood the math. If help did not come, the SS would eventually get over the wall. And these prisoners were not going to be taken alive.

**[VISUAL: Borotra, the tennis player, approaching Lee with a determined look.]**

**NARRATOR:** So someone had to go get help. And the volunteer for that job was the tennis champion. Jean Borotra offered to go over the wall, sprint through the SS positions surrounding the castle, and reach the American lines to bring a rescue.

**[VISUAL: Borotra crouched at the base of the wall, looking up at the top.]**

**NARRATOR:** This was close to suicide. The castle was surrounded. To get out, he had to physically go over the wall and run straight through the enemy. A middle-aged tennis star, unarmed, against the SS.

**[VISUAL: Borotra vaulting the castle wall in one clean motion and taking off downhill.]**

**NARRATOR:** So he did exactly that. Jean Borotra vaulted the wall, ran the gauntlet of SS strong points, and somehow made it through. The athlete's legs, it turns out, were good for more than tennis.

**[VISUAL: Borotra reaching an American column, breathless, pointing back up the hill.]**

**NARRATOR:** He reached American forces and told them what was happening at the castle. And then, because he apparently could not help himself, Borotra asked for a US Army uniform so he could turn around and guide the rescue back in personally. He had just escaped the castle. He volunteered to go straight back into it.

**[VISUAL: Back at the castle. The defenders down to their last rounds, the SS pressing closer.]**

**NARRATOR:** But relief was not there yet. And back at the castle, the situation was getting desperate. The defenders were nearly out of ammunition, the SS was closing in, and Major Sepp Gangl was watching the men and prisoners he had chosen to protect run low on everything.

**[VISUAL: The tone begins to shift. Colors muting slightly.]**

**NARRATOR:** And it is here that this story stops being unbelievable and becomes something else.

---

## ACT 6: THE ONLY MAN TO FALL (14:15 - 16:30)

**[VISUAL: Muted colors now. Sepp Gangl moving carefully along the wall, checking on the men. No jokes. TONE: STRAIGHT.]**

**NARRATOR:** As the fighting went on, Sepp Gangl kept moving among the defenders, directing them, keeping the thin line together. He had started this. He had walked to his enemies and asked them to help him save strangers. And he stayed in the worst of it.

**[VISUAL: Paul Reynaud, the former Prime Minister, exposed in the line of fire. Gangl seeing it.]**

**NARRATOR:** At one point, former Prime Minister Paul Reynaud was in the open, in the line of fire. Gangl saw it. And he moved to get Reynaud out of the way, to put himself between the old man and the guns.

**[VISUAL: A single sniper's rifle in the distance. TONE: STRAIGHT.]**

**NARRATOR:** As he did, an SS sniper found him.

**[VISUAL: Gangl struck. He falls. Quiet. No dramatics. TONE: STRAIGHT.]**

**NARRATOR:** Major Josef Gangl was hit and killed. He was the only defender to die in the battle. A German officer, killed by German bullets, protecting a French prisoner, while fighting alongside Americans. In the last days of a war he had already decided was wrong.

**[VISUAL: The men around him pausing, then continuing the fight because they have to. TONE: STRAIGHT.]**

**NARRATOR:** Four others were wounded. Gangl was the one who did not get up.

**[VISUAL: A quiet shot of Gangl. Hold on it. TONE: STRAIGHT.]**

**NARRATOR:** After the war, Austria did not forget him. Sepp Gangl is honored as a hero of the Austrian resistance. In the town of Wörgl, the one he had tried to protect, there is a street that carries his name. He was thirty-four years old. A few months earlier he had been a serving German officer. He ended as a man who died defending the people his own government had tried to imprison and kill.

**[VISUAL: The battle still raging around the fallen major. The defenders holding, barely.]**

**NARRATOR:** The line held. But it could not hold much longer. And on the road below, something was finally coming.

## ACT 7: RELIEF (16:30 - 18:30)

**[VISUAL: American infantry and armor of the relief column racing up the mountain road.]**

**NARRATOR:** Jean Borotra had gotten through. And the message got where it needed to go. A relief force from the 142nd Infantry Regiment of the 36th Division came driving hard up the road toward the castle.

**[VISUAL: The relief column smashing into the SS positions from behind and the flank.]**

**NARRATOR:** They hit the SS in the late afternoon. American reinforcements slammed into the attackers who had spent all day trying to crack the castle. And after hours of holding on by their fingernails, the defenders finally saw help arrive. For the men on the wall, nearly out of bullets, it was the difference between living and not.

**[VISUAL: The clock in the scene reads about 4 in the afternoon. The SS attack breaking apart.]**

**NARRATOR:** By around four in the afternoon on May 5th, the relief force had broken the siege. The SS attack collapsed. The castle held. And every prisoner inside was alive, except for the man who had come to save them.

**[VISUAL: The defenders and prisoners emerging into the courtyard, exhausted. Americans, Germans, and French together.]**

**NARRATOR:** So take one last look at the courtyard. American tankers. German soldiers. A turncoat SS officer. Two former French Prime Ministers who hated each other. Two rival generals. A tennis champion who had just outrun an entire SS division. All of them standing together, alive, in the same castle.

**[VISUAL: Jack Lee lighting a fresh cigar, looking at the wreck of Besotten Jenny.]**

**NARRATOR:** Jack Lee, the young lieutenant who said "sure, why not" to the maddest mission of his life, would later be promoted to captain and awarded the Distinguished Service Cross for the defense of Castle Itter.

**[VISUAL: A calendar. May 5th circled. Two days later, May 7th, stamped "SURRENDER."]**

**NARRATOR:** And then, two days later, on May 7th, Germany surrendered. The war in Europe was over. The Battle for Castle Itter was one of the very last battles fought in it.

## ACT 8: WHY IT STILL SOUNDS FAKE (18:30 - 19:30)

**[VISUAL: A stack of history books, one of them titled "The Last Battle" by Stephen Harding.]**

**NARRATOR:** Now, you will often hear this called "the strangest battle of World War II." And it is worth being honest: that is a nickname, not an official title. It comes from popular retellings and from books written about the fight, not from a plaque somewhere.

**[VISUAL: A checklist. Items ticked off: Americans and Germans allied. SS attacking. VIP prisoners. Tennis star escape. Two days before the war ends.]**

**NARRATOR:** But when you lay it out, you can see why the nickname stuck. American and German soldiers fought as allies, which almost never happened. The SS attacked a castle to recapture prisoners in a war they had already lost. Former Prime Ministers grabbed rifles. A tennis champion vaulted a wall and ran through the enemy for help.

**[VISUAL: Gangl's name on the Wörgl street sign, quiet, held for a beat.]**

**NARRATOR:** And a German major died protecting a French prisoner, fighting next to the men he had spent five years at war with. If it were in a movie, you would call it too much. It happened anyway.

**[VISUAL: The castle on its hill again, the way it looked at the start, peaceful.]**

**NARRATOR:** One castle. One day. Every side of a world war, on the same wall, for once pointing their guns the same direction.

## FACT-CHECK LOG

Well-documented late-war action. Beats verified against multiple reputable sources; popular exaggerations corrected.

- **Date and place:** 5 May 1945, Schloss Itter, Tyrol, Austria. Confirmed. [Wikipedia; Task & Purpose; Britannica] (HistoryNet's "May 4" refers to the buildup; standard record is 5 May.)
- **Context:** five days after Hitler's suicide (30 Apr), two days before German surrender (7 May). Confirmed.
- **Jack Lee:** Lieutenant John C. "Jack" Lee Jr., 23rd Tank Battalion, 12th Armored Division; promoted to captain and awarded the Distinguished Service Cross after. Confirmed. [Wikipedia; HistoryNet; Task & Purpose]
- **Besotten Jenny:** Lee's M4 Sherman, positioned at the main gate, destroyed by an 88mm round. Confirmed. [Wikipedia; HistoryNet; Task & Purpose]
- **Sepp Gangl:** Wehrmacht major who defected to the Austrian resistance under Alois Mayr at Wörgl, led the German defenders, killed by a sniper while protecting Paul Reynaud, the only defender to die. Confirmed. [Wikipedia "Battle of Castle Itter"; Wikipedia "Josef Gangl"; Task & Purpose] Street in Wörgl (Sepp-Gangl-Straße) named for him; honored as Austrian resistance hero. Confirmed. [Wikipedia "Josef Gangl"] Age 34 corroborated via biographical sources.
- **Schrader:** SS-Hauptsturmführer Kurt-Siegfried Schrader sided with the prisoners and helped defend the castle. Confirmed. [Wikipedia]
- **Attacking force:** ~100 to 150 Waffen-SS of the 17th SS Panzergrenadier Division "Götz von Berlichingen," with an 88mm/flak guns. Confirmed. [Wikipedia; Wikipedia "Josef Gangl"; Task & Purpose]
- **Defender numbers:** American force "just over a dozen men and two Sherman tanks" in the battle; Gangl's Germans "roughly a dozen to two dozen"; ~36 total defenders per infobox. Kept as ranges. Confirmed. [Wikipedia; Task & Purpose]
- **Casualties:** 1 defender killed (Gangl), 4 wounded. Confirmed. [Wikipedia; Task & Purpose]
- **French VIP roster:** Daladier, Reynaud, Weygand, Gamelin, Borotra, Jouhaux, Clemenceau, La Rocque, Marie-Agnès Cailliau (de Gaulle's sister). Confirmed across Wikipedia, Britannica, Task & Purpose. Daladier/Reynaud and Weygand/Gamelin rivalries are well documented.
- **Prisoners who fought:** Reynaud, Clemenceau, La Rocque, Borotra documented as firing. Held to these named few, not "everyone." Confirmed. [HistoryNet]
- **Borotra's run:** Jean Borotra vaulted the wall, ran through SS positions, reached American lines, and asked for a US uniform to guide the relief back. Confirmed. [Wikipedia; Task & Purpose]
- **Relief:** 2nd Battalion, 142nd Infantry Regiment (36th Division) arrived ~16:00 and broke the siege. Confirmed. [Wikipedia; HistoryNet]
- **"Strangest battle of WW2":** popular framing, not an official designation. Stated as such in Act 8. Confirmed. [Wikipedia; Task & Purpose; History Guild]
