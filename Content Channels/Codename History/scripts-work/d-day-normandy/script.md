# EPISODE SCRIPT: HOW A FAKE ARMY WON D-DAY

**Target runtime:** ~10 minutes (~1,600 narration words at ~150 wpm, slower with pauses)
**Style:** OverSimplified voice. Deadpan, sarcastic narration. STICK-FIGURE DOODLE visuals (per Ryan) — simple stick figures, big circular heads, dot eyes, thick brow lines, flat solid-color backgrounds, bold ALL-CAPS marker text. Less animated than usual; Claude-design SVG stills.
**Story type:** Famous operation, told through the parts nobody knows. Real, heavily documented.
**Title (locked):** How a Fake Army Won D-Day | Codename History

> VOICE NOTE: The comedy lives in the NARRATION. The narrator explains the largest invasion in human history like it is the most stressful group project of all time. Countries and generals get roasted. The deception, the weather, the nap, the shoes: those are the jokes. The beaches are not. Omaha is played completely, silently straight. No jokes touch the dead.

**NARRATOR:** This soldier is holding a tank over his head with one hand. He is not strong. The tank is a balloon.

**[VISUAL: Zoom out. A whole field of stick-figure soldiers inflating rubber tanks with bicycle pumps, like it's a kids' birthday party. A stern stick general nods, approving.]**

**NARRATOR:** In the spring of 1944, the Allies built an entire army out of rubber, plywood, and lies. And that fake army was about to win the most important day of the twentieth century.

**[VISUAL: Text slams on screen, red marker caps: "AND EVERY PART OF THIS IS REAL."]**

**NARRATOR:** Everyone knows D-Day. Almost nobody knows how weird it actually was. And I promise you, every part of this is real.

**[TITLE CARD: "HOW A FAKE ARMY WON D-DAY" — hand-lettered marker caps, stick soldier saluting a balloon tank beside it.]**

## ACT 1: THE IMPOSSIBLE DOOR (0:40 - 2:00)

**[VISUAL: A cartoon map of Europe, almost entirely stamped with a big black boot labeled "GERMANY." Britain is a nervous little island off to the left.]**

**NARRATOR:** By 1944, Nazi Germany controlled almost all of Western Europe. If the Allies ever wanted to win, they had to get an army back onto that continent. There was just one small problem. An ocean of concrete.

**[VISUAL: The entire French coastline drawn as one long wall of bunkers, guns, and stick soldiers. Label: "THE ATLANTIC WALL."]**

**NARRATOR:** The Germans had spent years fortifying the coast of France into something called the Atlantic Wall. Bunkers. Big guns. Millions of mines. The German commander in charge of stopping an invasion was Field Marshal Erwin Rommel, and Rommel understood the whole war better than anyone.

**[VISUAL: Stick-figure Rommel, cap, arms crossed, standing on a beach pointing down at the sand with total certainty.]**

**NARRATOR:** He knew that if the Allies ever got ashore in force, Germany was finished. So the invasion had to be stopped right here, on the beach, in the first day. He called it, and I am quoting him, "the longest day."

**[VISUAL: The Allied planners around a table, staring at a giant map. A thought bubble over all of them reads one word: "WHERE?"]**

**NARRATOR:** So the Allies had a door to kick down, and the man behind it was one of the best in the world. Their entire plan came down to one question. Where do you knock?

---

## ACT 2: THE BIGGEST LIE OF THE WAR (2:00 - 3:55)

**[VISUAL: Map of the English Channel. Two arrows point at France. A short fat arrow to "CALAIS." A longer arrow to "NORMANDY."]**

**NARRATOR:** There were two obvious choices. Calais, which was the shortest hop across the Channel and the answer any sane person would pick. Or Normandy, which was farther, harder, and therefore exactly where the Allies decided to go.

**[VISUAL: A stick general grinning, holding up a lightbulb. Text: "OPERATION FORTITUDE."]**

**NARRATOR:** But the beaches of Normandy were a trap waiting to happen. So the Allies ran the greatest deception in the history of warfare, to convince Germany the real attack was coming at Calais. They called it Operation Fortitude. And they went absolutely insane with it.

**[VISUAL: The rubber tank field again, now huge. Inflatable planes on fake runways. Plywood landing boats. Stick soldiers painting a flat sheet of wood to look like a truck.]**

**NARRATOR:** They built a fake army across from Calais. Inflatable tanks. Wooden planes. Dummy landing craft made of canvas. From a German spy plane flying high overhead, it looked like a massive force getting ready to invade.

**[VISUAL: A stick-figure General Patton, helmet, scowl, standing proudly at attention in front of the balloons. Label arrow: "REAL GENERAL. FAKE ARMY."]**

**NARRATOR:** And to sell it, they put the most feared general the Allies had, George Patton, in charge of the whole thing. The man the Germans respected most was given command of an army that was, structurally, mostly balloons.

**[VISUAL: A radio tower blasting nonstop chatter squiggles into the sky. Stick operators typing frantically at empty desks.]**

**NARRATOR:** They filled the airwaves with fake radio traffic for units that did not exist, so German eavesdroppers would hear a whole army chattering away. But the masterstroke was not the balloons. It was a man.

**[VISUAL: A slick stick figure in a suit, sweat-drop of confidence, labeled "GARBO." Behind him, a chart of 27 little stick heads, half of them faded/ghostly.]**

**NARRATOR:** His name was Juan Pujol Garcia, codename Garbo, a Spanish double agent working for Britain. Garbo told the Germans he was running a spy ring of twenty seven agents across Britain. Every single one of those agents was imaginary. He invented them. He invented their personalities.

**[VISUAL: A tiny gravestone with a stick figure lying next to it. Garbo dabs a fake tear. A newspaper reads "OBITUARY."]**

**NARRATOR:** One of his made-up spies got so inconvenient that Garbo had him die of illness, then arranged a real obituary in the newspaper, and got the man's fake widow a pension. For a person who never existed.

**[VISUAL: Split screen. On the left, Britain pins a medal on Garbo. On the right, Germany pins a different medal on Garbo. Garbo shrugs, wearing both.]**

**NARRATOR:** The Germans trusted Garbo so completely that they gave him the Iron Cross, one of their highest honors. Britain gave him a medal too. Which makes Garbo possibly the only man in the war to be decorated by both sides, for the same job, of lying to one of them.

## ACT 3: A WEATHERMAN AND A NAP (3:55 - 5:30)

**[VISUAL: A stick general, five stars, labeled "EISENHOWER," staring out a rain-streaked window. Storm outside.]**

**NARRATOR:** So the target is set. Normandy. The date is set. June fifth. And then the weather completely falls apart. A massive storm rolls into the Channel. You cannot land thousands of boats in a storm. The whole invasion is about to be postponed for weeks.

**[VISUAL: A nervous stick figure in an RAF cap holding a weather chart, labeled "GROUP CAPT. STAGG." Everyone in the room stares at him.]**

**NARRATOR:** The fate of the free world now rested on a Scottish weatherman named James Stagg. Stagg looked at his charts and told Eisenhower there would be one tiny break in the storm. A window. About twenty four hours, starting June sixth.

**[VISUAL: Eisenhower, alone, dot eyes small, in a quiet room. A thought bubble: the whole armada, or total disaster.]**

**NARRATOR:** Think about that decision. Go now, on the word of one man reading clouds, and maybe lose everything. Or wait, and give the Germans more time to find out. Eisenhower sat in the silence. And then he said four words. "Okay. We will go."

**[VISUAL: Eisenhower quietly folding a small note into his wallet. The note, shown close, reads in plain text: "the fault is mine alone."]**

**NARRATOR:** He was so unsure it would work that he wrote a second note in advance, to be read if the invasion failed. It took all the blame personally. He put it in his wallet and told no one. Then he launched the largest invasion in human history anyway.

**[VISUAL: Cut to the German side. Rommel, in civilian clothes, cheerfully carrying a shoebox with a bow on it, walking AWAY from the coast.]**

**NARRATOR:** Now here is the beautiful part. Because the Germans also saw that same storm, they were sure no one would be crazy enough to invade in it. So they relaxed. Rommel, the one man obsessed with stopping this exact moment, decided it was safe to drive home to Germany to give his wife a pair of shoes for her birthday.

**[VISUAL: A dark room. Stick-figure Hitler asleep in bed, "Zzz." Two terrified aides at the door, sweating, whispering. Below them, tank icons frozen with a red "LOCKED."]**

**NARRATOR:** And the powerful tank reserves that could crush any landing? They could only be released on the direct order of one man. Adolf Hitler. Who, on the morning of the invasion, was asleep. And not one person on his staff was brave enough to wake him up.

---

## ACT 4: THE LONGEST DAY (5:30 - 8:00)

**[VISUAL: Night. A black sky full of tiny stick-figure parachutes drifting down over a dark green countryside. Scattered everywhere.]**

**NARRATOR:** It started in the dark, hours before dawn. Thousands of paratroopers dropped behind enemy lines to seize bridges and roads. The drops did not exactly go to plan. Men were scattered miles from where they were supposed to be, wandering the French countryside alone, in the pitch black, behind an entire German army.

**[VISUAL: A stick-figure paratrooper hanging by his chute strings from the pointed steeple of a village church, playing dead, eyes squeezed shut. Label: "SAINTE-MERE-EGLISE."]**

**NARRATOR:** One paratrooper, John Steele, drifted straight into a church steeple and got his chute snagged on the point. He hung there, off the side of the tower, and survived by playing dead for hours while the fight went on below him. That really happened. There is still a parachute on that church today.

**[VISUAL: Dawn. The horizon of the sea fills, edge to edge, with tiny ships. Hundreds of them. Then hundreds more. A German stick soldier in a bunker drops his coffee.]**

**NARRATOR:** And then the sun came up. A German lookout peered out at the Channel that morning and saw, coming out of the mist, nearly seven thousand ships. It was the single largest fleet ever assembled. He is said to have simply stared. There was nothing in the manual for this.

**[VISUAL: Map of five beaches, west to east, each labeled: UTAH, OMAHA, GOLD, JUNO, SWORD. Little stick armies pouring toward each.]**

**NARRATOR:** The Allies hit five beaches at once. Utah. Omaha. Gold. Juno. And Sword. A hundred and fifty six thousand men would come ashore before the day was done.

**[VISUAL: Utah beach. A wave of stick soldiers who landed too far down the sand, scratching their heads at a signpost pointing the wrong way.]**

**NARRATOR:** At Utah, the boats drifted off course and landed the troops in completely the wrong spot. Which turned out to be the least defended part of the coast. It is one of the only times in military history that getting lost was the best possible outcome.

**[VISUAL: A calm elderly stick general strolling the chaos with a walking cane, totally relaxed, pointing at the ground. Label: "ROOSEVELT."]**

**NARRATOR:** Leading them was Brigadier General Theodore Roosevelt Junior, son of the president, the oldest man in the invasion, who walked the beach with a cane because of his heart and his arthritis. Told he had landed in the wrong place, he looked around, shrugged, and said, "We will start the war from right here."

**[VISUAL: Sheer cliffs. Stick Rangers climbing wobbly rope ladders straight up a vertical wall while things are thrown down at them. Label: "POINTE DU HOC."]**

**NARRATOR:** At a cliff called Pointe du Hoc, US Army Rangers climbed a hundred feet of sheer rock, under fire, on ropes and ladders, to destroy a battery of huge German guns at the top. They fought their way up, took the position, and found the guns were not there. The Germans had moved them and left fakes. So the Rangers hunted the real guns down a nearby road and destroyed them anyway.

**[VISUAL: TONE SHIFT. Muted colors. Omaha beach. Grey water, grey sand. Stick soldiers in the surf under heavy fire from bluffs above. No gags. No labels. Quiet.]**

**NARRATOR:** And then there was Omaha.

**[VISUAL: Held on Omaha. Swimming tanks sinking beneath grey waves, only antennas showing.]**

**NARRATOR:** Omaha Beach was overlooked by high bluffs, and the Germans defending it were far stronger than expected. The special swimming tanks meant to protect the first wave were launched too far out in rough seas. Twenty seven of the twenty nine sank almost immediately, taking their crews down with them.

**[VISUAL: Held on Omaha. A single landing ramp drops. Grey. Still. The narrator does not joke.]**

**NARRATOR:** The men in the first wave walked into machine gun fire the instant the ramps dropped. One American company from the small town of Bedford, Virginia, was cut down in minutes. Bedford lost more of its sons that morning than any town its size in America. They were farm boys. Most of them were teenagers.

---

## ACT 5: WHY THE LIE MATTERED (8:00 - 9:15)

**[VISUAL: Slowly, color returns. Stick soldiers crawling up off the sand, up the bluffs, one behind another. A thin line of them reaching the top.]**

**NARRATOR:** But they did not stop. In small groups, with no plan left intact, soldiers crawled up off that beach one by one and took the high ground the hard way. By the end of the day, every single beach was in Allied hands. And now the fake army pays off.

**[VISUAL: Cut to a German map room. Generals pointing frantically at Normandy, but a big red arrow and the balloons still sit menacingly over Calais. Label: "STILL WAITING."]**

**NARRATOR:** Because the German high command looked at Normandy and thought, this must be a trick. The real invasion is still coming at Calais, from Patton and his huge army. So they kept their best divisions sitting at Calais, guarding a beach against an attack that was never, ever coming.

**[VISUAL: Garbo at a radio, calmly sending a message. The Germans nodding along, reassured.]**

**NARRATOR:** Garbo even helped seal it, sending the Germans a message insisting Normandy was only a diversion. They believed their favorite spy over their own eyes. The reinforcements that could have thrown the Allies back into the sea stayed put for weeks. The lie held long after the landing was over.

**[VISUAL: Sun over the beach. A prefabricated harbour made of floating stick-drawn concrete blocks being towed into place. Trucks rolling ashore.]**

**NARRATOR:** The Allies even brought their own harbours, giant floating ports towed piece by piece across the Channel, so they could unload tanks and trucks onto a beach that had no port at all. They were ashore. They were staying. The door to Europe was finally, permanently open.

---

## OUTRO (9:15 - 10:05)

**[VISUAL: Quiet wide shot. Rows of small stick figures on the beach at dusk. Simple. Still. A number appears softly in the sky: "4,414."]**

**NARRATOR:** It was not free. At least four thousand four hundred Allied soldiers were confirmed dead by the end of that single day, and around ten thousand were killed, wounded, or missing. The balloons and the lies got the army to the door. Ordinary, terrified young men are the ones who walked through it.

**[VISUAL: A stick figure scratches his head at the words "D-DAY" floating above him, question marks everywhere.]**

**NARRATOR:** And one last thing, since everyone asks. The D in D-Day does not stand for Doom, or Destiny, or Deliverance. It stands for Day. It literally just means Day-Day. The military used it as a placeholder, and it stuck to the most famous invasion in history. Which is somehow the most fitting part of the whole story.

**[VISUAL: End card. Subscribe button. Stick soldier saluting. Two thumbnails for the next episodes.]**

**NARRATOR:** For the true stories from inside this war that almost nobody tells, subscribe. Next time: the tiny Allied submarine that was sunk by its own toilet. Yes, really.

**[END]**

## PRODUCTION NOTES

- Visual style is STICK-FIGURE DOODLE per Ryan, not the persistent OverSimplified cast sheets. No new character-library sheets generated. Backgrounds are flat solid color blocks. On-screen text is bold ALL-CAPS marker.
- Comedy sits entirely in narration + the absurd beats (rubber army, Garbo, the shoes, the nap, getting-lost-at-Utah, Day-Day). Omaha is played completely straight, muted color, no labels, no gags. This contrast is the channel's whole tone rule.
- Cold open lands the locked hook image (soldier lifting the balloon tank) inside the first 5 seconds, cold, no intro.
- Relatable bridge in the first 20 seconds: "Everyone knows D-Day. Almost nobody knows how weird it actually was."
- Re-hook every act on a cliffhanger; open loop on "the fake army" seeded in the cold open and paid off in Act 5.
- Shareable "tell someone this" fact: Garbo decorated by both sides. Second: Utah, getting lost was the best outcome. Third: D-Day means Day-Day.

## FACT-CHECK LOG (accuracy is the moat)

- **Operation Fortitude / dummy army:** Real. Part of Operation Bodyguard. Inflatable tanks, dummy landing craft, plywood aircraft, and fake radio traffic simulated a First US Army Group (FUSAG) opposite Pas-de-Calais. Confirmed.
- **Patton commanding FUSAG:** Real. Patton was publicly placed at the head of the fictional army group to lend it credibility. Confirmed. (FUSAG on paper included some real formations; the visible props opposite Calais were the dummies. Script says "structurally mostly balloons," which is fair for the deception display.)
- **Garbo (Juan Pujol Garcia):** Real. Spanish double agent, MI5 codename Garbo, ran a network of ~27 fictitious sub-agents. One fictional agent was "killed off" via illness with a published obituary and a pension arranged for the fictional widow. Awarded the Iron Cross (Germany) and an MBE (Britain). All confirmed.
- **Choice of Normandy over Calais; Calais expected by Germany:** Confirmed. Pas-de-Calais was the shortest crossing and the expected site; the deception reinforced that expectation.
- **Rommel and the Atlantic Wall; "longest day" quote:** Confirmed. Rommel commanded Army Group B, reinforced the beach defenses, and argued the invasion had to be defeated on the beach in the first 24 hours ("the longest day").
- **June 5 to June 6 postponement; James Stagg's forecast; "OK, we'll go":** Confirmed. Group Captain James Stagg forecast a narrow weather window; Eisenhower postponed 24 hours then ordered the go.
- **Eisenhower's "In Case of Failure" note:** Real. He drafted a message taking sole personal responsibility if the landings failed; carried it and never had to use it. Confirmed. (Script paraphrases the sentiment, does not fabricate the full text.)
- **Rommel away for his wife's birthday (June 6):** Confirmed he traveled home to Germany for his wife Lucia's birthday; the pair-of-shoes detail is widely reported. Framed lightly.
- **Hitler asleep / panzer reserves delayed:** Confirmed. The OKW reserve panzer divisions could not move without Hitler's authorization; on June 6 his staff would not wake him, delaying their release for hours.
- **Airborne scattered; John Steele at Sainte-Mere-Eglise:** Confirmed. Widespread scattered drops; paratrooper John Steele's chute caught on the church tower and he survived by feigning death. A parachute memorial hangs on the church.
- **~7,000 vessels; 156,000 troops; five beaches (Utah, Omaha, Gold, Juno, Sword):** Confirmed standard figures for D-Day (Operation Neptune).
- **Utah landed off-target into a less-defended sector; Theodore Roosevelt Jr.:** Confirmed. Currents pushed the landing ~1 mile southeast; the sector was lighter; Brig. Gen. Theodore Roosevelt Jr. (oldest man, walked with a cane) said "We'll start the war from right here." He later received the Medal of Honor.
- **Pointe du Hoc Rangers; guns moved:** Confirmed. 2nd Ranger Battalion under Lt. Col. James Rudder scaled ~100 ft cliffs; the coastal guns had been moved inland and were found and destroyed nearby. Heavy Ranger casualties.
- **Omaha bluffs, stronger defenders, DD tanks sink:** Confirmed. Omaha was overlooked by bluffs and defended in part by the veteran 352nd Division; of 29 DD swimming tanks launched by the 741st Tank Battalion, 27 foundered in heavy seas. First-wave casualties catastrophic.
- **The "Bedford Boys":** Confirmed. Company A, 116th Infantry (29th Division) included many men from Bedford, Virginia; the town suffered the highest known per-capita D-Day loss (about 19 dead on June 6).
- **Casualties ~4,414 confirmed dead / ~10,000 total:** The 4,413/4,414 confirmed-dead figure is from the US National D-Day Memorial Foundation; total Allied casualties are commonly estimated around 10,000. Both cited as such.
- **Germans held reserves at Calais for weeks; Garbo's diversion message:** Confirmed. The German 15th Army was largely held at Pas-de-Calais expecting the "real" invasion; Garbo's post-landing message reinforcing the diversion theory is documented.
- **Mulberry harbours:** Confirmed. Prefabricated artificial harbours were towed across and assembled off the beaches.
- **"D-Day" meaning:** Confirmed. The "D" is a generic military placeholder for the day an operation begins (as "H" is for hour); it does not stand for a specific word.
