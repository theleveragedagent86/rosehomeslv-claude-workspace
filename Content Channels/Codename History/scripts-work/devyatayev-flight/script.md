# EPISODE SCRIPT: THE POW WHO STOLE A NAZI BOMBER AND FLEW HOME

**Target runtime:** 11-13 minutes (~2,100 narration words at ~150 wpm)
**Style:** OverSimplified format. Comedic animated history. Flat colors, simple characters, exaggerated reactions, fake dialogue.
**Story type:** Single true escape (real, documented). One starving prisoner, one German bomber, one impossible flight.
**Title (standard tier):** `The POW Who Stole a Nazi Bomber and Flew Home | Codename History`

> ACCURACY NOTE FOR PRODUCTION: Core facts (date, aircraft, ten escapers, the guard, the flight, the 1957 medal) are solidly multi-sourced. A few Soviet-era claims about the intelligence value and the Korolev connection are attributed on purpose, not stated flat, because Western corroboration is thin. Keep it that way. Do not upgrade an attributed claim into a hard fact for a punchier line.
>
> VOICE NOTE: The comedy lives in the NARRATION, not just the visuals. Deadpan delivery. The narrator explains an impossible escape like it is a very stupid heist that somehow worked. Nazi Germany and the camp get roasted for real, documented arrogance. Second-person "you" pulls the viewer in. The camp conditions, the death sentence, the guard, and what happened to the men afterward are played completely straight.

## ACT 1: THE ACE WHO GOT SHOT DOWN (0:40 - 3:00)

**[VISUAL: A confident Soviet fighter pilot in a fur-collared jacket, standing in front of a sleek fighter plane, thumbs up.]**

**NARRATOR:** Okay. Rewind. Before he was a prisoner, Mikhail Devyatayev was a genuinely excellent fighter pilot. A Senior Lieutenant. An ace with nine confirmed kills. This man was very good at his job.

**[VISUAL: A Bell P-39 Airacobra with a red star, dogfighting over a map labeled "near Lvov, July 1944."]**

**NARRATOR:** On the thirteenth of July, nineteen forty-four, he was flying near Lvov when he got shot down over German-held territory. And here is where being a good pilot suddenly becomes a very bad thing to be.

**[VISUAL: German soldiers standing over the crashed pilot. One holds up a list titled "PILOTS." A big red stamp comes down on it.]**

**NARRATOR:** Because captured Soviet pilots had a nasty habit of being executed pretty much on arrival. The Germans did not want to hold onto them. They wanted them gone.

**[VISUAL: Devyatayev quietly pointing to himself and mouthing the word "infantry" while pretending to be just another soldier.]**

**NARRATOR:** So Devyatayev did the only smart thing available. He lied. He passed himself off as an ordinary foot soldier. Nobody special. Please do not shoot.

**[VISUAL: Barbed wire. A camp near Lodz, Poland. Devyatayev sizing up the fence.]**

**NARRATOR:** They shipped him to a camp near Lodz, in Poland. And Devyatayev, being Devyatayev, almost immediately tried to escape.

**[VISUAL: Guards dragging him back. A document stamped in harsh red: "CONDEMNED."]**

**NARRATOR:** He got caught. And for that, he was condemned to death and sent to Sachsenhausen.

**[VISUAL: The grim gate of Sachsenhausen. Muted colors. No jokes.]**

**NARRATOR:** Sachsenhausen was a concentration camp. Being condemned there meant exactly what it sounds like. He was, on paper, a dead man waiting his turn.

**[VISUAL: Hold on the gate. Beat.]**

**NARRATOR:** So how is he standing on an airfield seven months later? For that, a total stranger had to die, and Devyatayev had to steal his name.

## ACT 2: THE DEAD MAN'S NAME (3:00 - 5:00)

**[VISUAL: Inside a barracks. A camp barber quietly working, glancing over his shoulder.]**

**NARRATOR:** Here is one of the darkest, strangest survival tricks of the entire war, and it is completely real. In the camp there was a barber who was part of the prisoners' underground.

**[VISUAL: A prisoner who has died of starvation, lying still. A number tag on his uniform. TONE: STRAIGHT, no music sting.]**

**NARRATOR:** A prisoner named Nikitenko had just died of starvation and disease. He was an ordinary penal prisoner, not a condemned one.

**[VISUAL: The barber carefully cutting the number tag off the dead man's uniform and sewing it onto Devyatayev's.]**

**NARRATOR:** So the barber cut the dead man's number off his clothes, and sewed it onto Devyatayev's. And he cut Devyatayev's number off, and left it with the body.

**[VISUAL: Split image. On one side, the death list crosses off "Devyatayev." On the other, a living man now labeled "Nikitenko."]**

**NARRATOR:** On paper, the condemned prisoner Devyatayev was now dead, and got cremated as a matter of record. And a perfectly ordinary prisoner named Nikitenko was suddenly, quietly, alive. They were the same man.

**[VISUAL: Hold. Let it land. TONE: STRAIGHT.]**

**NARRATOR:** He survived by wearing a dead man's name. That is not a metaphor. That is what happened.

**[VISUAL: A train of prisoners, autumn, being shipped to a fortified island. A sign: "USEDOM."]**

**NARRATOR:** At the end of October, this new "Nikitenko" got shipped off with a big group of prisoners to an island in the Baltic Sea called Usedom.

**[VISUAL: A heavily guarded island base. Rockets and test rigs in the distance. Signs reading "PEENEMUNDE."]**

**NARRATOR:** And Usedom was not a normal camp. Usedom was Peenemunde. The top-secret site where Nazi Germany was developing its V-weapons. The rockets.

**[VISUAL: A German officer smugly gesturing at the island's fences, water, and towers.]**

**NARRATOR:** It was considered escape-proof. Surrounded by sea, packed with guards, wrapped in secrecy. Prisoners were told there was only one way off this island.

**[VISUAL: A crematorium chimney, smoking, against a grey sky. TONE: STRAIGHT.]**

**NARRATOR:** The chimney. That was the way off the island. So naturally, Devyatayev started planning to leave by air.

## ACT 3: THE WORST FLIGHT SCHOOL IN HISTORY (5:00 - 7:00)

**[VISUAL: Prisoners in a work gang on the airfield, shoveling snow near parked German planes. Devyatayev sneaking glances at a bomber.]**

**NARRATOR:** Devyatayev's work gang was put to work on the airfield itself. Which meant that every single day, he was standing a few feet away from fully fueled German aircraft.

**[VISUAL: Devyatayev staring longingly at a Heinkel He 111, a big twin-engine bomber. A little thought bubble: a cockpit full of confusing German switches.]**

**NARRATOR:** There was just one small problem. Devyatayev was a fighter pilot. He flew tiny, nimble, single-engine planes. This was a Heinkel He 111. A big, heavy, twin-engine bomber, with German labels on everything.

**[VISUAL: Devyatayev poking around a wrecked bomber on the field, secretly examining the cockpit, memorizing dials.]**

**NARRATOR:** He had never flown one. He could not read the instruments. The labels were in German. The layout was unfamiliar. And there was no manual, no instructor, and absolutely no room to practice. So he did his homework the only way he could. He studied wrecked planes on the field, memorized where the controls sat, and worked out how a bomber probably starts, based on vibes and desperation.

**[VISUAL: Devyatayev mentally rehearsing the startup sequence at night in the barracks, eyes closed, hands miming switches.]**

**NARRATOR:** He rehearsed the whole thing in his head, over and over, so that when the moment came he would not have to think. He would just have to do it perfectly, on the first and only try, with people shooting at him.

**[VISUAL: A small group of prisoners huddled, whispering. Faces: Krivonogov, Kutergin, Sokolov, Nemchenko.]**

**NARRATOR:** He was not alone. A small group of prisoners was in on it. Krivonogov. Kutergin. Sokolov. Nemchenko. Ten men total, all of whom understood exactly what the price of failure was.

**[VISUAL: The men glancing at the guard tower, then at the plane. A calendar page: "8 FEBRUARY 1945."]**

**NARRATOR:** The date was the eighth of February, nineteen forty-five. Midday. Lunchtime. The plan was simple, which is a polite way of saying insane. Kill the guard. Take the plane. Fly.

**[VISUAL: Prisoner Krivonogov picking up a crowbar behind the guard's back. Cut to black. TONE: STRAIGHT.]**

**NARRATOR:** One of the prisoners, Ivan Krivonogov, picked up a crowbar and killed the guard, and took his rifle.

**[VISUAL: Another prisoner, Kutergin, quickly pulling the dead guard's coat over his prison rags.]**

**NARRATOR:** Another man, Kutergin, threw on the guard's uniform, so that from a distance, the work gang would look like it still had a guard. And then all ten of them made a run for the bomber.

**[VISUAL: The prisoners scrambling into the He 111. Devyatayev diving into the pilot's seat.]**

**NARRATOR:** Devyatayev threw himself into the cockpit of an aircraft he had never flown, on the most defended airbase in the region, with the clock already running.

**[VISUAL: His hand on the controls. The engines cough. Beat.]**

**NARRATOR:** And then the plane would not take off.

## ACT 4: TWO TAKEOFFS AND A LOT OF ANGRY GERMANS (7:00 - 9:15)

**[VISUAL: The bomber rumbling down the runway, gaining speed, and then just... not lifting. Devyatayev panicking.]**

**NARRATOR:** First attempt. The bomber rolled down the runway, faster and faster, and refused to leave the ground. For a fighter pilot in a bomber he had never flown, this is roughly the worst possible moment for a pop quiz.

**[VISUAL: Germans across the field starting to notice. Pointing. Running toward the plane.]**

**NARRATOR:** And now people were noticing. Because a stolen bomber sprinting up and down your secret airfield is, it turns out, hard to miss.

**[VISUAL: Inside the cockpit, the prisoners frantically figuring out the trim controls together, hauling on levers.]**

**NARRATOR:** Devyatayev worked out the problem was in the trim, the setting that controls whether the nose wants to go up or down. He got the men to help him force the controls. He lined it up. And he tried again.

**[VISUAL: German soldiers now sprinting alongside the runway, firing. Devyatayev nearly runs some of them over.]**

**NARRATOR:** Second attempt. This time, with soldiers running at the plane and firing, and Devyatayev very nearly flattening a few of them with the wheels, the Heinkel finally clawed its way off the runway.

**[VISUAL: The bomber lifting into grey winter sky over the Baltic. Tiny furious Germans shrinking below.]**

**NARRATOR:** A starving prisoner, in a stolen enemy bomber he had reverse-engineered by staring at wrecks, was now airborne. From the airfield, Devyatayev said, no one had even reacted to the roar of the engines until it was too late.

**[VISUAL: A German fighter, a Focke-Wulf, scrambling to chase them across the sea.]**

**NARRATOR:** Of course, Nazi Germany was not going to just let a bomber full of prisoners fly off with it. A German fighter came up to hunt them down.

**[VISUAL: The fighter lining up behind the bomber, then peeling away, its pilot slapping the gun in frustration.]**

**NARRATOR:** And according to the accounts, that fighter could not finish the job, because it had already used up its ammunition. The one plane sent to stop them showed up to the fight with an empty gun.

**[VISUAL: The bomber flying east, low over a snowy landscape, toward a front line marked on a map.]**

**NARRATOR:** So the Heinkel kept flying east, toward the front, toward Soviet lines, toward home. Which brings us to the last group of people who wanted to shoot Devyatayev out of the sky. His own side.

---

## ACT 5: SHOT AT BY EVERYONE, LANDED ANYWAY (9:15 - 11:00)

**[VISUAL: Soviet anti-aircraft gunners on the ground spotting a German bomber overhead and immediately opening fire.]**

**NARRATOR:** Picture it from the Soviet side. You are an anti-aircraft gunner. A German bomber, a real one, with German markings, comes flying straight over your lines. You do not know it is full of escaped prisoners. You do what you are trained to do.

**[VISUAL: Flak bursts around the Heinkel. The plane shudders and takes hits.]**

**NARRATOR:** So Soviet guns opened up on him too. The plane got hit. Think about that for a second. He survived being shot down by the Germans. He survived a death sentence. He survived a secret rocket island. And now, in the last stretch, with home right underneath him, he was nearly shot out of the sky by the exact army he was risking everything to reach.

**[VISUAL: The battered bomber coming down hard in a snowy field behind Soviet lines. Prisoners tumbling out.]**

**NARRATOR:** But he got it down. On the eighth of February, the same day it started, the stolen Heinkel came down behind Soviet lines, and ten men who were supposed to leave that island through a chimney climbed out of a bomber instead.

**[VISUAL: The ragged, starving men standing in the snow, being approached by Soviet soldiers.]**

**NARRATOR:** They had just pulled off one of the most audacious escapes of the entire war. Enemy aircraft. Enemy secrets. Ten prisoners, alive. You would expect a parade.

**[VISUAL: The men's smiles fading as stern officers with clipboards surround them instead. TONE shifting.]**

**NARRATOR:** They did not get a parade.

**[VISUAL: A grim interrogation room. The escapees sitting under a bare bulb, guards at the door. TONE: STRAIGHT.]**

**NARRATOR:** Because the Soviet Union had a brutal policy about its own people who had been prisoners of the enemy. It suspected them. A former prisoner was a possible traitor until proven otherwise. It did not matter how you had been captured, or what you had done to get home. The very fact that you had been in enemy hands was treated as a mark against you. So instead of medals, the men were interrogated by counterintelligence as suspected enemy agents.

**[VISUAL: Documents stamped "penal battalion." Enlisted escapees marched off toward the front line. TONE: STRAIGHT.]**

**NARRATOR:** The enlisted men among them were sent to penal battalions and thrown back into the fighting. Most of them did not survive the war. Of the ten men who escaped that island together, only a few lived to see it end.

---

## ACT 6: THE DEAD MAN GETS HIS NAME BACK (11:00 - 12:45)

**[VISUAL: Devyatayev, years later, in civilian clothes, being turned away from jobs, papers marked "former POW."]**

**NARRATOR:** Devyatayev survived, but the suspicion followed him for years. Marked as a former prisoner, he struggled to find work and to be believed. The man who stole a bomber from the Nazis was treated at home like he might be one of them.

**[VISUAL: A drawing board covered in rocket diagrams. A stern engineer figure labeled "Korolev" examining them.]**

**NARRATOR:** Now, here is where the story gets its famous twist, and where we have to be careful, because a lot of this comes from Soviet-era accounts. That island, Peenemunde, was where the German rockets were built. The escapees said they brought back information about the German missile program.

**[VISUAL: The Korolev figure listening as Devyatayev points at a map, marking spots. Label: "attributed, Soviet-era account."]**

**NARRATOR:** And the story goes that Sergei Korolev, the man who would later run the Soviet space program, interviewed Devyatayev about the site, and that Devyatayev helped point out where things were. How decisive that intelligence really was, we honestly cannot verify to the same standard. So we will just say the men were there, and they talked.

**[VISUAL: A calendar flipping forward: 1945... 1950... 1957. A medal appearing at the end.]**

**NARRATOR:** What is certain is the timing. It took until nineteen fifty-seven. Twelve years. And according to the accounts, it was Korolev who finally vouched for him and helped clear his name.

**[VISUAL: Devyatayev receiving the Hero of the Soviet Union star. TONE: earnest, not comedic.]**

**NARRATOR:** On the fifteenth of August, nineteen fifty-seven, Mikhail Devyatayev was finally named a Hero of the Soviet Union. The dead man had his own name back at last.

**[VISUAL: An older Devyatayev, decades later, quietly proud. A caption: "died 2002, aged 85."]**

**NARRATOR:** He lived a long life after that, and died in two thousand two, at eighty-five. A fighter pilot who was shot down, condemned to die, wore a dead man's name to survive, taught himself to fly a bomber by staring at wrecks, stole one off the Nazis' most secret island, and got shot at by both sides on the way home.

## PRODUCTION NOTES

**Voice choices:**
- Comedy sits in the narration and the situational absurdity (a fighter pilot teaching himself a bomber "based on vibes and desperation," the pursuing fighter with an empty gun, being shot at by his own side). Never at the expense of the dead.
- Second-person "you" used for the anti-aircraft beat to make the friendly-fire irony land.
- Punchy fragments for timing ("They did not get a parade.").
- Every act ends on a hook or cliffhanger fragment.
- The camp, the death sentence, the identity swap, the guard's death, and the penal-battalion aftermath are all played completely straight and marked TONE: STRAIGHT for the build.

**Accuracy corrections / careful framing (see fact-check log):**
- The intelligence value of the escape and the Korolev connection are deliberately attributed to Soviet-era accounts, not stated as settled fact, because independent Western corroboration is thin. Do not upgrade these to hard facts.
- Survivor count of the ten is given as "only a few" rather than a single figure, because sources disagree (Wikipedia: five died in action; another cited source: only three survived the war).
- No specific flight distance in km is stated, because a Tier 1 figure was not confirmed.
- Aircraft identified as a Heinkel He 111 (H22 variant per aircrewremembered and Wikipedia).
