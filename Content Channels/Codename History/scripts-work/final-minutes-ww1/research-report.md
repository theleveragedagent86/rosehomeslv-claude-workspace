# Research Report: END OF WAR: the final minutes of WWI

## One-paragraph summary

At roughly 5:10 a.m. Paris time on November 11, 1918, Germany signed the Armistice in Marshal Ferdinand Foch's private railway carriage in the forest of Compiegne. The ceasefire did not take effect until 11:00 a.m., six hours later, because Clause 1 of the armistice set hostilities to end "six hours after the signature" and the signing time was officially recorded as 5:00 a.m. In that six-hour window, with the end of the war known to every headquarters, Allied commanders kept attacking. Marshal Foch had refused a German plea on November 8 to halt the fighting while terms were considered, and General Pershing, who believed the armistice was a mistake and Germany should be beaten into unconditional surrender, passed the news to his armies without any order to stop offensive operations, leaving the decision to subordinate commanders. The result, by historian Joseph Persico's widely cited count, was roughly 11,000 casualties on all sides on the final day, about 2,738 of them dead, more total casualties than D-Day in 1944. The individually documented last men killed: British private George Edwin Ellison at about 9:30 a.m. near Mons, French runner Augustin Trebuchon at about 10:45 a.m. at Vrigne-Meuse carrying a message about soup, Canadian private George Lawrence Price at 10:58 a.m. near Ville-sur-Haine, and American private Henry Gunther at 10:59 a.m. at Chaumont-devant-Damvillers, one minute before the ceasefire, charging a German machine-gun crew that tried to wave him away. A 1919-1920 US House subcommittee investigated the last-day losses, heard a general concede that a stand-fast order would have jeopardized nothing, drafted the phrase "needless slaughter", then softened the report. No one was ever punished.

## Verified timeline

- Oct 3-4, 1918 - The new German government under Prince Max of Baden asks President Wilson for an armistice based on the Fourteen Points; weeks of notes follow while Germany's allies collapse and revolution spreads at home (Kiel naval mutiny early November). [Wikipedia, Armistice of 11 November 1918; History.com, Why WWI Ended With an Armistice]
- Oct 30, 1918 - Pershing sends a letter to the Allied Supreme War Council arguing the Allies should refuse armistice terms and press the attack to unconditional surrender. [Persico, Eleventh Month, Eleventh Day, Eleventh Hour; Providence magazine summary of same]
- Nov 7, 1918 - The "False Armistice": United Press president Roy Howard cables from Brest that the armistice has been signed, after US Admiral Henry B. Wilson passes him an erroneous report. Over 400 US newspapers run it. New York declares a spontaneous holiday and celebrates the end of the war four days early. Admiral Wilson later takes responsibility. [History.com, The False WWI Armistice Report That Fooled America; US WWI Centennial Commission, Breaking News of the False Armistice]
- Night of Nov 7-8, 1918 - The German delegation led by civilian politician Matthias Erzberger crosses the front lines and travels to the Compiegne forest railway siding. [Wikipedia, Armistice of 11 November 1918; Open University / OpenLearn, Erzberger: Negotiating the Armistice]
- Morning of Nov 8, 1918 - Foch receives the Germans in carriage 2419 D. There is no negotiation. Asked for his proposals, Foch replies "I have no proposals to make." Terms are read out; Germany gets 72 hours, expiring 11 a.m. Nov 11, to accept. [History.com, Why WWI Ended With an Armistice; Wikipedia, Armistice of 11 November 1918]
- Nov 8, 1918 - Erzberger pleads for an immediate provisional ceasefire while the terms are considered: "For God's sake, Monsieur le Marechal, do not wait for those seventy-two hours. Stop the hostilities this very day." Foch refuses; he had declared his intent to pursue the retreating Germans "with a sword at their backs" until an armistice was signed. [Persico, in Military History / HistoryNet, Wasted Lives on Armistice Day; Army Times reprint of same; Legion Magazine, Foch & Erzberger]
- Nov 9, 1918 - Kaiser Wilhelm II's abdication is announced in Berlin; a republic is proclaimed. [Wikipedia, Armistice of 11 November 1918; History.com]
- Nov 10, 1918 - The German government radios the delegation authority to sign. That night, German positions opposite the US 92nd Division set off flares and fireworks; Brig. Gen. John Sherburne later testified it was "perfectly evident" the Germans knew the war was ending. [Persico / HistoryNet; Sherburne testimony as reported in Persico and Brookline Historical Society account]
- Night of Nov 10-11, 1918 - On Pershing's front, Maj. Gen. Charles P. Summerall orders the 2nd Division, including the 5th Marine Regiment, to force a crossing of the flooded Meuse River in fog and darkness on narrow pontoon bridges under fire. The crossings cost more than 1,100 casualties in the war's final hours. [US Naval Institute, Naval History Magazine, The War's Final Night (Dec 2018); Persico / HistoryNet]
- ~2:05-2:15 a.m., Nov 11 - The final session begins in Foch's carriage; the parties work through the terms clause by clause. [Wikipedia, Armistice of 11 November 1918; NatWest Group Remembers, Armistice Day 1918]
- ~5:10-5:20 a.m., Nov 11 - The Armistice is signed. Accounts of the exact minute vary (5:10 per the UK government history blog and Persico; some sources give up to 5:45). The official time is recorded as 5:00 a.m., which under Clause 1 ("six hours after the signature") fixes the ceasefire at exactly 11:00 a.m. Signatories: Foch and British First Sea Lord Admiral Rosslyn Wemyss for the Allies; Erzberger, Count Alfred von Oberndorff, Maj. Gen. Detlof von Winterfeldt and Navy Captain Ernst Vanselow for Germany. [UK Government History Blog (Tara Finn, FCO Historians); Wikipedia, Armistice of 11 November 1918; German History in Documents and Images (GHDI), armistice text; Persico / HistoryNet]
- At the signing - Erzberger protests the severity of the terms, closing "A nation of seventy millions can suffer, but it cannot die." Foch is reported to have replied only "Tres bien" and to have declined a handshake. [Wikipedia, Matthias Erzberger; OpenLearn, Erzberger: Negotiating the Armistice] (the handshake refusal is widely reported but anecdotal, phrase as "reportedly")
- ~5:45-6:00 a.m., Nov 11 - Foch's message goes out to Allied commanders: "Hostilities will be stopped on the entire front beginning at 11 o'clock, November 11th (French hour)." Pershing's headquarters is informed by around 6:00 a.m. AEF headquarters transmits the news without any instruction to halt local offensives, leaving discretion to army and corps commanders. [National WWI Museum and Memorial, Armistice / Nov. 11, 1918 pages; UK Government History Blog; Persico / HistoryNet]
- ~7:00-9:30 a.m., Nov 11 - The US 26th "Yankee" Division is first told only its artillery need fire until 11:00 and the infantry will stand fast; roughly an hour later the order is countermanded and the division is ordered to attack at 9:30 a.m. and advance until 11:00. [The Angry Staff Officer blog citing 26th Division records; Persico, Eleventh Month, Eleventh Day, Eleventh Hour] (specific order sequence rests mainly on the divisional account; see Gaps)
- ~8:00-11:00 a.m., Nov 11 - Maj. Gen. William M. Wright's 89th Division attacks and takes Stenay, the last sizable town captured by the AEF, at a cost of 365 casualties including 61 dead. His stated rationale included that the division "had been in the line a considerable period without proper bathing facilities" and Stenay had them. [Persico / HistoryNet; Wikipedia, William M. Wright; History of the 89th Division, U.S.A. (George H. English, 1920, divisional history)]
- ~9:30 a.m., Nov 11 - Private George Edwin Ellison, 5th Royal Irish Lancers, age 40, a Leeds coal miner and 1914 veteran of the first fighting at Mons, is shot by a sniper on the outskirts of Mons. He is the last British soldier killed in action in the war. His son James turned five days later. [Royal British Legion, The first and last soldiers to be killed in WWI; Western Front Association; CWGC casualty record 894827; Wikipedia]
- ~10:30 a.m., Nov 11 - The US 92nd Division, Black soldiers under white officers, engaged since 5 a.m., is ordered into a final charge at 10:30. Brig. Gen. John Sherburne later testified to the "absolutely needless waste of life" and said his units did not celebrate the armistice at all. [Persico / HistoryNet; Brookline Historical Society, Another Side of Armistice Day, quoting the hearing testimony]
- ~10:45 a.m., Nov 11 - Augustin Trebuchon, 40, a shepherd from Lozere serving as a runner in the 415th Infantry Regiment, is shot and killed at Vrigne-Meuse during the last French operation of the war, the Meuse crossing ordered by General Henri Gouraud's Fourth Army. The message in his hand: muster at 11:30 for food (soup would be served after the ceasefire). Roughly 91 French soldiers died in this final operation. He is the last French soldier killed. [France 24, Augustin Trebuchon: France's last fallen soldier; Wikipedia, Augustin Trebuchon; The Connexion; Western Front Association]
- 10:57:30 a.m., Nov 11 - US Naval Railway Battery No. 4, one of the Navy's 14-inch railway guns under Rear Adm. Charles P. Plunkett, fires the batteries' last shell, timed so it would land just before 11:00. The batteries fired 782 shells total in France. [US WWI Centennial Commission, First US Naval Rail Gun article; Wikipedia, 14-inch/50-caliber railway gun] (both trace to US Navy records; see Numbers)
- 10:58 a.m., Nov 11 - Private George Lawrence Price, 28th Battalion (Saskatchewan Regiment), Canadian Expeditionary Force, is shot through the chest by a sniper as he steps out of a house in Ville-sur-Haine near Mons. A Belgian nurse runs to him; he dies two minutes before the ceasefire, the last Canadian, and traditionally the last British Empire soldier, killed in the war. [Veterans Affairs Canada, Canadian Virtual War Memorial; CBC News; Wikipedia]
- 10:59 a.m., Nov 11 - Private Henry Gunther, 313th Infantry ("Baltimore's Own"), 79th Division, charges a German machine-gun position near Chaumont-devant-Damvillers through fog. His sergeant shouts at him to stop; the German gunners wave him off; when he keeps coming they fire a short burst. He dies instantly at 10:59 a.m., one minute before the armistice, officially the last American soldier, and generally recognized as the last soldier of any belligerent, killed in combat in World War I. [National Archives (US), Records Shed Light on Last American Killed in World War I; Persico / HistoryNet; Military.com feature (Nov 2025); Wikipedia]
- 11:00 a.m., Nov 11 - The guns stop on the Western Front. In Paris the bells ring; London crowds mass outside Buckingham Palace (news had reached London around 10:30); New York fills the streets. Wilson's announcement: "The armistice was signed this morning. Everything for which America fought has been accomplished." [Imperial War Museums, Armistice Day 1918 in pictures; Library of Congress, World War I: Armistice Day; National WWI Museum and Memorial]
- Shortly after 11:00 a.m., Nov 11 - By Persico's account, a German officer, a Lieutenant Tomas, walks toward American lines to tell them his men are vacating a house they could billet in. The Americans, not yet told of the ceasefire, shoot him. Possibly the last man killed of the war, but thinly documented (see Gaps). [Persico, Eleventh Month, Eleventh Day, Eleventh Hour; retellings at MilitaryHistoryNow and History Learning]
- Nov 13, 1918 - In East Africa, Gen. Paul von Lettow-Vorbeck, undefeated and unaware the war had ended until a captured dispatch rider told him, takes the town of Kasama two days after the armistice. He formally surrenders at Abercorn on November 25, 1918. [CWGC blog, The final hour: the end of the First World War in East Africa; UK Government History Blog; Wikipedia, Paul von Lettow-Vorbeck]
- Dec 1918 onward - The armistice, concluded for 36 days, is renewed three times until the Treaty of Versailles takes over in 1919. Germany never formally surrendered. [GHDI armistice text; History.com, Why WWI Ended With an Armistice]
- 1919-1920 - The US House adds "Subcommittee 3" to its Select Committee on Expenditures in the War Department to investigate the Armistice Day losses. Chaired by Rep. Royal Johnson (R-SD), with Rep. Oscar Bland (R-IN) and Rep. Daniel Flood (D-VA); counsel Samuel T. Ansell. Gen. Fox Conner concedes under Bland's questioning that American forces "would not have been jeopardized" by an order to stand fast. Johnson drafts a verdict of "needless slaughter"; after a partisan fight and a Flood minority report, the imputation is stripped from the final report. Congress finds no one culpable. Separately, Rep. Alvan T. Fuller (R-MA) presses Pershing on the losses before the House Committee on Military Affairs. [Persico / HistoryNet; Army Times reprint; hearing records, War Expenditures, Select Committee on Expenditures in the War Department, 66th Congress]
- Aug 26, 1921 - Matthias Erzberger, vilified in Germany as a "November criminal" for signing, is assassinated by right-wing ultranationalists. [Wikipedia, Matthias Erzberger; OpenLearn, Erzberger: Negotiating the Armistice]
- Epilogue - In June 1940 Hitler forces France to sign its surrender in the same railway carriage, No. 2419 D, hauled out of its museum to the same spot in Compiegne forest. [Wikipedia, Compiegne Wagon; Sky History, The Compiegne Wagon]

## Key figures

- Marshal Ferdinand Foch, Supreme Allied Commander - hosted and dictated the armistice terms in his private railway carriage; refused Erzberger's Nov 8 plea for an immediate provisional ceasefire; ordered the pursuit continued until 11:00 a.m. [Wikipedia; Persico / HistoryNet; Legion Magazine]
- Matthias Erzberger, German Catholic Centre Party politician, head of the German delegation - a civilian sent to sign so the generals would not have to; begged for an early ceasefire, was refused; assassinated in 1921 for his role. [OpenLearn; Wikipedia]
- Admiral Rosslyn Wemyss, British First Sea Lord - co-signed for the Allies. [Wikipedia, Armistice of 11 November 1918]
- Gen. Maxime Weygand, Foch's chief of staff - read the terms aloud to the Germans on Nov 8. [History.com; Wikipedia]
- Count Alfred von Oberndorff (Foreign Ministry), Maj. Gen. Detlof von Winterfeldt (army), Capt. Ernst Vanselow (navy) - the other German signatories. [Wikipedia]
- Gen. John J. Pershing, commander, AEF - opposed the armistice, wanted unconditional surrender (Oct 30 letter to the Supreme War Council); his HQ passed the ceasefire time to armies with no order to stop attacking, leaving discretion to commanders; later told Congress he had no official knowledge before ~6 a.m., which Persico calls disingenuous given overnight radio traffic. [Persico / HistoryNet; UK Government History Blog]
- Maj. Gen. William M. Wright, commander, 89th Division - ordered the capture of Stenay on the final morning, citing among other things the town's bathing facilities; 365 casualties, 61 dead; never punished. [Persico / HistoryNet; Wikipedia, William M. Wright]
- Maj. Gen. Charles P. Summerall, V Corps - ordered the 2nd Division / Marine crossing of the Meuse on the last night; 1,100+ casualties. [USNI Naval History Magazine; Persico / HistoryNet]
- Brig. Gen. John H. Sherburne, 167th Field Artillery Brigade, 92nd Division - star witness of the congressional inquiry; testified to the "absolutely needless waste of life." [Persico / HistoryNet; Brookline Historical Society]
- Gen. Fox Conner, AEF operations chief - testified that a stand-fast order would not have jeopardized American forces. [Persico / HistoryNet; Wikipedia, Fox Conner]
- Rear Adm. Charles P. Plunkett - commanded the US Navy 14-inch railway batteries; his Battery 4 fired its last shell at 10:57:30 a.m. [US WWI Centennial Commission; Wikipedia]
- Pvt. Henry N. Gunther, 313th Infantry, 79th Division - Baltimore German-American bank clerk, demoted from supply sergeant to private after censors intercepted a letter home criticizing the war and advising a friend to dodge the draft; spent his last weeks trying to redeem himself; killed at 10:59 a.m. charging a machine gun; posthumously restored to sergeant, awarded the Distinguished Service Cross and a divisional citation; named in Pershing's order of the day as the last American killed. Fiancee Olga Gruebl never married. [National Archives article; Military.com; Persico / HistoryNet; Wikipedia]
- Pvt. George Lawrence Price, 28th Battalion CEF - Nova Scotia-born, killed by a sniper at 10:58 a.m. at Ville-sur-Haine; last Canadian and traditionally last British Empire soldier killed. [Veterans Affairs Canada; CBC News]
- Pvt. George Edwin Ellison, 5th Royal Irish Lancers - fought at Mons in 1914 and died outside Mons at ~9:30 a.m. in 1918; last British soldier killed; buried at St Symphorien facing John Parr, the first British soldier killed, about seven yards away. [Royal British Legion; CWGC; Western Front Association]
- Pvt. John Parr, Middlesex Regiment - first British soldier killed (Aug 21, 1914, age 17, having lied about his age); shares St Symphorien cemetery with Ellison and Price. [Royal British Legion; CWGC]
- Soldat Augustin Trebuchon, 415th Infantry Regiment - shepherd and accordion player from Lozere, volunteered Aug 4, 1914, survived Verdun, the Somme and Artois as a runner; killed ~10:45 a.m. at Vrigne-Meuse carrying the soup message; last French soldier killed; grave 13 at Vrigne-Meuse, dated November 10. [France 24; Wikipedia; The Connexion; Western Front Association]
- Gen. Henri Gouraud, French Fourth Army - ordered the 163rd Division's Meuse crossing at Vrigne-Meuse in the war's last days. [Wikipedia, Augustin Trebuchon; France 24]
- Rep. Royal Johnson (R-SD) - chaired Subcommittee 3; himself a decorated WWI veteran; drafted then softened the "needless slaughter" verdict. [Persico / HistoryNet]
- Rep. Alvan T. Fuller (R-MA) - pressed Pershing before the House Military Affairs Committee on why the attacks went ahead. He did NOT chair the investigation (see Myth vs record). [Persico / HistoryNet]
- Gen. Paul von Lettow-Vorbeck - German commander in East Africa, still fighting (and winning) after Nov 11; surrendered undefeated Nov 25, 1918. [CWGC blog; Wikipedia]

## Numbers (high-risk, source each)

- Signing time: ~5:10 a.m. Paris time (UK Government History Blog: "agreed at 5.10am"; Persico: signed 5:10, officially recorded as 5:00; Wikipedia notes source range 5:00 to 5:45). Officially recorded as 5:00 a.m. Effective 11:00 a.m. Paris time. [UK Government History Blog; Persico / HistoryNet; Wikipedia]
- Clause 1 of the armistice: "Cessation of hostilities on land and in the air six hours after the signature of the Armistice." [German History in Documents and Images (German Historical Institute, Washington DC), armistice text]
- Armistice duration: 36 days with option to extend; renewed three times; 72-hour acceptance deadline given Nov 8. [GHDI armistice text; Wikipedia; History.com]
- Total casualties Nov 11, 1918, all sides: 10,944 casualties including 2,738 dead (Persico's count, cited by Wikipedia); "nearly 11,000 casualties, dead, missing and injured, exceeding those on D-Day in 1944. Over 3,500 of these were American" (UK Government History Blog). Persico's own article: "losses on all sides that day approached eleven thousand." [Persico, Eleventh Month, Eleventh Day, Eleventh Hour; UK Government History Blog; Wikipedia; AP via Military Times]
- Persico's national breakdown for Nov 11: British Empire ~2,400 casualties, French ~1,170, German ~4,120, American ~3,500 (official AEF count 268 killed and 2,769 seriously wounded, which excludes US divisions serving with the British and French; Persico's conservative estimate ~320 US dead, 3,240+ seriously wounded). [Persico / HistoryNet]
- D-Day comparison caveat: the comparison (Persico's own) is total casualties, ~11,000 on Nov 11, 1918 vs roughly 10,000 Allied casualties on June 6, 1944. Confirmed D-Day DEATHS (~4,400) exceeded Nov 11 deaths (~2,738). Script should say "more casualties than D-Day", not "more dead". [Persico / HistoryNet; UK Government History Blog]
- Persico's counterfactual: had Foch accepted Erzberger's Nov 8 plea, "some sixty-six hundred lives would likely have been saved." [Persico / HistoryNet]
- 89th Division at Stenay: 365 casualties including 61 dead, roughly 8:00 to 11:00 a.m. [Persico / HistoryNet; Wikipedia, William M. Wright]
- 2nd Division / Marine Meuse crossings, night of Nov 10-11: more than 1,100 casualties. [USNI Naval History Magazine, The War's Final Night; Persico / HistoryNet]
- US Navy railway batteries: 782 shells fired in France; last shell fired by Battery 4 at 10:57:30 a.m., Nov 11, timed to land just before 11:00. Note: the 10:57:30 figure appears in US Navy-derived accounts (WWI Centennial Commission, Wikipedia citing Navy records); treat the "timed to land" intent as the Navy's own account. [US WWI Centennial Commission; Wikipedia, 14-inch/50-caliber railway gun]
- Vrigne-Meuse (last French operation, Nov 9-11): ~91 French dead; 18 of the French soldiers killed there are buried at Vrigne-Meuse with crosses dated November 10, 1918. [France 24; Wikipedia, Augustin Trebuchon; The Connexion (18 graves, museum director Carole Marquet-Morelle quoted)]
- Times of last deaths: Ellison ~9:30 a.m.; Trebuchon ~10:45 a.m. (some accounts 10:50); Price 10:58 a.m.; Gunther 10:59 a.m. [Royal British Legion; France 24 / Wikipedia; Veterans Affairs Canada; National Archives / Persico]
- Ages: Ellison 40; Trebuchon 40; Price 25; Gunther 23 (born June 6, 1895). [Royal British Legion; Wikipedia; Veterans Affairs Canada; Military.com]
- East Africa: Lettow-Vorbeck took Kasama Nov 13; surrendered ~1,500 remaining men at Abercorn Nov 25, 1918. [CWGC blog; Wikipedia, Paul von Lettow-Vorbeck]
- False Armistice, Nov 7: UP report reached 400+ US newspapers; cleanup of New York's premature party was estimated at $85,000 (some New Yorkers suggested billing United Press). [History.com; US WWI Centennial Commission]

## Absurd / comedic beats (all real)

- The bathhouse offensive: Gen. Wright's 89th Division attacked Stenay on the war's final morning partly, in his own explanation, because the division "had been in the line a considerable period without proper bathing facilities" and if the Germans kept the town "our troops would be deprived of the probable bathing facilities there." 365 casualties, 61 dead, for showers the division could have walked into peacefully days later. Wright's other explanation to Congress: nobody told him how long the armistice would last, so he improved his tactical position. [Persico / HistoryNet; Wikipedia, William M. Wright]
- The soup message: the last French soldier killed, Trebuchon, was a runner shot at ~10:45 a.m. carrying a message that read, in effect, "Muster at 11:30 for food", telling the men hot soup would be served after the ceasefire. He was 15 minutes from the soup. [France 24; Wikipedia; Western Front Association]
- The artillery deadline flex: the US Navy's 14-inch railway gun crews fired their last shell at 10:57:30 a.m., timed so it would land on the Germans just before 11:00. Getting the war's last word in, by ballistics. Many Allied batteries similarly fired off stockpiles all morning. [US WWI Centennial Commission; Wikipedia]
- The take-it-or-leave-it "negotiation": asked what his armistice proposals were, Foch answered "I have no proposals to make." The terms were read at the Germans, not discussed with them. Erzberger's closing protest, "A nation of seventy millions can suffer, but it cannot die", got a reported "Tres bien" and no handshake. [History.com; Wikipedia, Matthias Erzberger; OpenLearn]
- America partied four days early: on Nov 7 the United Press flashed a false armistice report; New York shut down, barbers abandoned half-shaved customers, and the Associated Press suggested court-martialing the UP president. Four days later everyone had to celebrate again, this time correctly. [History.com; US WWI Centennial Commission]
- The order flip-flop: the 26th "Yankee" Division was told around 7 a.m. that its infantry could stand down and only artillery need fire until 11:00. About an hour later that was countermanded: attack at 9:30 a.m., keep advancing until 11:00, then stop wherever you are standing. [Angry Staff Officer citing 26th Division records; Persico covers the division's final-morning attack] (order-sequence detail is single-anchored; see Gaps)
- Pershing's shrug: AEF headquarters knew at ~6 a.m. (and unofficially the night before) that the war would end at 11:00. Its message to the armies passed along the fact and no instruction to stop attacking. Asked later why, the AEF's answer amounted to: commanders' discretion. [UK Government History Blog; Persico / HistoryNet]
- The bookends of St Symphorien: the first British soldier killed (John Parr, 1914, a 17-year-old who lied about his age) and the last (George Ellison, 9:30 a.m. on Nov 11, 1918) are buried in the same small cemetery near Mons, their headstones facing each other about seven yards apart. Nobody planned it. The last Canadian killed, George Price, lies in the same cemetery. The war ended geographically where Britain's war began. [Royal British Legion; CWGC; Veterans Affairs Canada]
- The 1940 sequel: Hitler made France sign its 1940 surrender in the exact same railway carriage, hauled out of its museum hall to the exact same clearing at Compiegne. [Wikipedia, Compiegne Wagon; Sky History]

## Human-cost beats (play straight)

- Henry Gunther, 23, demoted for writing home the truth about the front, spent his final weeks volunteering for dangerous duty to win his stripes back. At 10:59 a.m., over his sergeant's shouts and against German gunners actively waving him away, he charged a machine gun through the fog and was killed by a five-round burst, one minute before peace. The Army gave him his sergeant's rank back after he was dead, plus the Distinguished Service Cross. His fiancee Olga never married. TONE: STRAIGHT. [National Archives; Military.com; Persico / HistoryNet]
- George Price stepped out of a Belgian house at 10:58 a.m. and was shot through the chest by a sniper. A Belgian girl ran across the street under the last minutes of the war to help him. He died as the church bells were being readied. TONE: STRAIGHT. [Veterans Affairs Canada; CBC News]
- George Ellison survived the entire war, from the retreat at Mons in August 1914 to the last morning, and was killed 90 minutes before the end, back at Mons where he had started. His son turned five days later. TONE: STRAIGHT. [Royal British Legion; Western Front Association]
- Augustin Trebuchon volunteered in the war's first days at age 36 and served four years as a runner through Verdun and the Somme. The 415th Regiment, ordered across the flooded Meuse after the armistice was already being signed, lost roughly 91 men in the war's last operation. His regiment was left out of the 1919 victory parade. TONE: STRAIGHT. [France 24; Wikipedia; Western Front Association]
- The Marines and soldiers of the 2nd Division crossed the Meuse at night on 30-inch-wide pontoon bridges in freezing fog under machine-gun fire, hours before a ceasefire their commanders knew was coming. More than 1,100 casualties. TONE: STRAIGHT. [USNI Naval History Magazine; Persico / HistoryNet]
- The 92nd Division, Black Americans who had been given the worst equipment and the least credit, were ordered into a final charge at 10:30 a.m. Gen. Sherburne: "the effect of what we all considered an absolutely needless waste of life was such that I do not think any unit that I commanded took any part in any celebration of the armistice." TONE: STRAIGHT. [Persico / HistoryNet; Brookline Historical Society]
- Roughly 2,738 men died on the last morning of a war that was already over on paper, about the war's average daily toll, except this day every headquarters knew the ending and the time. TONE: STRAIGHT. [Persico; Wikipedia; UK Government History Blog]
- While men died at 10:59, crowds in Paris, London and New York were already celebrating. London knew by 10:30 a.m. TONE: STRAIGHT contrast beat. [IWM; Library of Congress; NatWest Group Remembers]
- Matthias Erzberger, the civilian who signed to spare the generals, was murdered by German ultranationalists in 1921. TONE: STRAIGHT (epilogue). [Wikipedia; OpenLearn]

## Myth vs record (corrections)

- Popular telling: the war ended when the armistice was signed. / Actual: it was signed about 5:10 a.m. but took effect at 11:00 a.m.; roughly 11,000 men became casualties, about 2,738 of them dead, in and around that six-hour gap. [GHDI armistice text; UK Government History Blog; Persico]
- Popular telling: 11 a.m. was chosen for the poetry of "the eleventh hour of the eleventh day of the eleventh month." / Actual: the armistice text simply set hostilities to end six hours after signature, and the six hours existed to give the ceasefire order time to reach the front lines. The tidy 11:00 came from officially recording the ~5:10 signing as 5:00 a.m. The symbolism was a byproduct that everyone then embraced. [GHDI armistice text, Clause 1; UK Government History Blog ("to allow time for the news to reach combatants"); Persico / HistoryNet (signing recorded as 5:00)]
- Popular telling: Germany surrendered on November 11, 1918. / Actual: an armistice is a ceasefire, not a surrender. It ran for 36 renewable days and was extended three times until the Versailles treaty. German troops marched home in formation, which helped feed the later "stab-in-the-back" lie that the army was never really beaten. Pershing predicted exactly this: "What I dread is that Germany doesn't know that she was licked." [GHDI armistice text; History.com, Why WWI Ended With an Armistice Instead of a Surrender; Persico (Pershing quote, as given in Persico's account)]
- Popular telling: the guns fell silent everywhere at 11:00. / Actual: in East Africa, Lettow-Vorbeck, who had no idea the war had ended, captured a town on November 13 and only surrendered, undefeated, on November 25. Fighting also continued for Allied troops in North Russia. [CWGC blog; UK Government History Blog]
- Popular telling: the congressional investigation was chaired by Alvan T. Fuller. / Actual: Fuller was a Massachusetts congressman who pressed Pershing before the House Military Affairs Committee, but the dedicated inquiry was "Subcommittee 3" of the House Select Committee on Expenditures in the War Department, chaired by Rep. Royal Johnson of South Dakota, with Oscar Bland and Daniel Flood. Its draft finding of "needless slaughter" was watered down after partisan fighting, and no one was ever punished. [Persico / HistoryNet; Army Times reprint]
- Popular telling (French): no French soldier died on Armistice Day, as the graves say November 10. / Actual: the crosses of Trebuchon and 17 comrades at Vrigne-Meuse really are dated 10 November 1918, but they were killed on the 11th. The backdating is documented fact; the REASON is not settled. The often-repeated widow-pension explanation "has been discounted" per the Ardennes museum director; the leading explanation is that deaths on the day of victory were considered unbearable, politically and emotionally. Script must not state the pension motive as fact. [The Connexion (Marquet-Morelle quotes); France 24; Wikipedia]

## Gaps

- NOT FOUND: any Tier 1 archival confirmation of the post-11:00 death of German "Lieutenant Tomas." The story originates with Persico's book and is repeated by Tier 2/3 outlets; the man's full name and unit are not established in surviving records. Treat as "by one account, possibly the war's very last death", single-sourced to Persico, or cut.
- Single-sourced: the 26th Division order sequence (7 a.m. stand-down message countermanded, attack at 9:30) is detailed mainly in divisional-history-based retellings (Angry Staff Officer blog citing 26th Division records). Persico confirms the division attacked on the final morning. Safe version: the 26th was ordered to keep attacking until 11:00 after an initial reprieve; do not lean hard on "Field Order 105" specifics.
- Signing-minute conflict: 5:10 (UK gov blog, Persico), "between 5:12 and 5:20" (common in centenary journalism), up to 5:45 (some sources per Wikipedia). Recommended script phrasing: "a little after five in the morning, officially recorded as five o'clock exactly."
- The exact wording of Pershing's "licked" quote varies by retelling; it traces through Persico's account. Use with "Pershing said words to the effect of" framing or quote Persico's version and attribute.
- Foch's refusal of the handshake and "Tres bien" is widely reported but anecdotal; phrase as "reportedly."
- NOT FOUND: primary-source confirmation that the Navy's 10:57:30 last shot was officially "timed to land just before 11:00" beyond Navy-derived accounts (WWI Centennial Commission, Wikipedia citing Navy records). The time itself is consistently documented; the intent phrasing should be attributed ("by the Navy's own account").
- National Archives article "Records Shed Light on Last American Killed in World War I" (archives.gov) was repeatedly unreachable (HTTP 503) during research; its content was verified via search index and corroborating outlets. QA should re-pull it before publish.
- Veterans Affairs Canada's memorial page loosely says Price died "two minutes before the Armistice signing"; the armistice was signed ~5:10 a.m., he died two minutes before it took EFFECT. Use "took effect."
- All hinted beats in the brief checked out except one: the claim that the congressional investigation was "chaired by Alvan T. Fuller" is wrong (chair was Royal Johnson; Fuller questioned Pershing separately). The Stenay bathing quote, the soup message, the 10:57:30 railway-gun shot, and the backdated French graves are all verified (with the caveats above).
