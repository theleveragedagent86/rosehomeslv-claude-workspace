# EPISODE SCRIPT: END OF WAR: THE FINAL MINUTES OF WWI

**Target runtime:** 10-13 minutes (~1,700-1,950 narration words at ~150 wpm)
**Style:** OverSimplified format. Comedic animated history. Flat colors, simple characters, exaggerated reactions, fake country dialogue. Comedy dies completely in Acts 5 and the cost beats; those play straight.
**Story type:** Single-event deep dive (real, documented). The Armistice of November 11, 1918, and the fighting ordered during the six-hour gap between signing and ceasefire.
**Title (standard tier):** `He Died 1 Minute Before Peace | Codename History`

> ACCURACY NOTE FOR PRODUCTION: Every factual claim in this script traces to the research report for this episode. Several popular-telling beats are corrected (the "eleventh hour was chosen for poetry" myth, "Germany surrendered," the Fuller-chaired inquiry, the French grave-backdating motive). Do not re-simplify the corrected beats back into myths. Anecdotal items are flagged in narration ("by one account," "by the Navy's own account") and in the fact-check log. Say "more casualties than D-Day," never "more dead than D-Day."
>
> VOICE NOTE: The comedy lives in the NARRATION. Deadpan delivery. The narrator roasts real documented decisions (the bathhouse offensive, the last-word artillery, Pershing's shrug) and never the dead. Acts 5 and every marked cost beat go quiet and factual, zero jokes, and should be tagged TONE: STRAIGHT in the build. Build-cheap: visuals are consolidated around recurring anchors (the clock, carriage 2419 D, the fog field, the front map, the message slip); one image holds across consecutive narration lines. Real motion is only needed for the cold-open charge, the railway-gun shot, and the 11:00 silence.

## COLD OPEN (0:00 - 1:00)

**[VISUAL: Foggy field, November 11, 1918. A giant faint clock face hangs in the sky reading 10:59. A lone American soldier charges with a bayonet through the fog. Ahead, a German machine-gun crew is standing up, waving their arms at him to go back. MOTION BEAT.]**

**NARRATOR:** November 11th, 1918. 10:59 in the morning. Through this fog, an American private is charging a German machine gun. And the Germans are not shooting. They are standing up, waving him back.

**NARRATOR:** Because every man on this field knows the war ends in sixty seconds.

**[VISUAL: Same frame, slow push toward the charging soldier. The sky clock ticks forward a few seconds.]**

**NARRATOR:** The armistice was signed hours ago. The exact minute of peace has been wired to every headquarters on the Western Front. And men are still being ordered to attack.

**NARRATOR:** You have watched a clock crawl toward the end of something. A shift. A school year. These men were watching a clock crawl toward the end of the deadliest war the world had yet seen. Their generals were watching it too. And the generals said keep going.

**[VISUAL: Freeze frame on the soldier one step from the gun. Hard cut to black.]**

**NARRATOR:** To understand how a man ends up here, one minute from peace, we need to rewind six hours. And I promise you, every part of this is real.

**[TITLE CARD: "THE FINAL MINUTES OF WORLD WAR 1"]**

## ACT 1: EVERYONE KNOWS IT'S OVER (1:00 - 2:15)

**[VISUAL: Cartoon map of Europe, November 1918. Germany's allies peel off the map one by one like stickers. Germany stands increasingly alone, sweating.]**

**NARRATOR:** November 1918. Germany is losing, badly. Its allies have quit the war one by one. Its army is being pushed back every day. And at home, things are going great, if your definition of great includes open revolution.

**[VISUAL: German sailors at Kiel raising mutiny flags and tossing their hats. In the corner, the Kaiser tiptoes out a side door carrying a suitcase.]**

**NARRATOR:** The navy mutinies. Revolution spreads city to city. On November 9th the Kaiser abdicates, and Germany becomes a republic roughly overnight. Since early October, the German government had been asking President Wilson about an armistice. Everyone on Earth can see how this ends.

**[VISUAL: A newspaper press hammering out the headline "ARMISTICE SIGNED, WAR OVER" with a tiny asterisk nobody reads.]**

**NARRATOR:** In fact, America was so ready for the end that on November 7th, it celebrated the end of the war. Which had not ended.

**[VISUAL: New York street party. Confetti, brass bands, a barber abandoning a customer with half a shaved face.]**

**NARRATOR:** A United Press report cabled from France claimed the armistice was signed. More than four hundred newspapers ran it. New York shut down and threw one of the biggest parties in its history. Barbers abandoned customers half-shaved. Four days early. Over nothing.

**AMERICA (character voice):** "WE DID IT! ...We did do it, right?"

**NARRATOR:** They had not done it. The real signing was still days away, in a forest in France, inside one very specific train car.

---

## ACT 2: THE TRAIN CAR IN THE FOREST (2:15 - 3:50)

**[VISUAL: The Compiegne forest clearing at dawn, mist between the trees. A single railway carriage on a siding, labeled 2419 D. Static wide shot, slow push.]**

**NARRATOR:** This is the forest of Compiegne, and that is the private railway carriage of Marshal Ferdinand Foch, supreme commander of the Allied armies. Remember this carriage. It comes back at the end of this video in the worst way possible.

**[VISUAL: Inside the carriage. Foch, iron mustache, iron posture, on one side of a table. Matthias Erzberger, a round, exhausted civilian clutching papers, on the other.]**

**NARRATOR:** On the morning of November 8th, the German delegation arrives. It is led not by a general but by Matthias Erzberger, a civilian politician. The generals who lost the war made sure a civilian would sign for it. File that away.

**NARRATOR:** Erzberger asks to hear the Allied proposals for an armistice. And Foch delivers one of the coldest lines in diplomatic history.

**[VISUAL: Extreme close-up of Foch's unmoving face.]**

**FOCH (character voice):** "I have no proposals to make."

**NARRATOR:** This is not a negotiation. The terms are read at the Germans, out loud, take it or leave it. They get 72 hours to accept, expiring at 11 a.m. on November 11th.

**[VISUAL: Same carriage interior. Erzberger on his feet, pleading across the table. Foch, arms folded, a stone wall.]**

**NARRATOR:** And then Erzberger begs. He pleads with Foch not to wait those seventy-two hours. Stop the fighting today, he says, while we consider the terms. Men are dying every hour.

**NARRATOR:** Foch refuses. He had vowed to pursue the retreating Germans, in his words, with a sword at their backs, until the very last minute. By historian Joseph Persico's count, that refusal cost around six thousand six hundred lives.

**NARRATOR:** So the war keeps running while the paperwork clears. Which brings us to the strangest six hours of the twentieth century.

---

## ACT 3: FIVE PLUS SIX EQUALS ELEVEN (3:50 - 5:35)

**[VISUAL: Carriage interior at night, lamps lit, men hunched over documents. A wall clock reads a little after 2 a.m.]**

**NARRATOR:** On November 10th, Berlin radios the delegation: sign it. Around two in the morning on November 11th, the final session begins in the carriage, and they grind through the terms clause by clause.

**[VISUAL: Close-up of pens signing. The wall clock shows 5:10. Below it, a hand writes "5:00 a.m." in the official record.]**

**NARRATOR:** The armistice is signed a little after five in the morning, officially recorded as five o'clock exactly. And clause one sets hostilities to end six hours after the signature. Five plus six. Eleven o'clock.

**[VISUAL: The giant clock motif alone on screen, hands winding from 5:00 to 11:00.]**

**NARRATOR:** Quick correction, because the version you have heard is a myth. Nobody chose the eleventh hour of the eleventh day of the eleventh month for the poetry. The six hours existed so the stop order could reach the front lines. The symbolism was an accident everyone embraced afterward.

**[VISUAL: A telegraph key hammering. A message slip slides across a map of the Western Front, headquarters to headquarters.]**

**NARRATOR:** Foch's order goes out to every Allied commander: hostilities will be stopped on the entire front at 11 o'clock. The war is officially ending. All anyone has to do, for six hours, is nothing.

**[VISUAL: General Pershing at a headquarters desk, jaw set, holding the armistice message at arm's length like it smells.]**

**NARRATOR:** Enter General John Pershing, commander of the American forces, who thought this armistice was a terrible idea.

**NARRATOR:** Pershing had formally argued that the Allies should refuse any armistice and fight on to unconditional surrender. As historian Persico records his words:

**PERSHING (character voice):** "What I dread is that Germany doesn't know that she was licked."

**NARRATOR:** So when the news reached American headquarters around six that morning, the message passed to the armies gave the exact time the war would end, and no order to stop attacking. Every commander could decide what to do with the last six hours of World War 1.

**NARRATOR:** So. What did the commanders do with that discretion?

---

## ACT 4: THE LAST MORNING (5:35 - 7:40)

**[VISUAL: Map of the American front with unit flags. A large clock in the corner reads 6:00 a.m. and ticks forward through this act.]**

**NARRATOR:** Some commanders ordered their men to stand fast and live. This act is about the other kind.

**[VISUAL: Major General Wright at a field desk, pointing at the town of Stenay on the map. Above his head, a thought bubble containing a bathtub.]**

**NARRATOR:** Major General William Wright of the 89th Division ordered an attack on the town of Stenay on the final morning. His own recorded explanation: his troops had gone a long time without proper bathing facilities, and Stenay had bathing facilities.

**WRIGHT (character voice):** "The men need baths."

**NARRATOR:** Taking Stenay cost 365 casualties. Sixty-one men died. For showers the division could have walked into peacefully days later. Nobody ever punished him for it.

**[VISUAL: TONE: STRAIGHT. Night, freezing fog. Marines crossing the flooded Meuse on a pontoon bridge barely wider than a plank, muzzle flashes on the far bank. Muted color.]**

**NARRATOR:** On the war's last night, General Summerall ordered the 2nd Division, including the Marines, to force a crossing of the flooded Meuse River. In darkness and freezing fog, on pontoon bridges thirty inches wide, under machine-gun fire, hours before a ceasefire their commanders knew was coming. The crossings cost more than eleven hundred casualties.

**[VISUAL: An order slip stamped STAND FAST is snatched off screen and replaced by one stamped ATTACK. The 26th Division flag on the map.]**

**NARRATOR:** The 26th Yankee Division was told at first that its infantry could stand down. About an hour later that order was reversed, and the division was told to attack anyway and keep advancing until the final minute.

**[VISUAL: An enormous US Navy 14-inch railway gun. The crew checks pocket watches. The gun fires. MOTION BEAT.]**

**NARRATOR:** The US Navy had brought giant 14-inch railway guns to France. Battery Four fired their last shell at exactly 10:57 and 30 seconds, timed, by the Navy's own account, so it would land on the Germans just before 11. Getting the war's last word in, by ballistics.

**[VISUAL: TONE: STRAIGHT. Soldiers of the 92nd Division fixed in a trench, an officer's whistle raised. The corner clock reads 10:30. Muted color.]**

**NARRATOR:** And the 92nd Division, Black American soldiers who had been handed the worst equipment and the least credit in the army, had been fighting since five that morning. At 10:30, they were ordered into one final charge. Their own general later called it an absolutely needless waste of life, and said his men took no part in any celebration of the armistice.

**[VISUAL: The front map again. Flash icons keep sparking along the line as the corner clock ticks toward 11:00.]**

**NARRATOR:** All along the front, under clocks counting down to the same minute, it went on like this. And the closer eleven o'clock came, the worse it got.

---

## ACT 5: THE LAST MEN (7:40 - 10:40) [TONE: STRAIGHT THROUGHOUT. No jokes in this act.]

**[VISUAL: Muted palette from here to the end of the act. A quiet lane on the outskirts of Mons. A lone British soldier stands by a brick wall. A small clock in the corner reads 9:30.]**

**NARRATOR:** George Edwin Ellison was a forty-year-old coal miner from Leeds. He fought at Mons in August 1914, in Britain's first battle of the war, and he survived everything that came after. All four years of it.

**NARRATOR:** At about 9:30 on the last morning, on the outskirts of Mons, where his war began, a sniper killed him. He was the last British soldier to die in the war. His son turned five days later.

**[VISUAL: St Symphorien cemetery. Two headstones facing each other across a narrow strip of grass.]**

**NARRATOR:** Ellison is buried at St Symphorien cemetery, his headstone facing the grave of John Parr, a seventeen-year-old who lied about his age and became the first British soldier killed. Seven yards apart. Nobody planned that.

**[VISUAL: A French runner moving low along a foggy riverbank, message slip in hand. Corner clock: 10:45.]**

**NARRATOR:** Augustin Trebuchon was a shepherd from southern France. He volunteered in the first week of the war and served four years as a runner, through Verdun and the Somme. On the last morning, his regiment was ordered across the flooded Meuse while the armistice was being signed.

**NARRATOR:** At about 10:45 he was shot carrying a message to the front line. The message told the men to muster at 11:30 for food. Hot soup, for after the ceasefire.

**[VISUAL: A row of small white crosses at Vrigne-Meuse, each dated 10 November 1918.]**

**NARRATOR:** The crosses over Trebuchon and seventeen of his comrades are dated November 10th. They died on the 11th. The graves really were backdated, and no one is certain why. The explanation historians now lean toward is that France could not bear the thought of men dying on the day of victory.

**[VISUAL: A Belgian village street. A Canadian soldier steps out of a doorway. Corner clock: 10:58.]**

**NARRATOR:** George Lawrence Price, a Canadian private from Nova Scotia, was checking houses in a village near Mons. At 10:58 he stepped out of a doorway, and a sniper shot him through the chest. A Belgian nurse ran to help him. He died two minutes before the armistice took effect.

**[VISUAL: Return to the exact cold-open frame. The fog field, the frozen charging soldier, the German crew waving. The frame unfreezes. MOTION BEAT.]**

**NARRATOR:** Which brings us back to the fog.

**[VISUAL: Inset portrait beat over the fog field: a young bank clerk in a stiff collar, then a letter home covered by a censor's stamp, then sergeant's stripes being taken away.]**

**NARRATOR:** Henry Gunther was 23, a bank clerk from Baltimore, a German-American. He was a supply sergeant until army censors read a letter home telling a friend how bad the front really was, and to stay out of the war. They demoted him to private. He spent his last weeks volunteering for the most dangerous duty he could find, trying to win his stripes back.

**NARRATOR:** At 10:59, near Chaumont-devant-Damvillers, Gunther charged a German machine-gun crew through the fog. His sergeant screamed at him to stop. The Germans waved him away. They did not want to kill a man sixty seconds before peace.

**[VISUAL: The fog field. A short burst of muzzle flashes, then cut to black. The sky clock reads 10:59.]**

**NARRATOR:** He kept coming. A short burst. Henry Gunther died instantly at 10:59 a.m., one minute before the end, the last soldier killed in combat in World War 1.

**NARRATOR:** The army gave him his sergeant's stripes back after he was dead, along with the Distinguished Service Cross. His fiancee, Olga, never married.

---

## ACT 6: ELEVEN O'CLOCK (10:40 - 12:55)

**[VISUAL: The full front map. The clock strikes 11:00 and every flash icon along the line goes dark at once. Hold the silent frame. MOTION BEAT.]**

**NARRATOR:** At eleven o'clock, the guns stopped. The Western Front went quiet for the first time in four years.

**[VISUAL: TONE: STRAIGHT. Split frame. One side: the silent, cratered front. Other side: Paris bells, London crowds, New York confetti.]**

**NARRATOR:** And while Gunther was charging through the fog, the celebrating had already begun. London had known since about 10:30. Paris was ringing its bells. New York was filling the streets again.

**NARRATOR:** Historian Joseph Persico's count for the final day: roughly eleven thousand casualties on all sides, about two thousand seven hundred and thirty-eight of them dead. More casualties than D-Day. On a morning when the war was already over on paper, and every headquarters knew the minute it would stop.

**[VISUAL: TONE: STRAIGHT. A lone German officer walking across open ground toward American lines, hands visible.]**

**NARRATOR:** And by one account, the last man of all died after eleven. A German officer, Lieutenant Tomas, walked toward American lines to offer them a house his men were vacating. Nobody had told those Americans the war was over. They shot him.

**[VISUAL: A congressional hearing room, 1920. A general in the witness chair, congressmen leaning in, a gavel.]**

**NARRATOR:** Congress wanted answers. A House subcommittee chaired by Representative Royal Johnson, a decorated veteran himself, investigated. Pershing's own operations chief conceded that a stand-fast order would not have jeopardized American forces. Johnson drafted the phrase needless slaughter. The final report softened it. No one was ever punished.

**[VISUAL: The map zooms way out to East Africa. A tiny undefeated German column marches through the bush, its commander squinting at a captured note.]**

**NARRATOR:** Three footnotes. In East Africa, the German general Lettow-Vorbeck had no idea any of this had happened. Undefeated, he captured a town two days after the armistice, and only surrendered on November 25th.

**[VISUAL: German troops marching home in neat formation under welcome banners, heads high.]**

**NARRATOR:** Second, the armistice was a ceasefire, not a surrender. It was renewed three times until the Treaty of Versailles, and German troops marched home in formation. Germany got to tell itself it had never really been beaten. Which is exactly what Pershing said he dreaded.

**[VISUAL: TONE: STRAIGHT. Erzberger's portrait beside a 1921 newspaper, then a black ribbon across the frame.]**

**NARRATOR:** And third, Matthias Erzberger, the civilian who signed so the generals would not have to, was branded a traitor at home. In 1921, German ultranationalists assassinated him.

**[VISUAL: The Compiegne clearing again, 1940. The same carriage, 2419 D, hauled out of a museum onto the same spot. New officers wait in silhouette.]**

**NARRATOR:** As for the carriage. In 1940, after conquering France, Hitler had carriage 2419 D dragged out of its museum and placed on the exact same spot in the Compiegne forest, so France would have to sign its surrender in the same seats. Told you it came back in the worst way possible.

## PRODUCTION NOTES

**Voice choices:**
- Cold open delivers the locked package promise beat for beat: the 10:59 fog field, the Germans waving Gunther back, the freeze one step from the gun, the six-hour rewind, "every part of this is real."
- Relatable bridge (the clock crawling toward the end of a shift) lands inside the first 20 seconds, honest, no fabricated modern parallel.
- Comedy targets only documented decisions: the False Armistice party, Foch's "I have no proposals to make," the bathhouse offensive, the Navy's last-word shell, Pershing's shrug. Wright and Pershing get one short character line each; both are their real recorded sentiments compressed.
- Open loops: (1) carriage 2419 D seeded in Act 2, paid off in Act 6 with 1940; (2) the frozen cold-open charge, paid off in Act 5 with Gunther; (3) Pershing's "licked" dread seeded in Act 3, paid off in Act 6 on the stab-in-the-back beat; (4) "file that away" on Erzberger the civilian, paid off with his 1921 assassination.
- The one "tell someone" fact, stated cleanly: roughly eleven thousand casualties on the final morning, more casualties than D-Day, after the armistice was signed.
- Act 5 and all marked cost beats are completely straight. No jokes touch Ellison, Trebuchon, Price, Gunther, the Meuse crossing, the 92nd Division, Tomas, or the casualty count. Build should tag every beat marked TONE: STRAIGHT.

**Build-cheap notes (token guardrails):**
- 41 distinct [VISUAL] frames; consecutive narration beats share images throughout. Recurring anchors: the giant clock, carriage 2419 D interior/exterior, the fog field, the front map with corner clock, the telegraph/message slip.
- Only four MOTION BEATS: the cold-open charge, the railway-gun shot, the unfreeze of the charge in Act 5, and the 11:00 all-flashes-go-dark silence. Everything else reads as static illustration with a slow push.

- The congressional inquiry was chaired by Royal Johnson (Subcommittee 3), not Alvan Fuller.
- The backdated French graves are fact; the widow-pension motive is NOT stated (not settled).
- Price died two minutes before the armistice took effect, not before the signing.
