# Research Report: Last Stand of Taffy 3 (SETUP/CONTEXT HALF)

## One-paragraph summary

On 25 October 1944, Task Unit 77.4.3, radio call sign "Taffy 3," was a Seventh Fleet anti-submarine and close-air-support unit of six escort carriers, three destroyers and four destroyer escorts, doing routine ground-support work off the east coast of Samar in the Philippines. It was not a battle fleet and was never meant to fight one. Because the Japanese Sho-1 plan had split the Imperial Japanese Navy into three groups, and because a decoy carrier force under Vice Admiral Jisaburo Ozawa successfully pulled Admiral William F. Halsey's entire Third Fleet north while Vice Admiral Thomas C. Kinkaid's Seventh Fleet battleships were still south at Surigao Strait, San Bernardino Strait was left completely unguarded. Vice Admiral Takeo Kurita's Center Force came through it overnight and at about 0650 ran headlong into Taffy 3: four battleships including the 65,000-ton Yamato with the largest naval guns ever mounted, six heavy cruisers, two light cruisers and eleven destroyers, against thirteen thin-skinned ships whose heaviest weapon was a single 5-inch gun per carrier. The gap that let it happen was a communications failure between two American fleets that reported to two different theater commanders and were forbidden to talk directly, compounded by an ambiguously worded dispatch about a battle line called "Task Force 34" that Halsey meant as a contingency and that Kinkaid, Nimitz and the White House all read as an accomplished fact.

## Verified timeline

- **10 to 16 October 1944** - Halsey's Third Fleet carriers strike Okinawa, Luzon and Formosa, destroying roughly 500 Japanese aircraft and gutting the air component of the Sho plan before the surface battle even starts. Kurita, Nishimura and Shima will fight with essentially no air cover. [NHHC H-038-1; NHHC H-038-2]
- **20 October 1944** - MacArthur's Sixth Army lands on Leyte, roughly 200,000 US Army troops eventually put ashore. [NHHC H-038-2; National WWII Museum]
- **22 October 1944** - Kurita's First Striking Force departs Brunei. [NHHC "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency"]
- **23 October 1944, 0524 to 0556** - Battle of Palawan Passage. USS Darter (SS-227) sinks Kurita's flagship, the heavy cruiser Atago (360 of her crew lost), and cripples Takao. USS Dace (SS-247) sinks Maya (336 lost, capsized in seven minutes). Kurita has to swim for it and is fished out by the destroyer Kishinami, then shifts his flag to Yamato. He has lost 3 of 10 heavy cruisers and 2 of 15 destroyers before the main battle. [NHHC H-038-2; NHHC H-038-1]
- **24 October 1944, daytime** - Battle of the Sibuyan Sea. Third Fleet aircraft repeatedly hit the same ship, the super-battleship Musashi, which absorbs roughly 19 torpedoes and 17 bombs and sinks about four hours after the last hit, taking 1,023 of her 2,399 crew. Heavy cruiser Myoko is knocked out of the battle. [NHHC National Museum of the US Navy, Musashi page; NHHC H-038-1]
- **24 October 1944, about 1600** - Kurita reverses course to the west. Combined with inflated US pilot damage claims, this convinces Halsey that Center Force is beaten. [NHHC H-038-2; NHHC "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency"]
- **24 October 1944, 1640** - Ozawa's Northern Force is finally spotted northeast of Luzon, after Ozawa had spent days deliberately breaking radio silence trying to get noticed. [NHHC H-038-2]
- **24 October 1944, 1715** - Kurita turns back east toward San Bernardino Strait with his four still-battleworthy battleships. Toyoda's rebuke ("With confidence in heavenly guidance, the entire force will attack!") reaches him only after he has already turned. [NHHC H-038-2]
- **24 October 1944, evening** - Halsey takes Third Fleet north with five fleet carriers, five light carriers, six fast battleships, two heavy cruisers, six light cruisers and 40 destroyers. San Bernardino Strait is left with nothing guarding it. Independence scout aircraft report Kurita heading back east and navigation lights lit in the strait. Halsey continues north anyway. [NHHC H-038-2]
- **24 to 25 October 1944, overnight** - Battle of Surigao Strait. Rear Admiral Jesse B. Oldendorf's six old battleships (five of them Pearl Harbor survivors), cruisers, destroyers and PT boats annihilate Nishimura's Southern Force. Battleships Fuso and Yamashiro are sunk. Nishimura dies, but his sacrificial mission succeeds: it holds Kinkaid's heavy units at the southern end of Leyte Gulf. [NHHC H-038-1; NHHC "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency"]
- **25 October 1944, about 0240** - Halsey's battleships finally combine as Task Force 34 under Vice Admiral Willis Lee. Purpose: to finish Ozawa's carriers after Mitscher's aircraft hit them. Not to cover San Bernardino Strait. [NHHC H-038-2]
- **25 October 1944, 0623** - Yamato's radar picks up US ASW patrol aircraft. Center Force is still in night search disposition and has not finished shifting to daytime AA formation. [NHHC Samar page]
- **25 October 1944, 0637** - Fanshaw Bay intercepts a Japanese transmission. Surface radar contact and anti-aircraft bursts follow. [NHHC "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency"]
- **25 October 1944, 0650** - Japanese lookouts sight what they take to be a large US task force of fleet carriers and cruisers on the eastern horizon and believe it is part of Third Fleet. It is Taffy 3. Kurita orders a pursuit formation, which, because the force is mid-shift between dispositions, scatters his ships and commits them piecemeal. Yamato opens fire. [NHHC Samar page]
- **25 October 1944, 0716 and 0742** - Rear Admiral Clifton A. F. Sprague orders two torpedo attacks by his screen. [NHHC Samar page]
- **25 October 1944, about 0800** - Kinkaid begins asking Halsey for TF 34. Halsey does not receive the messages until roughly 1000, by which time Kurita has already turned around. [NHHC H-038-2]
- **25 October 1944, about 1000** - Nimitz's dispatch reaches Halsey with the padding phrase still attached. [NHHC H-038-2]
- **25 October 1944, about 0900 to 0925** - Kurita breaks off and orders a general recall. Sprague's own words: "At 0925 my mind was occupied with dodging torpedoes when near the bridge I heard one of the signalmen yell 'They're getting away!'" [DANFS, Gambier Bay; NHHC Samar page]

## Key figures

- **Rear Admiral Clifton A. F. "Ziggy" Sprague**, Commander Task Unit 77.4.3 (Taffy 3), flag in Fanshaw Bay - Ordered smoke, launched everything that would fly regardless of armament, and threw his screen at battleships. [NHHC Samar page]
- **Rear Admiral Ralph A. Ofstie**, Commander Carrier Division 26, in Kitkun Bay - Second flag officer inside Taffy 3, with Kitkun Bay and Gambier Bay. [NHHC Samar page; navweaps order of battle]
- **Commander Ernest E. Evans**, USS Johnston (DD-557) - At his commissioning he told his crew he would "never run from a fight." On his own initiative he began a solo daylight torpedo attack. Posthumous Medal of Honor, the first Native American in the US Navy to receive it. [NHHC H-036; DANFS, Johnston I]
- **Lieutenant Commander Robert W. Copeland, USNR**, USS Samuel B. Roberts (DE-413) - Told his crew over the 1MC they were going into "a fight against overwhelming odds from which survival could not be expected." Navy Cross. His after-action report gave the phrase "no higher honor." [NHHC H-036; DANFS, Samuel B. Roberts I]
- **Vice Admiral Takeo Kurita**, Commander First Diversion Attack Force ("Center Force") - Lost his flagship on day one and had to be pulled out of the water. Ordered a "general attack" that dissolved his own command and control. [NHHC H-036; NHHC H-038-2]
- **Admiral William F. "Bull" Halsey**, Commander Third Fleet, in New Jersey (BB-62) - Took the bait and went north. Later said, "My real mistake was in turning around." [NHHC "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency"]
- **Vice Admiral Thomas C. Kinkaid**, Commander Seventh Fleet, in Wasatch (AGC-9) - Assumed TF 34 was covering the strait. Reported to MacArthur, not Nimitz. [NHHC H-038-2]
- **Rear Admiral Jesse B. Oldendorf**, Commander TG 77.2 - Crossed the T at Surigao Strait, which is exactly what the Japanese plan needed him to be doing. [NHHC H-038-1]
- **Vice Admiral Jisaburo Ozawa**, Commander Northern Force (decoy) - Senior Japanese commander present. Told postwar interrogators he did "not have much confidence in being a lure, but there was no other way than to try." [NHHC "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency," citing USSBS Interrogation Nav. No. 55]
- **Rear Admiral Felix B. Stump**, Commander Taffy 2 (TU 77.4.2) - Rearmed his Avengers with torpedoes and 500-pound bombs early and launched three strikes into Kurita's force. Routinely left out of the popular story. [NHHC Samar page; NHHC H-038-2]
- **Rear Admiral Robert B. "Mick" Carney**, Third Fleet chief of staff - Talked Halsey down after the dispatch, and earlier declined to wake him when his own intelligence officer wanted to warn him. [NHHC H-038-2]

**Yamato:**
- Displacement 65,000 tons, 72,000 tons fully loaded, the largest battleship ever built. [NHHC H-044-3, RADM Samuel J. Cox]
- Main battery: nine 18.1-inch guns in three triple turrets. Officially disguised by the Japanese as "40 cm/45 Type 94 (15.7 inch)" to hide the real caliber, which stayed secret until after the war. [NHHC H-044-3; navweaps 46 cm/45 Type 94, citing US Naval Technical Mission to Japan and NPG Report No. 289]

## Absurd / comedic beats (all real)

Every item below is sourced. None is invented.

1. **Kurita lost his own flagship two days before the battle and had to swim.** He was, unusually for a Japanese admiral, one of the first off the ship, and was picked out of the water by a destroyer. He fought the biggest moment of his career from a ship he had been on for less than 48 hours. [NHHC H-038-2]
2. **US pilots spent an entire day on 24 October beating the same battleship to death.** NHHC's own words: "the U.S. planes kept attacking the same ship over and over again (the super-battleship Musashi)." Musashi took roughly 19 torpedoes and 17 bombs while other ships went comparatively untouched. [NHHC H-038-1; NMUSN Musashi page]
3. **Ozawa spent days trying to get caught and the Americans kept not noticing.** He deliberately broke radio silence repeatedly, and US radio intelligence failed to intercept it. He finally had to launch nearly all of his aircraft just to draw attention to himself. [NHHC H-038-2]
4. **Kurita's "general attack" order broke his own fleet.** He gave it while his force was still mid-shift from night search disposition to daytime AA formation, which, per NHHC, "inadvertently led to confusion... committing the Japanese ships in a piecemeal fashion and dispersing their overwhelming firepower." The most powerful surface force left in the Japanese navy raced in "pell-mell in an uncoordinated fashion." [NHHC Samar page; NHHC H-036]
5. **The Japanese thought they had found Third Fleet.** They identified Taffy 3 as fleet carriers and cruisers. They kept believing it. Johnston laid down such heavy fire that the cruisers Haguro and Tone reported her as "a heavy cruiser." [NHHC Samar page]
6. **Kurita thought the jeep carriers were doing 30 knots.** They were doing about 18. He concluded he could never catch them and had lost them in the smoke. They were 7 nautical miles away. [NHHC "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency"]
7. **The armor-piercing shells worked too well.** Japanese AP rounds were designed to punch through battleship armor before detonating. Against unarmored escort carriers and destroyer escorts they "passed clean through the thin-skinned destroyer escorts" without arming. NHHC: "the full effect of their armor-piercing shells was wasted on the unarmored U.S. vessels." [NHHC H-036; NHHC Samar page]
8. **The destroyers were loaded with the wrong ammunition.** Mostly VT-fuzed anti-aircraft shells, because that was the threat they had actually been expecting. Result per NHHC: "carnage amongst Japanese topside anti-aircraft gunners, but did little to slow the Japanese heavy cruisers." [NHHC H-036]
9. **Aircraft attacked with what they had, which was sometimes nothing.** Some launched with depth charges meant for submarines. Some launched with insufficient fuel to get back anywhere. "A number of the aircraft had no weapons at all, yet repeatedly attacked anyway, to draw fire away from those that did." Gambier Bay's air group commander, Lt. Cmdr. Huxtable, flew an Avenger with no bombs and no torpedo and made repeated dummy bombing runs on a column of heavy cruisers. [NHHC H-036; DANFS, Gambier Bay]
10. **The escort carriers shot back with their one stern gun and actually hit something.** Kalinin Bay and White Plains scored hits on heavy cruisers. A chance White Plains hit on Chokai did enough damage that she fell out of formation and was later finished off by Kitkun Bay's aircraft. [NHHC Samar page]
11. **Johnston and Heermann nearly rammed each other in the middle of it.** Johnston's bow missed Heermann's "by about three inches," and per Heermann's captain, "a very loud spontaneous cheer arose from all hands on the topside of both vessels." [DANFS, Johnston I, quoting Cmdr. Hathaway]
12. **Both sides fired on their own ships.** Hoel was hit early by anti-aircraft shells from Johnston. Multiple near-collisions with friendly ships occurred on both sides. [NHHC H-036]
13. **A pilot shot a torpedo out of the water.** Lt. (j.g.) L. E. Waldrop spotted a full salvo headed for Kalinin Bay, dove astern of the torpedo formation, and exploded one with strafing fire. [NHHC "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency"]
14. **Kinkaid's desperate plain-language plea reached Kurita before it reached Halsey.** "My situation critical. Fast battleships and support by airstrikes may be able to keep enemy from destroying CVEs and entering Leyte." Japanese radio intelligence copied it. Halsey did not see it for roughly two hours. [NHHC H-038-2]
15. **And Kurita concluded it was a trap.** He assumed the Americans were transmitting in the clear as a ruse: "very, very unusual to intercept a message from the United States fleet and I thought perhaps they thought we could not understand English." [NHHC "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency"]
16. **Kurita turned away to go fight a task force that did not exist.** His stated reason included steaming north to engage an enemy force reported "113 miles bearing 5 degrees of Suluan Light at 0945." There was no such force. Halsey was over 300 miles away. [NHHC "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency"]
17. **His own second-in-command thought it was gutless.** Vice Admiral Matome Ugaki wrote in his diary: "I felt irritated on the same bridge seeing that they lack fighting spirit and promptitude." [NHHC "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency"]
18. **The Kinkaid-Halsey communications channel ran through MacArthur.** MacArthur forbade Kinkaid from communicating directly with Nimitz or Halsey. Everything had to route through a radio station at Manus, which could not handle the traffic. Kinkaid's direct question about whether TF 34 was guarding the strait sat there for over two hours. [NHHC H-038-2; NHHC "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency"]
19. **Kinkaid had to be prompted to ask the question at all.** His chief of staff, Captain Richard Cruzen, pointed out late that night: "We've never asked Halsey directly if Task Force 34 is guarding the San Bernardino Strait." [NHHC "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency"]
20. **A Seventh Fleet PBY flew through San Bernardino Strait that night just to be safe and missed the entire Japanese fleet by timing.** [NHHC H-038-2]
21. **Nobody woke Halsey.** His intelligence officer, Captain Michael Cheek, wanted to warn him. Carney would not do it. Vice Admiral Lee and Rear Admiral Bogan were brushed off. Arleigh Burke drafted a dispatch and Mitscher refused to send it: "Admiral Halsey is in command. He has all the information." Cheek's line about the Japanese: "They're coming through, I know, I've played poker with them." [NHHC H-038-2; NHHC "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency"]
22. **"Bull's Run."** The futile sprint north and then south became known by that name, in hindsight. [NHHC H-038-1]
23. **Halsey stomped on his own hat.** On reading the dispatch he "flew into a blinding rage, throwing his cover on the deck and stomping on it," until Carney got him under control. [NHHC H-038-2]
24. **Even if TF 34 had been formed when the Independence scouts reported, it could not have reached the strait in time.** Cox calls this "one fact missed by most histories of the battle." [NHHC H-038-2]
25. **St. Lo's crew called it.** Two weeks before, on being told the ship's name was changing from Midway to St. Lo, a crewman reportedly said, "You don't change the name of a Navy ship. We'll be at the bottom of the ocean in two weeks." [NHHC "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency"]

## Human-cost beats (play straight)

These are deaths and suffering. No jokes anywhere near them.

- **1,785 American crewmen went into the water from the four ships lost at Samar. 635 of them died, a significant number because the rescue effort was, in the words of the US Navy's own official historian-director, "botched and disgracefully delayed."** [NHHC H-036]
- **Survivors were in the water for over 50 hours.** Johnston's 141 survivors were pulled out on 27 October after clinging to life rafts "for over 50 hours, battling barracudas, sharks, and stingrays." Two of them had floated the entire time in nothing but a life jacket. [DANFS, Johnston I]
- **Three friendly aircraft flew over Johnston's survivors within two hours of the sinking.** Lt. Hagen, her gunnery officer: "We were only fifty miles east of Samar and we figured we'd be picked up in a few hours with so many ships around. A lot of men would be alive today if rescue had come sooner." [DANFS, Johnston I, quoting Hagen in The Saturday Evening Post]
- **Commander Ernest Evans was last seen alive in the water on the night of 25 October, wounded, in a life jacket.** He is thought to have made it off the ship and then drifted away from the rafts. Two other officers with him were never seen again either. Copeland's last sight of him was Evans bloody and bare-chested, waving from Johnston's fantail as she made her final charge. [DANFS, Johnston I; NHHC "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency"]
- **Johnston's executive officer, Lt. Elton B. "Silver" Stirling, survived the sinking, got to a raft, and drifted away from it in the night of 26 October from sheer exhaustion.** [DANFS, Johnston I]
- **Johnston's medical officer, Lt. (j.g.) Robert Browne, aged 28, stayed aboard after the abandon-ship order handing out life jackets to wounded men who had none.** He was last seen in the wardroom tending the wounded. Posthumous Navy Cross. [DANFS, Johnston I]
- **Machinist Marley O. Polk, aboard less than a month, volunteered to go into a flooded compartment open to the sea to close a valve.** He was killed in the act of closing it. Posthumous Navy Cross. [DANFS, Johnston I]
- **Gunner's Mate 3rd Class Paul H. Carr, Samuel B. Roberts, kept firing the ship's last working 5-inch gun until a breech explosion destroyed it.** Mortally wounded, he repeatedly tried to load one final round while others tried to help him. Posthumous Silver Star. [NHHC "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency"]
- **The water itself was the enemy.** DANFS: men were covered in thick black oil that burned their eyes, swallowed it and were sick, drank seawater and suffered "stomach pains, psychosis, and hallucinations." "Sharks added to the men's misery as they mercilessly savaged some of the sailors in the water." [DANFS, Gambier Bay]
- **On Gambier Bay herself: "The flames horribly burned some men, while machinery tore loose and crushed other men, and others succumbed to exhaustion or shock."** She capsized at 0907 and sank at 0911. [DANFS, Gambier Bay]
- **Hoel went down with all engineering spaces flooded and her No. 1 magazine on fire, her forward turret still firing as she went under.** [NHHC Samar page; NHHC H-036]
- **St. Lo sank in under 30 minutes, taking 141 men, the first major warship sunk by a kamikaze.** [NHHC Samar page; NHHC "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency"]
- **The Japanese side too.** Atago took 360 men down in 18 minutes. Maya capsized in seven minutes with 336. Most of Chokai's survivors were rescued by the destroyer Fujinami, which was sunk with all hands two days later, killing every one of them. Chikuma's survivors were taken aboard the destroyer Nowaki, which was sunk that same night with her entire crew and all of them, roughly 1,400 men. [NHHC H-038-2]
- **Musashi took 1,023 of her 2,399 crew.** [NMUSN Musashi page]
- **Cox's summing-up, worth quoting straight:** the Battle off Samar "actually cost more American lives than the disastrous loss at the Battle of Savo Island in August 1942. Victory has a high price." [NHHC H-036]

## Myth vs record (corrections)

**1. The "world wonders" dispatch**
- Popular telling: Nimitz sent Halsey a sarcastic message reading "WHERE IS TASK FORCE 34, THE WORLD WONDERS."
- Actual: The complete transmitted plaintext was **"TURKEY TROTS TO WATER GG FROM CINCPAC ACTION COM THIRD FLEET INFO COMINCH CTF SEVENTY-SEVEN X WHERE IS RPT WHERE IS TASK FORCE THIRTY FOUR RR THE WORLD WONDERS."** Nimitz's actual question was only "Where is, repeat, where is Task Force Thirty Four?" "TURKEY TROTS TO WATER" and "THE WORLD WONDERS" were security padding added by an enciphering clerk at Pearl Harbor to defeat Japanese known-plaintext cryptanalysis. The double letters GG and RR are the markers that bracket the padding, and standing procedure required padding to be nonsensical and required the receiving station to strip it. Of every ship and station that received the message, only the decoder aboard Halsey's flagship New Jersey failed to remove it. Nimitz never wrote the insult and did not know it had been read as one. [NHHC H-038-2, RADM Samuel J. Cox; David Kahn, *The Codebreakers*, Scribner 1996, p. 609; Richard B. Frank, "'The World Wonders,'" USNI *Naval History*, October 2019]
- The clerk's own explanation, quoted by Kahn: the phrase was "just something that popped into my head." [Kahn, p. 609]
- On the Tennyson theory: 25 October 1944 was the 90th anniversary of the Charge of the Light Brigade, and Tennyson's poem twice contains "All the world wonder'd." NHHC states only that the padding "may have been derived" from it. **Present this as speculation, not fact.** [NHHC H-038-2]

**2. "Halsey turned around because Nimitz shamed him"**
- Popular telling: the insult stung Halsey into reversing course and saving the day.
- Actual: Halsey knew it was already far too late for his battleships to affect Samar. He had received Ultra-derived warning earlier that morning that a Japanese force was east of Samar and had chosen not to react for exactly that reason. He turned south at about 1100 "only with the greatest of reluctance," and TG 34.5 arrived after Kurita had already re-entered the strait. Nothing Halsey did affected the outcome of the Battle off Samar. [NHHC H-038-2]

**3. "Halsey never told anyone about Task Force 34"**
- Popular telling: pure negligence.
- Actual: it was a wording failure. His message stated TF 34 "will be formed," which he meant as a contingency. Kinkaid, Nimitz and the White House Map Room all read it as an accomplished fact. Halsey did send a clarification at 1710 that TF 34 would be formed "when directed," but sent it by voice radio, which Seventh Fleet did not hear. [NHHC H-038-2]

**4. "Escort carriers were converted merchant ships"**
- Popular telling: Taffy 3's carriers were merchant hulls with a flight deck bolted on.
- Actual: partly true of the type, false of these ships. The earliest CVEs were converted C-3 merchant hulls, and the Sangamon class in Taffy 1 were converted Cimarron-class fleet oilers. But all six of Taffy 3's carriers were Casablanca class, **Maritime Commission type S4-S2-BB3**, built as escort carriers by Kaiser at Vancouver, Washington. Gambier Bay was "laid down as an auxiliary aircraft carrier (ACV-73)," never a merchant ship. The correct line is that they were built fast and cheap to a Maritime Commission design, with merchant-ship construction standards and no armor, not that they were converted merchantmen. [DANFS, Gambier Bay; DANFS, Fanshaw Bay; DANFS, Kalinin Bay; DANFS, Kitkun Bay; DANFS, Liscome Bay]

**5. "Combustible, Vulnerable, Expendable"**
- Popular telling: often quoted with no source, sometimes dismissed as invented later.
- Actual: it is documented in official US Navy history. NHHC's DANFS entry for Liscome Bay states directly: "The hastily built warships, with the hull designation CVE, were often described by members of their cynical crews as 'combustible, vulnerable, expendable.' Their cynicism was not without merit, as events would bear out." It is a real crew backronym for the CVE hull code, alongside "jeep carrier" and "baby flattop." **What is NOT documented is a first user, a first date, or an origin story.** Do not attribute it to a named sailor. [NHHC DANFS, Liscome Bay; corroborated in William T. Y'Blood, *The Little Giants: U.S. Escort Carriers Against Japan*, Naval Institute Press, 1987]

**6. "Taffy 3 drove off Kurita by itself"**
- Popular telling: thirteen ships beat the Japanese fleet.
- Actual: NHHC repeatedly stresses that Taffy 2's aircraft, under Rear Admiral Felix Stump, did much of the killing. Stump had time to rearm Avengers with torpedoes and launched three strikes. Taffy 2 and Taffy 3 aircraft together account for most of the damage that sank Chokai, Chikuma and Suzuya. Cox: "Taffy 3 might not have survived if Kurita's ships had not been under air attack during the entire Samar engagement." NHHC's own summary of the causes lists "initiative, aggressiveness, and outright heroism demonstrated by Taffy 3, combined with determined U.S. naval air attacks, limited Japanese situational awareness, and pure dumb luck of the Americans." [NHHC Samar page; NHHC H-036; NHHC H-038-2]

**7. "Kurita retreated for no reason at all"**
- Popular telling: "inexplicable."
- Actual: there is a long list of documented reasons, and Cox flags one that is usually underrated: "just how badly his ships had been hurt." By the time he re-entered the strait, three of his six heavy cruisers were sunk and a fourth was crippled, and almost every ship in the force had shell, bomb or strafing damage. His last scout aircraft was shot down shortly after 0900, leaving him blind. Add fuel worry, a phantom task force report, an intercepted plaintext message he read as a trap, and two days without sleep after being sunk out from under himself. It was a bad decision, not a random one. [NHHC H-036; NHHC "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency"]

**8. "Nobody in the US Navy saw it coming"**
- Actual: several people did and were overruled or ignored. Burke, Mitscher's chief of staff, was convinced Ozawa was a decoy. Halsey's intelligence officer Cheek and his analyst Harris Cox reached the same conclusion. Lee and Bogan both raised concerns. The failure was one of command culture, not of intelligence. [NHHC H-038-2]

**9. "Yamato was a monster whose guns dominated the battle"**
- Actual: Samar was the only occasion in Yamato's entire career on which she fired her main battery at enemy ships. Early in the action, a torpedo attack by Heermann bluffed Kurita's flagship into turning away, taking her out of the fight for a period while she opened the range. NHHC (Cox) describes it as "agonizing (for the Japanese) minutes to get back into the fight." The widely repeated figure of "about ten minutes" comes from secondary accounts descending from Morison, not from a Tier 1 record I could verify. Use "for several minutes" unless the writer wants to attribute the ten-minute figure to Morison explicitly. [navweaps; NHHC H-036]

**10. "Yamato's guns could shoot 22 miles"**
- Actual: 42,030 m at maximum turret elevation, which is 26.1 statute miles or 22.7 nautical miles. NHHC's "just over 22 miles" is nautical. Both are correct, and quoting "22 miles" without the qualifier understates it by about 4 miles. [NHHC H-044-3; navweaps]

## Gaps

- **NOT FOUND:** the name of the enciphering officer at Pearl Harbor who wrote "THE WORLD WONDERS," verified in a Tier 1 source. Wikipedia names an "Ensign J. D. Kaster" without a solid citation. Richard B. Frank's October 2019 USNI *Naval History* article is reported to identify him as a sailor promoted from the enlisted ranks for valor who had never been formally trained in drafting high-level coded communications, but the article itself is behind a Cloudflare wall I could not read directly. **Do not name him on screen.**
- **NOT FOUND:** the exact clock time Yamato fired her first salvo at Taffy 3. NHHC states Kurita ordered engagement "shortly after" the 0650 sighting. Times of 0658 and 0659 circulate in Tier 2 sources without a primary anchor.
- **NOT FOUND:** how many 18.1-inch rounds Yamato fired at Samar.
- **CONTESTED, not resolved:** which ship put the fatal 18.1-inch hits into Johnston. DANFS states "three 18.1-inch shells from Yamato." For Gambier Bay, DANFS explicitly notes the dispute, with US survivors crediting Chikuma's 8-inch fire and Japanese accounts debating between Yamato and Kongo. Treat the "Yamato hit her" line as claimed, not settled.
- **NOT FOUND:** a Tier 1 published armor scheme for the Casablanca class. NHHC repeatedly calls them "unarmored," which is the safe on-screen phrasing. Do not quote an inch figure.
- **NOT FOUND:** total personnel embarked across all thirteen Taffy 3 ships on the morning of 25 October, from a single authoritative source. It can only be estimated by summing DANFS complements, which are design figures, not muster figures.
- **CONFLICT, unresolved:** Taffy 3's total killed and missing. Cox (NHHC) gives 1,118 ships' crew plus 43 aviators killed across Taffy 3 and Taffy 1 including the kamikaze attacks. A figure of 1,583 for Taffy 3 alone circulates in Tier 2 sources. The scopes are not the same and the numbers cannot be reconciled from what I found. Use Cox and state the scope explicitly.
- **Known errors in otherwise Tier 1 sources, flagged so the writer does not copy them:** NHHC's "Ships of TG 77.4.3" page and several H-grams spell Heermann as "Heerman." H-Gram 036 and H-038-1 both give Raymond's hull number as DE-431; the correct number is DE-341 per DANFS and the main Samar page. H-038-1 lists Santee as "CV-29"; she was CVE-29. DANFS for Kitkun Bay carries a header typo of "CVE-70"; she was CVE-71. The National WWII Museum's Leyte Gulf article places the Darter and Dace submarine attack "in the Sibuyan Sea"; it happened in Palawan Passage.
