# Research Report: Last Stand of Taffy 3 (BATTLE/AFTERMATH HALF)

## One-paragraph summary

At dawn on 25 October 1944, Task Unit 77.4.3 ("Taffy 3"), six thin-skinned escort carriers, three destroyers and four destroyer escorts under Rear Adm. Clifton A. F. "Ziggy" Sprague, was doing routine close air support off Samar when the pagoda masts of Vice Adm. Takeo Kurita's Center Force, four battleships including Yamato, six heavy cruisers, two light cruisers and eleven destroyers, came over the horizon roughly 20 miles away. Sprague turned east into the wind, launched every plane that could fly, made smoke, and ran for a rain squall while ordering his destroyers and destroyer escorts to charge a battle line that outweighed them by orders of magnitude. Over roughly two and a half hours, Cdr. Ernest E. Evans in Johnston attacked before he was ordered to and blew the bow off the heavy cruiser Kumano; Hoel absorbed what her captain estimated as more than forty direct hits before sinking; Heermann's torpedoes missed Haruna but frightened Yamato into running ten miles north, taking the Japanese fleet commander out of his own battle; and Lt. Cdr. Robert W. Copeland's destroyer escort Samuel B. Roberts emptied 608 of her 650 5-inch shells into a heavy cruiser before three 14-inch shells from Kongo tore her open. Kurita, who believed he was fighting fleet carriers making 30 knots, who was hearing American plain-language distress calls he assumed were a trap, and whose armor-piercing shells were punching clean through unarmored American hulls without exploding, suspended the pursuit around 0910 and eventually withdrew. He sank one escort carrier, two destroyers and one destroyer escort. He lost three heavy cruisers. A separate kamikaze strike sank St. Lo at about 1051. Roughly 1,785 Americans went into the water, and because the rescue was botched, many of them stayed there for over two days.

## Verified timeline

All times US local, zone Item (+9), as written in American action reports ("0653I/25"). Japanese-record times on combinedfleet TROM pages run approximately one hour earlier; conversions are flagged.

- **0637, 25 Oct 1944** - Ens. William C. Brooks Jr., USNR, flying a TBM-1C of VC-65 from St. Lo (CVE-63) on antisubmarine patrol, sights the pagoda masts of Kurita's force. He first assumes they are Allied reinforcements, follows procedure and radios a sighting report. His superiors demand confirmation, so he closes the range to positively identify them, then attacks. [NHHC DANFS, Gambier Bay (CVE-73)]
- **0645** - Heermann's radar operators report jamming. [NHHC DANFS, Heermann (DD-532)]
- **~0650** - Sprague orders Taffy 3 to turn east to course 090, launch all aircraft regardless of readiness state, and make smoke. [NHHC DANFS Gambier Bay; NHHC, "The Battle off Samar"]
- **0653I/25** - Time at which Seventh Fleet logs the antisubmarine-patrol contact report. [CTF 77 (Kinkaid) Action Report]
- **0655** - Samuel B. Roberts goes to general quarters. 0658, lookouts report heavy-caliber splashes with green and purple dye markings falling between Samuel B. Roberts and Johnston. [NHHC DANFS, Samuel B. Roberts (DE-413)]
- **~0700** - Yamato opens fire. Kurita himself corrected his interrogators on this: "The engagement off SAMAR opened about 0700, not at 0650 as suggested yesterday." [USSBS Interrogation Nav No. 9]
- **0700** - Taffy 3's escorts begin laying black funnel smoke. [NHHC DANFS Samuel B. Roberts]
- **0703 (approx.)** - Kurita orders a "general attack," dissolving his formation into an every-division-for-itself pursuit. [NHHC H-Gram 084; NHHC, "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency." Original Japanese wording NOT FOUND at Tier 1.]
- **0710** - Johnston opens fire, unordered, and begins her run. [NHHC DANFS, Johnston (DD-557)]
- **0716** - Samuel B. Roberts enters a rain squall, concealed for the next 11 minutes. [NHHC DANFS Samuel B. Roberts]
- **0724 or 0727** - Johnston's torpedo strikes Kumano, tearing off a section of her bow. [combinedfleet.com Kumano TROM gives 0724; Anthony Tully, "Ship of Nine Lives," gives 0727. Kurita confirmed a destroyer torpedo hit in USSBS Nav No. 9. **Conflict, do not average.**]
- **~0735** - Sprague orders the first torpedo attack, Johnston, Hoel and Heermann. **Time conflict:** NHHC's Samar essay says 0716 for the first attack order and 0742 for the second; DANFS Samuel B. Roberts says 0735 for the first and 0800 for the second. [NHHC, "The Battle off Samar"; NHHC DANFS Samuel B. Roberts]
- **0740** - Taffy 1 (TU 77.4.1) is struck by the first successful organized kamikaze attack of the day. Santee (CVE-29) becomes the first US carrier hit by a suicide plane. [NHHC, "The Battle off Samar"; CTF 77 Action Report]
- **~0750** - Kalinin Bay (CVE-68), the "Tail-end-Charlie" of the formation, takes the first of 15 direct hits, from Haruna at 16,998 yards. [NHHC DANFS, Kalinin Bay]
- **~0754** (combinedfleet TROM 0654, Japanese base) - Heermann fires three torpedoes at Haruna. They miss Haruna and run toward Yamato, which turns away to port and steams north for ten miles until they run out of fuel. combinedfleet: this "puts YAMATO and the Force's commander, Vice Admiral Kurita out of the battle." [combinedfleet.com Yamato TROM and Kongo TROM; NHHC H-Gram 036]
- **0805** - Copeland shifts Samuel B. Roberts' fire to the heavy cruiser Chikuma. [NHHC DANFS Samuel B. Roberts]
- **0808** - Noshiro hits White Plains (CVE-66) with several 6-inch shells. [combinedfleet.com Noshiro TROM]
- **0818 to 0832** - Tone fires 90 main-battery rounds at a target she logs as a "RANGER class" carrier. It is Gambier Bay. [combinedfleet.com Tone TROM]
- **~0825** - Haguro is hit by a 100-pound GP bomb that knocks out her No. 2 turret, killing 30 gunners. [combinedfleet.com Haguro TROM]
- **0830** - Hoel is dead in the water. [NHHC, "The Battle off Samar"]
- **0838** - Noshiro is hit on the starboard side by a 5-inch shell from an American destroyer, killing one sailor and injuring three. The single clearest documented gunfire hit by a Taffy 3 escort on a Japanese warship. Source does not name the destroyer. [combinedfleet.com Noshiro TROM]
- **0840** - Gambier Bay is dead in the water. [NHHC, "The Battle off Samar"]
- **0845** - Heermann duels Tone at close range. Also at 0845 the light cruiser Yahagi and several destroyers launch a torpedo attack on Taffy 3, repulsed by Johnston's fire and by strafing. [NHHC DANFS Heermann; NHHC, "The Battle off Samar"]
- **0851** - Chokai takes six 5-inch hits to her port side amidships. [combinedfleet.com Chokai TROM]
- **0853** - Chikuma is hit by a Mk. 13 aerial torpedo from four Taffy 2 Avengers, disabling her port screw and rudder. Dead in the water two minutes later. [combinedfleet.com Chikuma TROM]
- **0855** - Hoel sinks. [NHHC DANFS, Hoel (DD-533)]
- **0859** - A secondary explosion, probably of Chokai's own deck-mounted torpedoes, knocks out her engines and rudder. White Plains' own report notes a secondary explosion at 0859 "possibly as some of her torpedoes cooked off." [combinedfleet.com Chokai TROM; NHHC DANFS, White Plains]
- **~0900** - A second salvo of three 8-inch shells guts Samuel B. Roberts. Several Japanese ships have by now switched from armor-piercing to high-explosive. Only Gun Mount 52, captained by GM3c Paul Henry Carr, remains in action. [NHHC DANFS Samuel B. Roberts]
- **0907 capsized, 0911 sank** - Gambier Bay (CVE-73), the only US aircraft carrier of the war sunk by enemy surface gunfire. [NHHC DANFS Gambier Bay]
- **~0910** - Kurita suspends the pursuit. Rear Adm. Tomiji Koyanagi, Kurita's chief of staff: "Admiral Kurita accordingly suspended the pursuit at 0910." [Koyanagi, "With Kurita in the Battle for Leyte Gulf," USNI *Proceedings*, Feb 1953]
- **0910** - Copeland orders Samuel B. Roberts abandoned. [NHHC DANFS Samuel B. Roberts]
- **0920** - Johnston, steering gone, is being conned from the fantail by voice through the hatch to the steering engine room. [NHHC DANFS Johnston]
- **0945** - The phantom sighting report reaches Kurita: a "U.S. Carrier Striking Task Force" bearing 5 degrees from Suluan Light. Koyanagi's note on it: "(This report was later found groundless.)" [Koyanagi, *Proceedings*, Feb 1953; NHHC, "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency"]
- **0950** - Johnston is abandoned. [NHHC, "The Battle off Samar"]
- **~1000** - Halsey aboard New Jersey receives the CINCPAC dispatch with the unstripped padding "THE WORLD WONDERS," arriving almost simultaneously with Kinkaid's plain-language "WHERE IS LEE. SEND LEE." [Halsey, *Admiral Halsey's Story*, 1947; Hamilton, *Proceedings*, Oct 1960; NHHC, "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency"]
- **1007** - Samuel B. Roberts sinks. [NHHC DANFS Samuel B. Roberts]
- **1010** - Johnston sinks. [NHHC DANFS Johnston]
- **~1050 to 1051** - A kamikaze crashes St. Lo (CVE-63). She sinks after internal explosions. [CTF 77 Action Report logs 1050I; NHHC DANFS, Midway II / St. Lo]
- **1050** - A near miss detonates Suzuya's No. 1 torpedo mount, loaded with Type 93s. Her remaining torpedoes and ammunition explode around noon. [combinedfleet.com Suzuya TROM; Kurita, USSBS Nav No. 9]
- **1115** - Halsey's TF 34 and TG 38.2 reverse course to due south at 18 N, 126 E. Ozawa's crippled carriers were 42 miles away. [CTF 34 (Lee) Action Report]
- **1249** - Tone is hit aft by a dive bomber during the retirement, disabling her steering. [combinedfleet.com Tone TROM]
- **1255I/25** - Kinkaid's Seventh Fleet action report logs the enemy's final turn-away, course 015. [CTF 77 Action Report]
- **1322** - Suzuya sinks. **~1430** - Chikuma capsizes and sinks. [combinedfleet.com TROMs]
- **1701** - TG 34.5 under Rear Adm. Oscar C. Badger II departs for San Bernardino Strait, course 195, speed 24. [CTF 34 Action Report]
- **2148** - Chokai is scuttled by torpedoes from the destroyer Fujinami. [combinedfleet.com Chokai TROM]
- **~2200** - Kurita re-enters San Bernardino Strait westbound, roughly two hours ahead of Badger. [Halsey, *Admiral Halsey's Story*]
- **0149, 26 Oct** - The destroyer Nowaki, dead in the water while standing by Chikuma, is sunk by TG 34.5 with all hands, including Chikuma's survivors. [combinedfleet.com Chikuma TROM; NHHC H-Gram 038-2]
- **27 Oct** - Survivors of Johnston, Hoel, Samuel B. Roberts and Gambier Bay are finally pulled from the water, after more than 50 hours for some. [NHHC DANFS Johnston; NHHC DANFS Gambier Bay]

## Key figures

- **Rear Adm. Clifton A. F. "Ziggy" Sprague, CTU 77.4.3** - Turned east into the wind, launched everything airborne regardless of arming state, made smoke, used a rain squall for concealment, reversed course inside it to break Kurita's encirclement, and ordered the destroyers and then the destroyer escorts to make torpedo attacks. From his action report: "It did not appear any of our ships could survive another five minutes. . . . The task unit was surrounded by the ultimate of desperate circumstances." [NHHC, "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency," footnoted to his action report in NARA RG 38]
- **Cdr. Ernest E. Evans, USS Johnston (DD-557)** - Part Cherokee, born in Pawnee, Oklahoma, of Cherokee and Muscogee (Creek) descent. Attacked before Sprague ordered it. Fired nearly 200 rounds of 5-inch, then a full spread of ten torpedoes, blowing the bow off Kumano. Fought on after losing an engine room, three 5-inch mounts, his steering and two fingers, conning from the fantail. Last seen by Copeland bloody and bare-chested, waving from the fantail. He got off the ship but drifted away from the rafts with two junior officers and was never recovered. Posthumous Medal of Honor. [NHHC DANFS Johnston; NHHC H-Gram 084; NHHC, "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency"]
- **Cdr. Leon S. Kintberger, USS Hoel (DD-533)** - Led the first torpedo attack, engaging Kongo at 14,000 yards. Estimated in his action report that Hoel took more than 40 direct hits before going down at 0855. His torpedo officer, Lt. Cdr. William Sanders Jr., on being ordered to attack: "Line up and let's go." [NHHC DANFS Hoel]
- **Cdr. Amos T. Hathaway, USS Heermann (DD-532)** - Charged out of the smoke into three separate near-collisions with friendly ships. His action report on the American attack: "no column was ever really formed." Torpedoes at Haruna missed but chased Yamato out of the battle. Duelled Tone at close range. Navy Cross. Heermann was the only one of the three destroyers to survive. [NHHC DANFS Heermann]
- **Lt. Cdr. Robert W. Copeland, USNR, USS Samuel B. Roberts (DE-413)** - A reservist. Told his crew over the 1MC exactly what was coming. Waited for the senior destroyer-escort skippers to move, saw that none did, and led the DE attack himself. Fired 608 of the ship's 650 5-inch shells. Awarded the Navy Cross, presented at Norfolk on 16 July 1945. His action-report line gave the Navy its 244th birthday theme: "Men zealously manned their stations wherever they might be, and fought and worked with such calmness, courage and efficiency that no higher honor could be conceived than to command such a group of men." [NHHC DANFS Samuel B. Roberts; NHHC, "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency," footnoted to RG 38 p. 14]
- **GM3c Paul Henry Carr, USNR, Gun Mount 52, Samuel B. Roberts** - Of Checotah, Oklahoma. Kept the aft 5-inch gun firing by hand after the power rammer and the automatic gas ejection system failed, until a powder charge cooked off in the hot breech. Awarded the **Silver Star**, posthumously. See "Myth vs record" below. [NHHC DANFS, Carr (FFG-52); Military Times Hall of Valor]
- **MM2c Chalmer Goheen, Samuel B. Roberts** - The actual eyewitness to the Carr scene. Copeland was on the bridge and did not see it. [Hornfischer, *The Last Stand of the Tin Can Sailors*, Bantam 2004, ch. 41]
- **Ens. William C. Brooks Jr., USNR, VC-65 (from St. Lo)** - Made the sighting that started the battle, then bombed and strafed capital ships in a TBM. [NHHC DANFS Gambier Bay]
- **Lt. Cdr. Edward J. Huxtable, VC-10 (Gambier Bay)** - Flew repeated dummy attack runs at Japanese warships in an Avenger carrying no ordnance. [NHHC DANFS Gambier Bay]
- **Vice Adm. Takeo Kurita, IJN, Center Force** - Believed he was fighting fleet carriers doing 30 knots. Ordered a "general attack," lost tactical control, suspended pursuit around 0910, and turned north. See scope item 8 below.
- **Rear Adm. Tomiji Koyanagi, IJN, Kurita's chief of staff** - The best Japanese-side narrator, and his own harshest critic. "Looking back today, when all events have been made clear, we should have continued to Leyte Gulf." [Koyanagi, USNI *Proceedings*, Feb 1953]

## Numbers (high-risk, source each)

**Forces**
- Taffy 3: 6 escort carriers, 3 destroyers, 4 destroyer escorts. Escort carrier top speed 17 to 18 knots. [NHHC H-Gram 084 says 17 knots; NHHC "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency" and Morison say 18. **Both Tier 1, conflict noted.**]
- Kurita's Center Force at Samar: 4 battleships, 6 heavy cruisers, 2 light cruisers, 11 destroyers. [NHHC, "The Battle off Samar"]

**Ship-by-ship American losses**
- **Johnston (DD-557):** 327 aboard, 186 lost, 141 survivors. Breakdown: 50 killed in action, 45 died of wounds or exposure, 91 missing. [NHHC DANFS Johnston; NHHC, "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency"]
- **Hoel (DD-533):** 86 survived, 252 perished. [NHHC DANFS Hoel]
- **Samuel B. Roberts (DE-413):** 120 survived, 90 dead. Fired 608 of 650 5-inch shells. [NHHC DANFS Samuel B. Roberts]
- **Gambier Bay (CVE-73):** 706 survivors. [NHHC DANFS Gambier Bay]
- **St. Lo (CVE-63):** dead figure conflicts across sources at 141, "more than 140," and 143. **Do not state a single number without a caveat.** [NHHC DANFS Midway II / St. Lo]
- **Heermann (DD-532):** 4 killed, 17 wounded. [NHHC DANFS Heermann]
- **Kalinin Bay (CVE-68):** 15 direct hits, 60 casualties including 9 dead. Survived. [NHHC DANFS Kalinin Bay]

**Aggregates (use with the caveat below)**
- Roughly **1,785 men went into the water**. **635 died** there or of wounds. Total 25 October losses across Taffy 3: **1,118 ship's company killed plus 43 aviators, and 913 wounded.** [NHHC H-Gram 084, RADM Samuel J. Cox, Director of Naval History, 28 Oct 2024]
- **Arithmetic caveat, flag it:** the per-ship DANFS dead figures (186 + 252 + 90 + Gambier Bay's losses) do not reconcile cleanly with Cox's 635, and the per-ship survivor totals do not reconcile with the number rescued. Present Cox's aggregate as the best single Tier 1 figure and note that the ship-level records do not sum to it. **Do not average.**

**Japanese losses**
- Three heavy cruisers lost: **Chokai, Chikuma, Suzuya.** No Japanese destroyers lost on 25 October. [combinedfleet.com TROMs; NHHC, "The Battle off Samar"]
- **Suzuya:** 247 lost, 401 rescued by the destroyer Okinami. [combinedfleet.com Suzuya TROM]
- **Haguro:** 55 officers and men killed during the battle, 30 of them in the No. 2 turret. [combinedfleet.com Haguro TROM]
- **Kongo's expenditure:** 310 14-inch rounds (99 Type 3 incendiary AA, 211 Type 1), 347 6-inch, 2,128 5-inch, 50,230 25 mm. Roughly a third of her main-battery expenditure at ships was anti-aircraft ammunition. [combinedfleet.com Kongo TROM]
- **Tone's expenditure:** 408 main-battery rounds. [combinedfleet.com Tone TROM]
- **Total damage inflicted on Yamato by Taffy 3's guns:** one shell. It was a dud. [Kurita and Cdr. Otani, USSBS Interrogations Nav No. 9 and Nav No. 41]

**Awards**
- **1 Medal of Honor** (Evans), **22 Navy Crosses**, **2 Silver Stars**, **2 Bronze Stars with Combat "V"**, plus the Presidential Unit Citation and the Philippine Presidential Unit Citation. [NHHC, "TG 77.4.3 ('Taffy 3') Presidential Unit Citation and Other Awards"]
- **Caveat, flag it:** the "2 Silver Stars" tally conflicts with the DANFS ship histories, which name five Silver Star recipients on Heermann alone plus Carr on Samuel B. Roberts. The NHHC tally page is probably scoped narrower than it appears. **Do not cite the "2 Silver Stars" figure as a battle-wide total.**

## Absurd / comedic beats (all real)

1. **The Japanese shot at Taffy 3 with the wrong ammunition, and it saved them.** Kurita's ships were loaded with armor-piercing shells fused to punch through battleship armor. American escort carriers and destroyer escorts have essentially no armor, so the shells went in one side and out the other without arming. The CO of Kalinin Bay wrote it up in his own action report: "Two shells passed through the port side skin of the ship and out the starboard side without exploding. One shell apparently broke into two parts without exploding." He credited survival to "good luck, a Japanese error in using armor-piercing shells against ships without armor protection, and poor quality of Japanese shells." Kalinin Bay took 15 hits and lived. [Naval War College, "Battle Experience: Battle for Leyte Gulf," CO Kalinin Bay comments, HyperWar; NHHC DANFS Kalinin Bay]
2. **Sprague's action report thanks God by name.** He listed the causes of survival as the smoke screen, the torpedo counter-attack, the air attacks, timely maneuvers, "and the definite partiality of Almighty God." That is in an official US Navy document. [NHHC DANFS Kalinin Bay, quoting Sprague's action report]
3. **Japanese torpedoes swam harmlessly under the American ships** because they had been set shallow for big targets. Sailors on Samuel B. Roberts braced for impact and then watched Type 93 Long Lances go by underneath. [NHHC DANFS Samuel B. Roberts]
4. **Aircraft made attack runs with nothing to attack with.** Kinkaid's own Seventh Fleet action report: "the enemy was hit with every conceivable form of plane attack, including dummy torpedo runs by planes without torpedoes." Huxtable of VC-10 flew repeated dry runs in an unarmed Avenger. Others strafed battleships with .50-caliber machine guns and dropped depth charges on surface warships. [CTF 77 Action Report, HyperWar; NHHC DANFS Gambier Bay]
5. **Three tin cans nearly rammed each other on the way in.** Hathaway's action report on the destroyer charge: "no column was ever really formed." Heermann had three separate near-collisions with friendly ships, one of which DANFS Johnston records as passing within "three inches." Copeland's torpedo run on Chokai was aborted mid-solution because Heermann nearly hit him. [NHHC DANFS Heermann; NHHC DANFS Johnston; NHHC DANFS Samuel B. Roberts]
6. **A destroyer was reported as a heavy cruiser.** Johnston laid such heavy fire on Haguro and Tone that the Japanese logged her as a "heavy cruiser." [NHHC, "The Battle off Samar"]
7. **The Japanese could not agree on what they were shooting at, and said so out loud after the war.** Cdr. Otani Tonosuke, Kurita's operations officer, to USSBS: "We gave that question much consideration, but never fully made up our minds. We found ourselves perplexed by your carriers because they did not correspond to their photographs, and first we thought that they were regular carriers; but after the battle, we decided that they were auxiliary or converted carriers." Yamato's log misidentified Taffy 3 as "six fleet carriers, escorted by three cruisers and two destroyers." Tone's log records firing at an "INDEPENDENCE class carrier," then a "RANGER class" carrier, then two "cruisers." [USSBS Interrogation Nav No. 41; combinedfleet.com Yamato and Tone TROMs]
8. **Kurita's fleet believed it could not catch ships making 30 knots. The escort carriers were making 17 or 18, and one Japanese cruiser division was closing them at 33.** Koyanagi: "We could not close the range your speed was so high, so we gave up the chase." Meanwhile the Chikuma TROM: "CruDiv 7 steers SE in a single column. They close the escort carriers on the port quarter at 33 knots." When Kurita broke off believing he had lost them, they were seven nautical miles away. [USSBS Nav No. 35 and Nav No. 41; combinedfleet.com Chikuma TROM; NHHC, "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency," citing Morison]
9. **Three destroyers scared the largest battleship ever built out of its own battle.** Heermann's three torpedoes missed Haruna, ran on toward Yamato, and Yamato turned away and steamed north for ten miles until the torpedoes ran out of fuel. combinedfleet: it "puts YAMATO and the Force's commander, Vice Admiral Kurita out of the battle." Kurita spent a chunk of the engagement running the wrong direction. [combinedfleet.com Yamato TROM; NHHC H-Gram 036]
10. **Two of the three Japanese heavy cruisers lost were killed by their own torpedoes.** Suzuya was destroyed when a near miss detonated her own loaded Type 93 mount. Chokai was crippled by a secondary explosion "probably caused by CHOKAI's own torpedoes on deck." Kurita confirmed the Suzuya mechanism himself to USSBS. [combinedfleet.com Suzuya and Chokai TROMs; USSBS Nav No. 9]
11. **Chikuma's anti-aircraft gunners ran out of ammunition and started firing practice rounds** at attacking American planes. [combinedfleet.com Chikuma TROM]
12. **Kurita broke off partly because the Americans were begging for help in plain English, and he assumed it was a trick.** In his own words, it was "very, very unusual to intercept a message from the United States fleet and I thought perhaps they thought we could not understand English." [NHHC, "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency," citing Shelley Mydans to Adm. Kinkaid, 11 Sep 1947]
13. **The Japanese ended the day convinced they had sunk four fleet carriers, two or three cruisers and two or three destroyers.** They had sunk one escort carrier, two destroyers and one destroyer escort, and lost three heavy cruisers doing it. Otani's own summary of the confusion: "There was some confusion between the high gunnery control platform and the bridge. There may have been a repeat report which was understood as two carriers sunk." [USSBS Nav No. 41 and Nav No. 35]
14. **The Hoel's captain on Japanese anti-aircraft gunnery, in an official report:** "The Japanese anti-aircraft fire can only be described as pitiful." [Naval War College, "Battle Experience," CO Hoel comments, HyperWar]
15. **The damaged Kumano, limping away, was then attacked by three Japanese aircraft** who mistook her for an enemy. They missed. **Single-source caveat:** this appears only in the Hackett and Kingsepp TROM and is absent from Tully's own detailed Kumano article. Attribute explicitly if used. [combinedfleet.com Kumano TROM]
16. **The rescue delay had a paperwork component.** Kinkaid's approval for the rescue group took roughly two hours before TG 78.12 could sail. [NHHC DANFS Samuel B. Roberts]

## Human-cost beats (play straight)

- **Roughly 1,785 men went into the water. About 635 of them died there.** They were not found for over two days. Some of Johnston's survivors were in the sea for more than 50 hours. [NHHC H-Gram 084; NHHC DANFS Johnston]
- **DANFS records what was in the water with them, in the official history's own words:** they spent that time "battling barracudas, sharks, and stingrays." This is not folklore added later. It is in the ship's history. [NHHC DANFS Johnston]
- **The rescue failure was not bad luck.** RADM Samuel J. Cox, the Navy's own Director of Naval History, calls it "botched and disgracefully delayed." Part of the cause is documented in DANFS: the search effort spent time "searching an area too far to the east." [NHHC H-Gram 084; NHHC DANFS Hoel]
- **Cdr. Evans got off his ship and then drifted away from the life rafts with two junior officers.** He was never recovered. [NHHC, "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency"]
- **Hoel lost 252 of 338. Johnston lost 186 of 327.** Roughly three out of four men on Hoel and well over half the crew of Johnston did not come home. [NHHC DANFS Hoel; NHHC DANFS Johnston]
- **Samuel B. Roberts, the steam.** A 14-inch shell from Kongo ruptured the main steam line. DANFS: "All but two men…were instantly scalded to death in temperatures that soared to more than 800° or, half baked, begged for death as steam rose from their bodies." [NHHC DANFS Samuel B. Roberts]
- **Paul Henry Carr.** Torn open from neck to groin, he was found still holding the last shell, asking a shipmate to help him load it. Goheen took the shell away and laid him down. Goheen carried another man out, a sailor whose leg had been severed near the hip. When Goheen came back, Carr was on his feet again with the shell in his arms. He died on the deck five minutes later, at 21. [NHHC DANFS Samuel B. Roberts; Hornfischer, *Tin Can Sailors*, ch. 41]
- **The dog.** Sammy, Samuel B. Roberts' mascot, ran terrified around the decks through the whole action and was last seen leaping into the water. A survivor who had jumped without a life vest later found her vest floating nearby. It was too small for him. [NHHC DANFS Samuel B. Roberts]
- **The Japanese side.** Haguro's No. 2 turret killed 30 gunners in one bomb hit; the survivors flooded the magazine themselves to prevent the ship exploding. Suzuya lost 247. Chokai's survivors were taken aboard the destroyer Fujinami, which was later sunk with all hands. Chikuma's survivors were aboard the destroyer Nowaki when TG 34.5 sank her with all hands at 0149 on 26 October, roughly 1,400 men. [combinedfleet.com Haguro, Suzuya, Chokai and Chikuma TROMs; NHHC H-Gram 038-2]

## Myth vs record (corrections)

**1. Paul Henry Carr's medal**
- **Popular telling:** Carr received the Navy Cross, or should have received the Medal of Honor and a campaign is underway to upgrade it.
- **Actual:** He received the **Silver Star**, posthumously, on a citation signed by Secretary of the Navy James Forrestal. He is not a Medal of Honor recipient. **Genuine documentary wrinkle worth using:** a wartime Navy Department Office of Public Information release states "Upon the recommendation of his Commanding Officer, Carr was on March 1, 1945 awarded the Navy Cross posthumously." This single wartime document conflicts with the Forrestal citation, NHHC, and Hall of Valor. The defensible framing is an unresolved discrepancy, not a proven downgrade. A direct search of the GPO govinfo full-text federal record (Congressional Record and BILLS, indexed through July 2026) returned **zero** results for any Carr medal legislation or upgrade. The 2023 Oklahoma delegation letter to SecNav asked for a **ship naming**, not a medal, and explicitly accepts the Silver Star. **Do not say an upgrade campaign exists.** [NHHC DANFS, Carr (FFG-52); Military Times Hall of Valor; GPO govinfo API; Lankford letter, 13 Nov 2023]

**2. "The 54th of 55 rounds"**
- **Popular telling:** Carr was loading the 54th of 55 shells.
- **Actual:** Hornfischer, working from the survivors, gives **324 of the 325 rounds** in the after magazine. "54" appears to be a corruption of the projectile's **weight**: Hornfischer describes "fifty-four-pound, twenty-one-inch-long projectiles," and the 1944 Navy release calls it a "50-pound projectile." Use 324 of 325. [Hornfischer, *Tin Can Sailors*, ch. 41; NHHC DANFS Samuel B. Roberts]

**3. Carr's dying words**
- **Popular telling:** Carr said something quotable as he died.
- **Actual:** **NOT FOUND.** No source contains direct quoted speech from Carr. The strongest documented version is Hornfischer's, that he "begged Goheen to help him load it." Any quoted sentence attributed to Carr is invented. The best real beat is that he tried **twice**. [Hornfischer, *Tin Can Sailors*, ch. 41]

**4. Only one Medal of Honor came out of Samar**
- **Popular telling:** Various. Some tellings imply multiple.
- **Actual:** **One**, to Cdr. Ernest E. Evans, posthumously. NHHC's official awards tally for Taffy 3 lists 1 Medal of Honor, 22 Navy Crosses, and the Presidential Unit Citation signed by James Forrestal. [NHHC, "TG 77.4.3 ('Taffy 3') Presidential Unit Citation and Other Awards"]

**5. Evans' commissioning-day quote**
- **Popular telling:** Presented as a documented, on-the-record statement at Johnston's commissioning on 27 October 1943.
- **Actual:** The wording that circulates most is on NHHC's own popular Johnston page: "This is going to be a fighting ship. I intend to go in harm's way, and anyone who doesn't want to go along had better get off right now." But the **log-based, expanded DANFS history of Johnston by Robert J. Cressman, which reconstructs commissioning day from the ship's records, does not contain the quote at all.** And NHHC's own scholarly 508 publication footnotes a slightly different wording to **Hornfischer p. 48**, a popular narrative built from survivor recollection. So both circulating versions trace to postwar memory, not to a contemporaneous document. **Correct framing: he is remembered as having said it, the phrase "go in harm's way" is John Paul Jones', and the exact wording is not in the archival record.** [NHHC, Johnston (DD-557) topic page; NHHC DANFS Johnston; NHHC, "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency," n. on Hornfischer p. 48]

**6. Sprague's reaction line**
- **Popular telling:** Sprague is quoted saying something like "Air plot, tell him to check his identification."
- **Actual:** **NOT FOUND** at Tier 1. Sprague's *documented* action-report wording is: "It did not appear any of our ships could survive another five minutes. . . . The task unit was surrounded by the ultimate of desperate circumstances." What is independently documented is that **Brooks' superiors demanded he confirm the sighting** before they would accept it, which is the same beat and is sourced. Also **NOT FOUND**: the verbatim text of Sprague's own transmissions. The widely quoted "AN ENEMY FLEET CONSISTING OF BBs AND CRUISERS FIFTEEN MILES ASTERN CLOSING US" appears only in popular sites. Use Kinkaid's documented relay instead: "ENEMY BATTLESHIPS AND CRUISERS REPORTED FIRING ON TASK UNIT 77.4.3, FROM 15 MILES ASTERN." [NHHC DANFS Gambier Bay; NHHC, "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency"; CTF 77 Action Report]

**7. Copeland's address to the crew**
- **Popular telling:** Wildly variable paraphrases.
- **Actual:** This one **is** documented, in his own action report, footnoted to NARA RG 38. He told them it would be "a fight against overwhelming odds from which survival could not be expected, during which time we would do what damage we could." (Cox's H-Gram 084 renders it in the first person: "going into battle against overwhelming odds, from which survival cannot be expected. We will do what damage we can." Minor tense variation between renderings, same document.) He later admitted: "My hands were ice cold from fear." [NHHC, "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency," footnoted to RG 38 p. 14; NHHC H-Gram 084; NHHC DANFS Samuel B. Roberts]

**8. What killed Chokai**
- **Popular telling:** White Plains sank a heavy cruiser with her single 5-inch stern gun.
- **Actual:** Partial at best, and the official record hedges. Chokai took six 5-inch hits at 0851, attributed variously to the escort carriers and to Samuel B. Roberts. At 0859 a **secondary explosion, probably of her own deck-mounted torpedoes**, knocked out her engines and rudder. After 0905 four Avengers from Kitkun Bay's VC-5 put a 500-pound SAP bomb into her stern. She was finally **scuttled by torpedoes from the Japanese destroyer Fujinami at 2148.** NHHC's own Director hedges the origin of the crippling hit: "a shell from White Plains (CVE-66) **may** have hit the torpedo bank on the heavy cruiser Chokai… (the hit **might** also have been from a bomb)." [combinedfleet.com Chokai TROM; NHHC, "The Battle off Samar"; NHHC H-Gram 084]

**9. What killed Chikuma**
- **Popular telling:** Sunk by Samuel B. Roberts' 608 rounds, or by surface gunfire generally.
- **Actual:** **Aircraft torpedoes.** Chikuma was hit by a Mk. 13 from Taffy 2 Avengers at 0853, dead in the water two minutes later, hit by two more from Kitkun Bay's VC-5 at 1105, and by three more from Ommaney Bay's VC-75 after 1415. She capsized around 1430. **No Japanese source records surface-gunfire damage to Chikuma.** Samuel B. Roberts did put 608 rounds into her upper works, and it did suppress and blind her, but it did not sink her. **The claim that Roberts fired 600-plus rounds into Chokai conflates two different cruisers.** [combinedfleet.com Chikuma TROM; NHHC DANFS Samuel B. Roberts]

**10. What killed Suzuya**
- **Popular telling:** Sunk by American bombs.
- **Actual:** Sunk by **her own Long Lance torpedoes.** A near miss to starboard at 1050 detonated her loaded No. 1 torpedo mount; her remaining torpedoes and ammunition cooked off at noon; she sank at 1322. Kurita confirmed the mechanism to USSBS. [combinedfleet.com Suzuya TROM; USSBS Nav No. 9]

**11. Heermann sank or hit Haruna**
- **Popular telling:** Heermann torpedoed Haruna.
- **Actual:** Heermann's rangefinder operator observed what looked like a hit, but DANFS itself states: "**Japanese records do not confirm that Haruna was hit by any torpedoes during the engagement.**" Haruna's TROM records evading torpedoes and no hits. The real, documented, and much better story is that the missed spread ran on and chased Yamato out of the fight. [NHHC DANFS Heermann; combinedfleet.com Haruna TROM]

**12. St. Lo and "the first kamikaze attack"**
- **Popular telling:** St. Lo was the first successful organized kamikaze attack.
- **Actual:** **Split the claim.** St. Lo was the **first major warship sunk by a kamikaze**. But the first successful organized kamikaze attack of that day struck **Taffy 1** about three hours earlier, at 0740, when Santee (CVE-29) became the first US carrier hit by a suicide plane. Say "first ship sunk," not "first attack." [NHHC, "The Battle off Samar"; CTF 77 Action Report]

**13. Kurita ran out of fuel**
- **Popular telling:** He broke off because he was low on fuel.
- **Actual:** He explicitly denied it. USSBS Nav No. 9: "**There was no consideration for fuel. There was no consideration for how to get home. We had enough ammunition.**" Fuel was his reason for wanting to be through San Bernardino Strait before dark, but it is not what he gave as the reason for breaking off or for skipping Leyte Gulf. (Koyanagi does mention fuel shortage as a factor in abandoning the chase, so present the nuance, not a flat contradiction.) [USSBS Nav No. 9; USSBS Nav No. 35]

**14. Why Kurita actually turned back**
- **Popular telling:** Cowardice, or exhaustion, take your pick.
- **Actual:** Kurita gave **different reasons at different points in his life**, and only the earliest has a paper trail. Lay them out rather than picking one.
  - **1945, USSBS, the only sworn contemporaneous account:** the Gulf was militarily pointless and lethal. "The conclusion from our gunfire and anti-aircraft fire during the day had led me to believe in my uselessness, my ineffectual position, if I proceeded into LEYTE Bay where I would come under even heavier aircraft attack. I therefore concluded to go north and join Admiral OZAWA." And: "It wasn't a question of destruction that was neither here nor there. It was a question of what good I could do in the Bay." And: "**The decision was a momentary one.**" **Note the absence: he says nothing about exhaustion, sleeplessness, or saving his men.**
  - **1947, to *Life*:** he had been "definitely influenced in deciding to withdraw" by hearing American plain-language transmissions. [Morison, Vol. XII, pp. 299-300, citing Gilbert Cant, *Life*, 24 Nov 1947, p. 85]
  - **1950s, to journalist Masanori Ito:** a "mistake of exhaustion," he had not slept for three days.
  - **Just before his death in 1978, to a hometown schoolmate:** he turned around because it was not worth sacrificing his men.
  - **Caveat you must keep:** the 1950s and 1978 versions are **hearsay reported by Evan Thomas** from a journalist interview and an unnamed friend. Thomas is credentialed and the venue is USNI, so it clears the bar as a citation, but it is not a documented Kurita statement.
  - **The misidentification is documented and matters:** Yamato's log recorded "six fleet carriers." Kurita's staff estimated the escort carriers at 30 knots when they were making 17 or 18, and believed they had lost them when they were seven miles away.
  - **The 0945 phantom carrier report is documented as having been received and as having been false.** Koyanagi: "(This report was later found groundless.)" **No contemporaneous record of the actual telegram has ever been found in Japan.** Some critics suspect Kurita's staff concocted it; some wonder if it was an American ruse. **Both theories are unverified. Do not assert either.**
  - **Morison's verdict (1958):** not cowardice but imagination and lost control. "Kurita imagined his enemy to be manyfold the strength that it was, ordered General Attack, lost tactical control of his force and broke off after doing relatively slight damage." Morison's Vol. XII contains **no** attribution of the decision to fatigue. **Anyone saying "Morison called it exhaustion" is wrong.**
  - **Koyanagi's self-contradiction is real and worth airing:** on the tactical break-off, "there was no alternative to what we did." On the operational decision, "we should have continued to Leyte Gulf. And even under the circumstances and with our estimate of the enemy situation at that time, we should have gone into the gulf." He is defending one call and condemning the other. Do not smooth it.
  [USSBS Nav No. 9; Koyanagi, USNI *Proceedings*, Feb 1953; Morison Vol. XII; Evan Thomas, *Naval History*, Oct 2004 and Dec 2006]

**15. "The world wonders" and Kurita**
- **Popular telling:** Kurita intercepted the "world wonders" message, or the padding incident somehow reached the Japanese.
- **Actual:** **Two entirely separate things, and conflating them is the single most common error in retellings.** The padding incident is purely internal to the US Navy. Nimitz's actual message was six words: "WHERE IS RPT WHERE IS TASK FORCE THIRTY FOUR." Everything before "GG" and after "RR" was cryptographic padding. What **Kurita** actually intercepted was **Kinkaid's uncoded plain-language distress traffic**, which he assumed was a trap. **No historian found links the padding incident to anything Kurita received.** [NHHC H-Gram 038-2; Richard B. Frank, *Naval History*, Oct 2019; Morison Vol. XII pp. 299-300; Koyanagi, *Proceedings*, Feb 1953]

**16. Nimitz was sarcastically rebuking Halsey**
- **Popular telling:** Nimitz needled Halsey with a taunt.
- **Actual:** **False.** Cdr. Ross Mobilia, USN (Ret.), *Naval History* Oct 2024: "The phrase 'The World Wonders' was **not authored by Nimitz**. It was routine padding added by CINCPAC's communications watch officer." The encoder, hauled in front of then-Capt. Redman, "swore that he meant nothing by the phrase. '**It just popped into my head**.'" He was reassigned, not court-martialed. Richard B. Frank identified him in 2019 as **Ens. John Donald Kaster**, a mustang who had never attended the formal communications-officer course that teaches the padding rules. **The Tennyson theory is plausible but unconfirmed and the encoder's own statement contradicts it.** (The date coincidence is real: the Charge of the Light Brigade was 25 October 1854, exactly 90 years earlier, and Tennyson's line is "All the world wonder'd.") [Mobilia, *Naval History*, Oct 2024; Hamilton, *Proceedings*, Oct 1960; Kahn, *The Codebreakers*, p. 609; Frank, *Naval History*, Oct 2019; NHHC H-038-2]

**17. Halsey's reaction**
- **Popular telling:** He threw his hat, stomped on it, and wept.
- **Actual, his own 1947 words:** "I was as stunned as if I had been struck in the face. The paper rattled in my hands. I snatched off my **cap**, threw it on the deck, and shouted something that I am ashamed to remember." Carney grabbed his arm: "Stop it! What the hell's the matter with you? Pull yourself together!" **He wrote cap, not hat. He did not write that he stomped on it (that is Cox's rendering). Weeping is NOT FOUND in any Tier 1 source.** Also note: **Halsey's own memoir misquotes the message**, rendering the padding as "The whole world wants to know." Every archival source says "THE WORLD WONDERS." He was writing from memory three years later. That is a real primary-source discrepancy worth showing rather than hiding. [Halsey and Bryan, *Admiral Halsey's Story*, 1947, ch. 13]

**18. Halsey turned around because of "the world wonders"**
- **Popular telling:** The padding made him turn.
- **Actual:** Partly. Kinkaid's "WHERE IS LEE. SEND LEE." arrived at essentially the same moment, and Halsey's official war-diary justification cites "the urgent request for assistance from Commander Seventh Fleet," not CINCPAC. Hamilton's careful phrasing: "It tipped the scales." Also note NHHC's own correction: **even if TF 34 had been formed on the Independence night contact, it would not have reached San Bernardino Strait in time to intercept Kurita.** [Halsey memoir; Hamilton, *Proceedings*, Oct 1960; NHHC H-038-2]

**19. The communications failure was a Halsey failure**
- **Popular telling:** Poor radio discipline, one admiral's fault.
- **Actual:** MacArthur had **forbidden** Kinkaid from signalling Nimitz or Halsey directly. All Seventh Fleet traffic was routed through a radio station on Manus that could not cope with the volume. Halsey discovered that a dispatch handed to him at 0922 had been **filed at 0725**, one hour and fifty-seven minutes earlier. Cox adds the kicker: Japanese radio intelligence intercepted Kinkaid's "My situation critical" message, so **Kurita had it before Halsey did.** [CTF 77 Action Report; NHHC H-038-2; NHHC, "Calmness, Courage, and Efficiency"]

**20. Kurita's break-off time**
- **Popular telling:** "At 0911 Kurita broke off."
- **Actual:** The Tier 1 participant figure is **0910**, from Koyanagi: "Admiral Kurita accordingly suspended the pursuit at 0910." NHHC says his last scout plane was shot down "shortly after 0900" and he then decided to break off the surface action. Note that **suspending the pursuit at 0910 and the definitive turn north are two different events**: Kurita told USSBS he turned north "about two hours; anyway less than three hours" after breaking off, and Kinkaid's action report logs the final turn away on course 015 at **1255I/25**. **The "0911" figure circulating widely could not be traced to a Tier 1 source, and the exact wording of Kurita's signal is NOT FOUND.** [Koyanagi, USNI *Proceedings*, Feb 1953; NHHC, "The Battle off Samar"; USSBS Nav No. 9; CTF 77 Action Report]

## Gaps

- **NOT FOUND:** The exact wording and confirmed time of Kurita's break-off signal. Only Koyanagi's "suspended the pursuit at 0910" is Tier 1. The widely circulated "0911" and any quoted signal text trace only to Tier 2/Tier 3 sources.
- **NOT FOUND:** The original Japanese wording of Kurita's 0703 "general attack" order. ("Zengun totsugeki seyo" belongs to Toyoda's 24 October 1800 message and is Tier 2 sourced only.)
- **NOT FOUND:** Sprague's often-quoted first reaction line ("check his identification" and variants). Also not found: the verbatim text of Sprague's own radio transmissions, and the text of his 1945 *American Magazine* first-person account. (americanheritage.com/battle-samar is **not** Sprague's account. It is a third-person narrative by Wilfred P. Deac, *American Heritage*, Dec 1966, Vol 18 Issue 1.)
- **NOT FOUND:** Any archival, log-based source for Evans' commissioning-day quote. Both circulating versions trace to postwar narrative.
- **NOT FOUND:** Any quoted speech from Paul Henry Carr. Also not found: the date his Silver Star was approved or presented, Copeland's action-report text on the Carr scene, and any evidence Copeland's recommendation was formally downgraded.
- **NOT FOUND:** Any Medal of Honor upgrade legislation, BCNR case, or DoD statement for Carr. Searched the federal record directly (GPO govinfo, Congressional Record and BILLS, indexed through July 2026). **Zero results.**
- **NOT FOUND:** A named, specific documented instance of one Japanese ship hitting another with gunfire. RADM Cox's general statement, "both sides fired on their own ships," is the ceiling of the sourcing. (The documented US instance: Hoel was hit "accidentally by anti-aircraft shells from Johnston.")
- **NOT FOUND:** A documented time or order for the Japanese switch from armor-piercing to high-explosive. DANFS observes it around 0850 to 0900 with no timestamp.
- **NOT FOUND:** Any Japanese source stating they loaded AP *because* they expected fleet carriers and battleships. The AP loadout and its uselessness are documented. The causal link is a historian's inference.
- **NOT FOUND:** A numeric duration for Yamato's turn-away. Sources give **ten miles**, not ten minutes. Cox says only "agonizing minutes." Do not say "ten minutes."
- **NOT FOUND:** Damage to Nagato at Samar. Also not supported: any hits on Yamato, Kongo or Haruna from Taffy 3's escorts beyond the single dud shell on Yamato.
- **NOT FOUND:** The names of the officers aboard New Jersey who failed to strip the padding. The account naming Ens. Burton Goldstein and Lt. Charles Fox traces to E. B. Potter, *Nimitz* (Naval Institute Press, 1976), pp. 339-340, which could not be read directly. Attribute to Potter on screen or leave them unnamed.
- **NOT FOUND:** Verbatim assessments of Kurita's decision from Cutler, Hornfischer, Vego, Friedman or Prados. Also not found: any peer-reviewed evaluation of Robert Lundgren's *The World Wonder'd* argument, or the Senshi Sosho position on the turnaway. **Do not attribute a position to Senshi Sosho on air.**
- **BLOCKED:** usni.org returns HTTP 403 to direct fetch. Wayback Machine mirrors work intermittently. Several USNI articles were read via mirrors; Potter's *Nimitz* page text could not be obtained through Google Books, HathiTrust or Internet Archive.

**Unresolved conflicts to state rather than average:**
- Brooks' sighting time: **0637** (DANFS Gambier Bay) vs "about 0650" (DANFS Midway II / St. Lo). His carrier: **St. Lo, VC-65** (two DANFS entries) vs Fanshaw Bay (DANFS Samuel B. Roberts).
- Torpedo attack order times: **0716 / 0742** (NHHC Samar essay) vs **0735 / 0800** (DANFS Samuel B. Roberts).
- Kumano torpedo hit: **0724** (Hackett/Kingsepp TROM) vs **0727** (Tully). Damage location: bow (all sources) vs "near the stern" (Kurita, USSBS, who is almost certainly wrong).
- Escort carrier speed: **17 knots** (Cox) vs **18 knots** (NHHC/Morison). Both Tier 1.
- Yamato's dud shell: "one 15-cm shell that had damaged a motor" above the engine room (Kurita) vs "102 mm dud in the rice locker" (Otani). Two members of the same staff, same event, different answers.
- Samuel B. Roberts firing duration: **"50 furious minutes"** (1944 Navy OPI release) vs 35 minutes (USNI and derivatives).
- St. Lo dead: **141** vs "more than 140" vs **143**.
- TG 78.12 rescue-group departure: shortly after 1700 on 25 Oct vs 0600 on 26 Oct, between two DANFS entries.
- Cox's aggregate 635 dead vs the per-ship DANFS sum, which does not reconcile.
- NHHC's "2 Silver Stars" tally vs the DANFS ship histories, which name at least six recipients.
- Padding separator rule: "double consonants" (Potter, Mobilia) vs "double letters" (Hamilton, from Pacific Fleet communications instructions). GG and RR satisfy either. Safest wording: "a doubled-letter marker."
