# Research Report: The Mark 14 Torpedo Scandal

Research agent, Wave 1. Prepared for the script writer and the QA gate.
No em-dashes used. Every number, date and quote is sourced individually below.

## One-paragraph summary

From December 1941 to roughly September 1943 the standard American submarine torpedo, the Mark 14, carried three separate defects that each hid the next. It ran about 10 to 11 feet deeper than the depth it was set for, so its secret Mark 6 magnetic influence exploder never got close enough to a hull to work correctly. Once the depth error was corrected, the magnetic feature began detonating torpedoes prematurely, on roughly 10 percent of early war shots. Once the magnetic feature was switched off in June 1943, the plain contact exploder was exposed as defective too: on a square 90 degree hit at the Mark 14's 46 knot speed, the guide pins that carried the firing pin block bent under the impact and the pin failed to reach the primer cap, so a perfect shot was more likely to be a dud than a glancing one. All three defects were diagnosed and fixed in the field by the operating forces at Fremantle and Pearl Harbor, using improvised tests: torpedoes fired into a fishing net in Australia, warshots fired into a submerged cliff at Kahoolawe, and inert warheads dropped onto a steel plate. The Bureau of Ordnance had never once fired a production Mark 14 with a live warhead at a real target before the war, and had not conducted a single destructive test of the magnetic exploder since 1926. The scandal is real, well documented, and confirmed by the Navy's own official ordnance history. It is also not unique: the German Navy had substantially the same three failures in 1939 to 1940, and dealt with them by court-martialling four officers.

## Verified timeline

**Prewar**

- **1922** - Development of a magnetic influence exploder begins at the Naval Torpedo Station, Newport, Rhode Island. It becomes the Mark 6. [Rowland and Boyd, official BuOrd history, ch. 6; Wikipedia/Mark 6 exploder citing Blair 1975]
- **8 May 1926** - The only live-fire tests of the magnetic exploder before the war. Two shots fired in Narragansett Bay at the hulked submarine L-8. **Sources disagree on the result.** The official BuOrd history says the hulk was "sunk by the first shot." Wildenberg and Polmar (*Ship Killer*, Naval Institute Press, 2010) say the first torpedo ran underneath the target and did not explode, and the second succeeded. Victor Alpher in *The Submarine Review* (2010) agrees there were two shots and calls it "a 50% success." **The two-shot version is better sourced and more interesting; use it, and note the disagreement is with the Navy's own official history.**
- **After 1926** - No destructive test of the magnetic exploder is ever made again before the war. Official history: "Never again during the 19 years of prewar exploder development was a destructive test made with a torpedo equipped with a magnetic influence exploder. How ironic that success should have been its own deterrent!" [Rowland and Boyd, ch. 6]
- **1931 (January)** - Mark 14 design work begins at Newport; roughly $143,000 allocated. [Wikipedia/Mark 14, cite 4. Tier 2, treat as approximate.]
- **Interwar** - To protect the secret, BuOrd produces the **Mark 5**, a dummy exploder identical to the Mark 6 except for the magnetic feature, and issues it with every torpedo that would later receive the real thing. Official history: "Meanwhile, the fleet for which the weapon was designed did not even know of its existence." And: "even the men working on the mechanism were allowed only the vaguest idea about their project." [Rowland and Boyd, ch. 6]
- **1938** - A destroyer command reports torpedo failures and offers as evidence sand from the bottom on recovered torpedoes. Official history: "evidence of poor maintenance or rough handling impressed the Bureau representative more than the bottom sand which constituted the destroyer command's exhibit A." [Rowland and Boyd, ch. 6]
- **1939** - Newport reports unexplained prematures to the Bureau. Admiral Furlong sends a civilian physicist to Newport. In roughly one week the scientist and his assistants find **four separate sources of prematures**, and report that "the responsible engineers at Newport were not employing proper tests on the Mark 6." [Rowland and Boyd, ch. 6]
- **Mid-1941** - The Mark 6 is issued to the fleet. **Claim requiring hedging:** that only commanding officers and torpedo officers were briefed and enlisted torpedomen were not told. See the hedging section below.
- **October 1941** - Newport tests are interpreted as showing the Mark 14 runs about 4 feet deeper than set. **This is not reported to the submarine commands at the time.** [Milford, *The Submarine Review*, 1996]

**Defect one: depth**

- **10 December 1941** - Japanese air raid destroys Cavite Navy Yard, Philippines. Clay Blair (*Silent Victory*) puts the torpedoes lost ashore at **233**. [Blair via USNI *Naval History*, Dec 2021, "Bleak December"]
- **14 December 1941** - USS Sargo (SS-188), Lt. Cdr. Tyrrell D. Jacobs, fires at a convoy near Cam Ranh Bay. A torpedo explodes about 18 seconds after leaving the tube. Over the patrol Sargo makes eight attacks and scores no hits. Jacobs deactivates the magnetic feature of his Mark 6 exploders **on his own authority** and gets into "considerable difficulty for doing so." This is **18 months before** CINCPAC orders the same thing. [DANFS Sargo I (SS-188), NHHC; Milford 1996]
- **5 January 1942** - BuOrd advises the fleet that the older Mark 10 torpedo runs 4 feet deeper than set. [Milford 1996; Rowland and Boyd give "January 1942"]
- **February to March 1942** - Newport tests, ordered directly by the Chief of BuOrd, confirm a 4 foot error for the Mark 14. [Milford 1996]
- **30 March 1942** - Rear Admiral William H. P. Blandy, Chief of BuOrd, notifies Rear Admiral Thomas Withers Jr., ComSubPac, by letter. [Milford 1996]
- **29 April 1942** - General notification to the submarine forces, BuOrd Circular Letter T-174. [Milford 1996]
- **20 June 1942** - **The net test.** A fishing net is rigged in **Frenchman's Bay at Albany, Western Australia** (NOT Frenchman Bay, Maine). Torpedoes are fired into it at 850 yards. Holes in the net show the torpedoes ran 11 to 15 feet below set depth. Wikipedia's account, citing Bowling, has USS Skipjack (Lt. Cdr. James W. "Jim" Coe) firing a torpedo set for 10 feet that pierced the net at 25 feet. Follow-up firings the next day gave an average of about 11 feet deep. [Bowling, *The Submarine Review*, 2002; Milford 1996; NHHC H-gram 008-3]
- **1 August 1942** - BuOrd formally concedes the error. Official history: "On August 1, 1942, the services were officially informed of the 10-foot error." Bowling: "BuOrd confirmed that tests conducted at Newport corroborated reports that Mk 14 torpedoes were running 10 feet deeper than set." This is **almost eight months into the war**. [Rowland and Boyd; Bowling 2002]

**Defect two: the magnetic exploder**

- **Fall 1942** - With the depth error corrected, prematures surface as a problem. "Direct hits were often duds; on perhaps 10 percent of the early war shots premature explosions made hits impossible." [Rowland and Boyd, ch. 6]
- **Late 1942** - BuOrd recommends a stronger spring to actuate the firing pin on contact settings, but is "too involved with deep running, prematures, and production problems to recognize the full import of reported duds." [Rowland and Boyd, ch. 6]
- **27 April 1943** - BuOrd states the Mark 6 is susceptible to prematuring if set for 12 feet or less, and recommends disconnecting the magnetic feature in favor of contact shots. [Bowling 2002]
- **3 and 7 May 1943** - BuOrd tells Admiral King, CNO, that Mark 6 effectiveness would rise 10 to 30 percent if the arming distance were raised from 450 to 700 yards, subject to a list of further firing restrictions. King rejects this and concurs with Lockwood that the Mark 6 should be replaced outright. [Bowling 2002; Rowland and Boyd]
- **24 June 1943** - **Admiral Chester W. Nimitz, CinCPacFlt, orders ComSubPac and ComDesPac to inactivate the magnetic exploders on all torpedoes.** [NHHC H-gram 008-3, "Deactivation was ordered on 24 June 1943"; Bowling 2002; Milford 1996; Alpher 2010; Wikipedia citing Blair] **NOTE THE CONFLICT:** the official BuOrd history (Rowland and Boyd) dates this order to **July 24**. See Myth vs Record.
- **25 June 1943 (the next day)** - BuOrd asks Nimitz why. Nimitz replies that his decision was made because the Mark 6 was "ineffective" and because of "the impracticability of selecting the proper conditions [recommended by BuOrd] under which to fire." [Bowling 2002, quoting the exchange]

**Defect three: the contact exploder**

- **24 July 1943** - **USS Tinosa (SS-283), Lt. Cdr. Lawrence Randall "Dan" Daspit, vs Tonan Maru No. 3.** Numbers in the next section. Daspit brings the last torpedo home unfired for examination. [Rowland and Boyd; NHHC DANFS Tinosa I (SS-283); Bowling 2002]
- **Within a month of Tinosa** - USS Haddock, after damaging a 10,500 ton tanker with two hits, fires **11 more torpedoes in three further attacks on the same ship** without a single further explosion. [Rowland and Boyd, ch. 6]
- **August to September 1943** - Lockwood's Pearl Harbor tests, running concurrently with BuOrd's own tests in Chesapeake Bay. At Pearl the submariners fire warshots into a submerged cliff at **Kahoolawe** and drop inert-loaded warheads onto a steel plate. Findings: the firing pin was released but did not travel far enough along its guide rails to strike the primer cap hard enough. The guide pins bent under a deceleration equivalent to about 500 g with roughly 190 pounds of friction on the guide rails on a square hit. [Bowling 2002; Rowland and Boyd; NHHC H-gram 008-3]
- **30 September 1943** - USS Barb departs Pearl Harbor with 20 torpedoes, all fitted with the modified firing pins. [Bowling 2002]
- **Mid-October 1943** - The submarine tender USS Holland has produced enough modified firing pins for every torpedo issued to submarines leaving Pearl on patrol. [Bowling 2002]

**Afterward**

- **December 1943 to March 1944** - Southwest Pacific submarines under Rear Admiral Ralph Waldo Christie give up the magnetic feature. **Sources conflict on the date.** Milford (1996): "COMSUBSOWESTPAC reluctantly followed suit in December 1943." Bowling (2002): "not until March 1944." Rowland and Boyd: "By spring 1944." Wikipedia, citing Blair, has Vice Admiral Thomas C. Kinkaid ordering it on **20 January 1944**, over Christie's head. Present as "over the winter of 1943 to 1944, months after the Pacific Fleet."
- **24 to 25 October 1944** - Loss of USS Tang. See the Tang section.

## Key figures (rank at the time of the events)

- **Rear Admiral (later Vice Admiral) Charles Andrews Lockwood Jr.** - Took over Submarines Southwest Pacific (Fremantle) in late May/June 1942; ordered the Frenchman's Bay net tests there. Became **Commander Submarine Force Pacific Fleet (ComSubPac)** in February 1943 after Rear Adm. Robert H. English was killed in an air crash, and ran the Kahoolawe cliff and drop tests from Pearl Harbor in August and September 1943. Called the Mark 6 a "Rube Goldberg" device "with 5 or 6 things that might go wrong." Note: Bowling's 2002 article mislabels his 1942 job as "ComSubSoPac"; the correct 1942 command is Southwest Pacific. [Rowland and Boyd; NHHC H-gram 008-3; Bowling 2002]
- **Admiral Chester W. Nimitz**, Commander in Chief, Pacific Fleet - Granted Lockwood's request and ordered the magnetic exploders inactivated, 24 June 1943. [NHHC H-gram 008-3]
- **Admiral Ernest J. King**, Commander in Chief US Fleet and Chief of Naval Operations - Intervened after the Australian net tests, forcing BuOrd to re-investigate the depth error; in May 1943 rejected BuOrd's arming-distance workaround and backed replacing the Mark 6. The official history credits the summer 1942 net firings to "the Commander in Chief, United States Fleet." [Rowland and Boyd; Milford 1996; Bowling 2002]
- **Rear Admiral William H. P. "Spike" Blandy**, Chief of the Bureau of Ordnance (from February 1941) - Signed the 30 March 1942 letter to Withers on the 4 foot depth error. [Milford 1996]
- **Rear Admiral William R. Furlong**, Chief of BuOrd in the late 1930s - Sent the outside physicist to Newport in 1939. [Rowland and Boyd]
- **Rear Admiral Thomas Withers Jr.**, ComSubPac at the outbreak of war - Refused to let his skippers deactivate the Mark 6, and is the man on the receiving end of Blandy's depth-error letter. [Milford 1996; Wikipedia/Mark 14 cite 71, Tier 2 for the refusal detail]
- **Rear Admiral Robert H. English**, ComSubPac from May 1942 until his death in January 1943 - Also refused to believe the exploder was defective. [Tier 2, Wikipedia and Fandom summaries. Corroborate before asserting.]
- **Rear Admiral Ralph Waldo Christie**, Commander Submarines Southwest Pacific from 1943 (Fremantle) - A torpedo design officer who had worked on the Mark 6 at Newport and its chief defender in the Pacific. The official history says his continued use of it "stemmed from no feeling of satisfaction with the mechanism, but from fear that if his command joined those to the north in inactivation, the magnetic exploder, with all its potentialities, might become a dead issue for the war." His diary on giving it up: "Today, the long hard battle on the Mark VI magnetic feature ends, with defeat. I am forced to inactivate all magnetic exploders. We are licked." [Rowland and Boyd; Alpher 2010 for the diary quote]
- **Lieutenant Commander Lawrence Randall "Dan" Daspit**, commanding USS Tinosa (SS-283) - Commissioning CO, in command 15 January 1943. Fired his torpedoes into Tonan Maru No. 3 on 24 July 1943 and brought the last one home for examination. [NHHC DANFS Tinosa I (SS-283) gives his full name and rank]
- **Lieutenant Commander Tyrrell D. Jacobs**, commanding USS Sargo (SS-188) - Deactivated his magnetic exploders in December 1941, 18 months before it was authorized, and was reprimanded. [Milford 1996; DANFS Sargo I]
- **Lieutenant Commander James W. "Jim" Coe**, commanding USS Skipjack - Fired the net-test torpedo at Albany. [Tier 2 for the attribution to Coe specifically; the net test itself is Tier 1-adjacent.]
- **Commander H. P. Hottle**, commanding USS Grouper - His September 1943 patrol report to Christie: "It would appear far better to sink the enemy vessels encountered than to continue spoiling good chances just to prove that a really useless mechanism can be made to function a fair proportion of the time." [Bowling 2002]
- **Commander Richard Hetherington O'Kane**, commanding USS Tang (SS-306) - NHHC's ship page and the Medal of Honor citation both style him **Lieutenant Commander** at the time of Tang's loss; he was promoted to Commander while a prisoner of war and received the Medal of Honor from President Truman on 27 March 1946. **Use "Lieutenant Commander" for October 1944.** [NHHC, USS Tang (SS-306)]
- **NOT a correct name for this story: "Charles Wilkes."** The submarine commander in the Philippines in December 1941 was **Captain John Wilkes**, Commander Submarines Asiatic Fleet. Do not write "Charles Wilkes" (that is the 19th century explorer).

## Numbers (high risk, each sourced)

**Depth error**
- **10 feet deeper than set** on average, from submerged tubes. Official BuOrd history, and the figure BuOrd formally admitted on 1 August 1942. [Rowland and Boyd, ch. 6]
- **11 feet** was the figure the operating forces measured at Albany; net holes ranged **11 to 15 feet** below set depth. [Bowling 2002; Milford 1996]
- **Safe phrasing: "about ten to eleven feet deeper than it was set for."** The pitch's "ran 10 feet deeper" is defensible and is the Navy's own official number. Do not say the measured error was exactly 10 feet, because the fleet's own measurement was 11.
- Corroborating detail: a torpedo set for **10 feet pierced the net at 25 feet**, the single most dramatic data point. [Wikipedia/Mark 14 citing Bowling. Tier 2 attribution, hedge it or attribute it as "one shot".]

**Prematures**
- "on perhaps 10 percent of the early war shots premature explosions made hits impossible." [Rowland and Boyd, ch. 6] Note the word "perhaps": the official history is itself estimating.

**Duds / contact exploder**
- **Roughly 70 percent** of exploders failed to detonate on a 90 degree hit, per the Pearl Harbor test results. [Wikipedia/Mark 14 cite 87, tracing to Blair] Bowling's independent account of the drop tests gives **7 duds out of 10 dummy warheads**, which is 70 percent and corroborates the figure by a different route. [Bowling 2002]
- Drop test: **10 inert warheads fitted with Mark 6 exploders, dropped from 90 feet onto a steel plate, 7 duds.** [Bowling 2002] **The ibiblio transcription of the official BuOrd history says "900 feet." That is almost certainly wrong.** A 90 foot drop yields an impact of about 76 feet per second, roughly 45 knots, which matches the Mark 14's 46 knot high speed exactly. A 900 foot drop would give about 240 feet per second, roughly 142 knots, which simulates nothing. **Use 90 feet.**
- Impact physics: going from the 33.5 knot Mark 13 to the 46.3 knot Mark 14 nearly doubled the inertial forces at impact, bending the vertical guide pins so the firing pin block sometimes missed the percussion caps. [Milford 1996]

**Tinosa, 24 July 1943 (the numbers vary; give the range or use the official history)**
- Official BuOrd history: **"In all, 15 torpedoes were fired at the oiler; 12 known hits were claimed, yet the dream target drifted on. Only the first 2 spreads produced explosions."** [Rowland and Boyd, ch. 6] This is the safest single citation.
- Bowling (2002) describes the second phase specifically: **8 Mark 14s fired at a dead-in-the-water 19,262 ton target from point blank range of 850 yards on an optimum 90 degree track.** [Bowling 2002]
- Wikipedia's breakdown: 4 fired from 4,000 yards, 2 hit; 2 more fired, 2 hit; then 9 more from 875 yards, all duds. That totals 15 fired, 13 hits, 11 duds.
- Popular figures in circulation range from "15 fired, 13 hits, 11 duds" to "15 fired, 10 hits, 8 duds."
- **Recommended narration: "Fifteen torpedoes. About a dozen hits. Two explosions."** That is the official history's own arithmetic and survives any QA challenge. **Avoid asserting a precise dud count.**
- Target: **Tonan Maru No. 3, 19,262 gross tons**, a converted whale factory ship in service as a tanker, running Palau to Truk. She was **damaged, not sunk**, on this occasion. NHHC's DANFS entry for Tinosa's patrol is blunt: "Handicapped by the faulty firing mechanism of her Mark 14 torpedoes, she damaged only a single tanker on her second patrol." [NHHC DANFS Tinosa I (SS-283)]
- **Patrol numbering conflict:** NHHC/DANFS calls this Tinosa's **second** war patrol (departed Midway 7 July 1943, returned Hawaii 4 August 1943). Several popular accounts call it her third. **Follow NHHC.**

**Haddock, August 1943**
- After 2 hits on a 10,500 ton tanker, **11 more torpedoes in 3 further attacks, no further explosions.** [Rowland and Boyd, ch. 6]

**Cost and production**
- A torpedo in the interwar period "was valued at around $10,000 (about the same as a fighter aircraft airframe complete except for engine) and destroying one in testing was a risk that only the fearless were willing to run." [Milford, *The Submarine Review*, 1996] NHHC independently puts the cost at "about $160K in today's dollars," which is consistent. [NHHC H-gram 008-3] **The $10,000 figure is sourced and usable.**
- Mark 6 exploders: 30 production units from General Electric at Schenectady at $1,000 apiece. [Wikipedia/Mark 6 citing Blair 1975, p. 61. Tier 2 attribution to a Tier 1 book; hedge or attribute to Blair.]
- Approximately **13,000 Mark 14 torpedoes** were manufactured during the war. [US Naval Undersea Museum]
- Total US torpedo production 1939 to 1945: **almost 60,000**, split about evenly between Navy torpedo stations and civilian contractors. [Milford 1996]
- **By the time of the 1942 net tests, Pacific Fleet submarines had fired over 800 torpedoes, roughly a year's production at the time, with very little to show for it.** [NHHC H-gram 008-3]

**Duration of the scandal**
- Wikipedia's summary, tracing to Blair: **"After twenty-one months of war, the three major defects of the Mark 14 torpedo had at last been isolated."**
- Bowling: "Almost two years later, all of these defects had been detected and corrected."
- US Naval Undersea Museum: "By fall 1943, after a year and a half of frantic efforts."
- Milford: the scandal "emerged and peaked between December 1941 and August 1943."
- **December 1941 to September 1943 is 21 months.** See Myth vs Record on the "twenty months" framing.

**Human cost of the submarine war (context)**
- **52 US submarines lost, about 3,500 officers and enlisted men killed**, the highest casualty rate of any branch of the American armed forces, roughly one in five. [NHHC, US Submarine Losses World War II; National Park Service, "Submarines in World War II"; US Naval Undersea Museum] Sources give 3,505 or 3,506; **use "about 3,500" rather than picking one.**

## Absurd / comedic beats (all real, all sourced)

1. **The Mark 5 decoy exploder.** BuOrd built a dummy version of the Mark 6, identical except for the secret magnetic feature, and issued one with every torpedo that would later receive the real thing, purely to preserve the secret. [Rowland and Boyd]
2. **The fleet did not know the weapon existed.** Official history, verbatim: "Meanwhile, the fleet for which the weapon was designed did not even know of its existence." [Rowland and Boyd]
3. **The workers building it were kept in the dark too.** "even the men working on the mechanism were allowed only the vaguest idea about their project. A selected group from the research section at Newport did all of the assembling and testing in rigidly maintained seclusion." [Rowland and Boyd]
4. **The secrecy was pointless.** England, Germany and Italy all had magnetic exploders of their own before the war started. Japan did not have one only because she thought them impractical, and may have over-degaussed her ships as a countermeasure anyway. [Rowland and Boyd]
5. **Success killed the testing.** One successful shot in 1926 ended nineteen years of destructive testing. "How ironic that success should have been its own deterrent!" [Rowland and Boyd]
6. **The measuring instrument shared the bug.** The depth recorder in the test head measured water pressure at a point on the torpedo skin where the hydrodynamic pressure was low by almost exactly the same amount as at the depth gear's sensing port. So the instrument checking the depth was wrong in the same direction and by the same amount as the fault it was supposed to detect, and the torpedo appeared to run at the set depth while actually running deep. [Milford 1996; Rowland and Boyd: "the measuring device checked each other, but both were improperly placed."]
7. **They tested with heads that float.** Exercise heads were made to float so the expensive torpedo could be cheaply recovered, which changed the weight and trim from the heavier warhead and hid the deep run. [Rowland and Boyd]
8. **The Bureau discounted the net tests on principle.** When net firings and recorder data disagreed, "the results of the latter were discounted," because nets do not hang perfectly straight in a current. [Rowland and Boyd] Which is why, when Lockwood used a net in 1942, BuOrd's first move was to attack the methodology. [Milford 1996]
9. **The dud definition.** Among submarine captains "the feeling was soon prevalent that the Bureau definition of a dud was 'a skipper's alibi to explain his miss.'" [Rowland and Boyd] **See the hedging note: this is the official history reporting what submariners believed, not a BuOrd statement.**
10. **The 1938 sand exhibit.** A destroyer command brought bottom sand from recovered torpedoes as physical proof the weapons were running deep; the Bureau's man was more impressed by "evidence of poor maintenance or rough handling." [Rowland and Boyd]
11. **Lockwood's Rube Goldberg line.** He called the Mark 6 a "Rube Goldberg" device with five or six things that might go wrong. [Rowland and Boyd]
12. **The day after.** Nimitz orders the magnetic exploders switched off on 24 June 1943. The next day BuOrd sends a message asking why. [Bowling 2002]
13. **BuOrd's proposed fix was a list of restrictions.** BuOrd told King the Mark 6 would be 10 to 30 percent more effective if the arming run were lengthened from 450 to 700 yards, along with a list of further conditions on when you were allowed to shoot. King said no and asked for a different exploder. [Bowling 2002]
14. **The tactical workaround was to aim badly on purpose.** Because a perfect square hit was the one most likely to bend the guide pins, submarines were told to take oblique shots, which cut the dud rate roughly in half. [NHHC H-gram 008-3; Wikipedia/Mark 14 cite 88]
15. **Testing by shooting a cliff.** Warshot Mark 14s fired into a submerged cliff face at Kahoolawe, and the duds swum out and recovered for autopsy. [Bowling 2002; Rowland and Boyd; NHHC H-gram 008-3]
16. **Testing by dropping warheads off a crane.** Ten inert warheads with live exploders dropped 90 feet onto a steel plate. Seven duds. [Bowling 2002]
17. **The Grouper patrol report line** about proving "that a really useless mechanism can be made to function a fair proportion of the time," written to Christie, who had helped design it. [Bowling 2002]
18. **The fix was machined on a repair ship.** The modified firing pins were produced not by Newport but by the machine shop of the submarine tender USS Holland at Pearl Harbor, and within about six weeks Holland had made enough for every torpedo leaving Pearl. [Bowling 2002]

## USS Tang (verified separately, per instruction)

- **Fatal weapon: a Mark 18 electric torpedo, NOT a Mark 14.** NHHC, verbatim: "Tang (SS-306,) Lieutenant Commander Richard O'Kane commanding, was sunk on 25 October 1944 by her own circling torpedo, a new Mark 18." [NHHC H-gram 008-3] This is a Tier 1 .mil confirmation. **State it plainly in the script.**
- **Date.** The official *US Submarine Losses World War II* volume dates the loss **24 October 1944**. The action ran through the night of 24 to 25 October; NHHC's H-gram says 25 October, and O'Kane's account puts the final shot at about 0230. **Safest phrasing: "the night of 24 October 1944."** Do not assert a clock time as record.
- **Casualties: 78 lost, 9 survivors.** Both figures in the pitch are correct and confirmed. [*US Submarine Losses World War II*; NHHC USS Tang (SS-306) page confirms "Only nine of the crew, including the commanding officer, survived"]
- **The escape.** Thirteen men escaped from the forward torpedo room using Momsen lungs. Of those 13, **only eight reached the surface, and of those only five were able to keep swimming until picked up.** Together with men already in the water from the bridge, that made nine survivors. Official volume detail: by the time the last man got out, "the paint on the bulkhead was scorching, melting, and running down." [*US Submarine Losses World War II*, Tang entry]
- **Aftermath.** The nine were picked up by a Japanese escort vessel the following morning and spent the rest of the war as prisoners. [NHHC USS Tang (SS-306)]
- **The patrol.** Tang's fifth and last patrol was the most successful patrol by a US submarine in the war. Of 24 torpedoes carried, 22 hit, one missed, and the 24th and last sank her. [*US Submarine Losses World War II*] O'Kane received the Medal of Honor for the actions of 23 and 24 October 1944. [NHHC]
- **Is the Mark 18 a fair connective thread?** **Partly, and it needs care.**
  - Fair: circular runs were a known, unsolved US torpedo problem. NHHC states: "The fourth major problem with the Mark 14 was a tendency to run in circles... this problem was never completely solved." And: "The Mark 18 had no protection against circular runs, a defect which claimed Tang for certain." [NHHC H-gram 008-3; Wikipedia/Mark 18]
  - Fair: the Mark 18 had its own troubled development. It was reverse-engineered from the German G7e electric torpedo after examples came into Allied hands, pushed by King in 1942 because BuOrd had no wakeless torpedo. Its batteries underperformed and gave off dangerous hydrogen. It only met Bureau specifications in spring 1943 and use was left voluntary until a better battery arrived in fall 1943. [Rowland and Boyd ch. 6; Wikipedia/Mark 18 and Milford's NSL series]
  - **A stretch:** treating Tang as the closing chapter of the *Mark 14* scandal. Different torpedo, different defect, and by October 1944 the Mark 14's three defects had been fixed for a year. **Frame it as "the Navy shipped another under-tested torpedo, and it cost a boat," not "the Mark 14 killed Tang."**
  - Also relevant: **USS Tullibee (SS-284) was lost to a circular run on 26 March 1944, and NHHC says the torpedo type is unknown.** [NHHC H-gram 008-3] Do not attribute Tullibee to a Mark 14 or a Mark 18.

## Myth vs record (corrections)

**1. "Frenchman Bay" is in Australia, not Maine.**
Popular telling and the pitch imply Lockwood shot into nets somewhere convenient to Newport. Actual: the net tests were at **Frenchman's Bay, Albany, Western Australia**, on 20 June 1942, run by Lockwood as commander of Southwest Pacific submarines out of Fremantle. [Bowling 2002; Milford 1996; NHHC H-gram 008-3] Getting this wrong is a common tell in the YouTube retellings.

**2. The magnetic exploder deactivation date.**
Popular and best-supported: **24 June 1943**, by Nimitz, to ComSubPac and ComDesPac. Supported by NHHC (Director's H-gram 008-3, a .mil source), by Bowling and Milford in *The Submarine Review*, and by Blair via Wikipedia. **The Navy's own official ordnance history (Rowland and Boyd) says July 24, and adds that this was the very day of the Tinosa attack.** That neat coincidence is almost certainly an error in the official history: every other account, and the causal logic of the story, requires the order to precede Tinosa, because Tinosa's duds are what proved the *contact* pistol defective after the magnetic feature was already off. **Use 24 June 1943 and, if the script wants the detail, note that the Navy's own official history got its own date wrong by a month.**

**3. The Tang beat.**
Tang was sunk by a **Mark 18 electric torpedo**, not a Mark 14, confirmed by NHHC. Any script that folds Tang in as "the Mark 14's last victim" is wrong. [NHHC H-gram 008-3]

**4. "Twenty months."**
The pitch says twenty months. The best-sourced framing is **twenty-one months**, December 1941 to September 1943, which is Blair's own phrasing as carried in Wikipedia's summary. "Almost two years" (Bowling) and "a year and a half" (Naval Undersea Museum, measuring from mid-1942) are also in circulation. **Recommend "nearly two years" or "twenty-one months." Avoid "twenty months," it is short and unsupported.**

**5. Total torpedoes wasted.**
There is no defensible single number. NHHC gives one solid anchor: **by mid-1942 Pacific Fleet submarines had fired over 800 torpedoes with very little to show for it.** [NHHC H-gram 008-3] **Per the standing caveat, do not give a total-wasted figure.**

**6. "The Bureau said the submariners were aiming wrong."**
**This is directionally true but the popular telling is stronger than the documents support.** What is actually documented:
- The Bureau's own official history records that submarine captains believed "the Bureau definition of a dud was 'a skipper's alibi to explain his miss,'" and then immediately adds that this attitude "was only partly justified by facts." [Rowland and Boyd] That is the fleet's perception, recorded by the Bureau, not a Bureau statement.
- In 1938 a Bureau representative dismissed a destroyer command's physical evidence in favor of "poor maintenance or rough handling." [Rowland and Boyd]
- BuOrd and Newport "criticized the methodology and were reluctant to accept the results of the Frenchman's Bay firings." [Milford 1996]
- ComSubPac Withers refused his skippers permission to deactivate the exploder. [Tier 2]
- NHHC states flatly: "The initial response from the Bureau of Ordnance (BuOrd) was to blame the submarine skippers ('operator error')." [NHHC H-gram 008-3]
**NOT FOUND: a single dated BuOrd document that literally tells submariners they are aiming wrong.** The blame is documented as an institutional posture, in NHHC's own words and in the Bureau's own history, but the script should say "the Bureau's answer was operator error" and attribute it to NHHC, rather than inventing a quotation.

**7. Was BuOrd uniquely negligent? No, and this is the most useful complication available.**
The **German Navy had substantially the same three failures at the same scale, earlier.** Milford, writing in *The Submarine Review*: "This spectrum of problems was not unique to the U.S. torpedo establishment. Almost the same set, defective depth control, unsatisfactory and untested magnetic exploder and a contact exploder that did not work at certain striking angles, occurred in the German Navy and many of the responses of the shore establishment to the problems were also the same." In April 1940 during the Norway campaign German U-boats suffered torpedo failures "on a massive scale," with failure rates reckoned at 25 to 30 percent at the start of 1940; U-boats were ordered to use contact pistols only in Norwegian waters. Dönitz got Raeder to convene a board of inquiry. **The difference in outcome is the sharp end: four senior German officers were court-martialled, convicted and punished, where in the American case nobody was.** [Milford 1996; uboat.net, "The Norwegian Operation and the Torpedo Crisis," Tier 2 for the German specifics, corroborate before quoting a figure] The British abandoned magnetic exploders early; the Germans tried a latitude sensitivity adjustment and gave it up too. [Milford 1996]

**8. Was the failure one man's fault, or institutional? Institutional.**
Milford identifies the drivers as (a) Depression-era budgets that made destroying a $10,000 torpedo in a test unthinkable, (b) the near-total isolation of Newport from the wider American engineering community after it won a monopoly on torpedo development in 1923, partly protected by New England jobs politics, and (c) "draconian security, which in some cases even excluded the operating forces from full knowledge of the weapons they were expected to use." He describes the resulting culture as an "arrogant we are the experts attitude." **No single individual is fairly blamed.** Withers, English and Christie each blocked a fix at some point, and Blandy's Bureau was slow, but the failure mode was structural. Contrast: at roughly the same time BuOrd co-opted Ford Instrument, Arma and Sperry for fire control range keepers, and NRL shared radar with the Army Signal Corps, Bell Labs and RCA. Newport did the opposite. [Milford 1996]

**9. "The submariners fixed everything themselves without Newport or Washington" is an overstatement.**
Milford quotes an unnamed "distinguished and truly great submariner" writing that all three defects were solved by the operating forces "without help from Newport or Washington," and answers directly: **"This is certainly an overstatement."** He notes BuOrd's response, once the contact exploder problem was identified, "was notably prompt," and that BuOrd was running its own parallel investigation in Chesapeake Bay that reached the same diagnosis. [Milford 1996; Rowland and Boyd confirms the parallel Chesapeake Bay armor-plate firings]

**10. A revisionist case exists and should be acknowledged, carefully.**
Victor S. Alpher, writing in *The Submarine Review* in 2010 from Record Group 74 material declassified in December 1996, argues BuOrd was simultaneously developing the Mark 9 and Mark 10 exploder mechanisms and Mark 6 Mods 5 and 6 with electronic detonators, and that "BuOrd can neither be said to have been unresponsive nor bumbling in reaction to the Mark 14 debacle as is often implied." **Treat as a minority position by a credentialed author, and note that the same article contains clear errors** (it claims to have found "no specific reports of Mark 18s fired during the war in combat," which is plainly wrong, and calls Christie the inventor of the Mark 6, which overstates his role). **Do not build a beat on Alpher alone.**

**11. "Bounced off Japanese hulls with a clang."**
The audible thud of a dud striking a hull is a real and repeatedly retold detail of the Tinosa attack, but the versions in circulation trace to retellings rather than to the patrol report text. The official history's plainer version is safe: 15 fired, about 12 hits claimed, "yet the dream target drifted on." **If the script uses the sound, attribute it to the crew's account rather than asserting it as record.**

**12. The 900 foot drop test.**
The ibiblio transcription of the official BuOrd history says the Pearl Harbor drop tests were from "a height of 900 feet." Bowling says 90 feet, and 90 feet is the figure that produces an impact speed matching the Mark 14's 46 knots. **Use 90 feet.**

## Memoir-attributed or single-sourced claims (writer must hedge these in narration)

- **"Enlisted torpedomen were not told the Mark 6 existed."** The official history supports the broader claim ("the fleet for which the weapon was designed did not even know of its existence"; Newport's own assembly workers "were allowed only the vaguest idea about their project") and Milford supports the operational version ("draconian security, which in some cases even excluded the operating forces from full knowledge of the weapons they were expected to use"). The **specific** version, that when the exploder was issued in mid-1941 only COs and torpedo officers were briefed while enlisted torpedomen were left in the dark, traces to a USNI *Naval History* article of December 1992 that I could not retrieve directly, and to Anthony Newpower's *Iron Men and Tin Fish* (Praeger, 2006) via secondary summaries. **Recommended phrasing: attribute the sharp version to the fleet, e.g. "the Navy kept it so secret that the fleet it was built for was not told it existed," which is the official history's own sentence. Do not assert the specific officer-versus-enlisted split as record without pulling Newpower.**
- **Paint dabs on the exploder screws to prevent tampering.** Appears in search summaries; I could not find it in any Tier 1 source. **Treat as unverified; leave it out or hedge it hard.**
- **Firing pins machined from Japanese aircraft propellers recovered from the Pearl Harbor attack.** Widely repeated. **What is Tier 1 confirmed:** the fix was a **lighter firing pin**, and the material was **aluminum alloy rather than steel**. [Rowland and Boyd: "at Pearl Harbor, the submariners got similar results by lightening the firing pin"; Milford 1996: "It was a simple solution to make aluminum alloy (rather than steel) firing pin blocks"] **What is NOT Tier 1 confirmed:** that the aluminum specifically came from downed Japanese aircraft propellers or a melted-down Japanese fighter engine, or from Hickam Field. This traces to Blair and to popular retellings. **Recommended narration: "They machined the new pins out of aluminum, and the story in the fleet was that the metal came from the propellers of Japanese planes shot down over Pearl Harbor." That is honest and keeps the beat.**
- **Christie's diary quote ("We are licked")** is a participant's private diary, quoted by Alpher. Attribute it as a diary entry.
- **The Grouper patrol report quote** comes to us through Bowling and should be attributed to him.
- **Nimitz's reply to BuOrd's "why"** comes through Bowling, quoting the exchange. Attribute rather than voice as omniscient narration.
- **O'Kane's *Clear the Bridge!*** is the source for most of the moment-by-moment Tang detail in circulation, including the 0230 timing and the fishtail maneuver. The bare facts of the circular run, 78 dead, 9 survivors, and the escape numbers are independently in the official *US Submarine Losses* volume. **Anything beyond that is memoir.**
- **Lockwood's own memoirs (*Sink 'Em All*, *Down to the Sea in Ships*)** are the origin of much of the heroic framing. Milford explicitly warns that the operating forces "tended to exaggerate their contributions to the solution of the problems and deprecate those of NTS-Newport."

## Gaps

- **NOT FOUND: a dated, quotable BuOrd document stating that submariners were aiming or shooting incorrectly.** The blame posture is documented by NHHC and by the Bureau's own history, but no verbatim BuOrd sentence to that effect was located. Do not fabricate one.
- **NOT FOUND: an authoritative total of torpedoes wasted to the three defects.** Only NHHC's "over 800 torpedoes" fired by Pacific Fleet submarines by mid-1942 is solid, and that is torpedoes fired, not torpedoes wasted.
- **NOT FOUND at Tier 1: the exact torpedo/hit/dud split of the Tinosa attack from the patrol report itself.** I could not retrieve the patrol report text; the Submarine Force Library and Museum Association's article on the attack is behind a bot check. The official BuOrd history's "15 fired, 12 known hits claimed, only the first 2 spreads produced explosions" is the best available anchor.
- **NOT FOUND at Tier 1: the 18 July 1942 Saury follow-up net test.** It appears only in Australian naval-history-society material. The 20 June 1942 Skipjack test and the "repeated the tests" follow-up are well supported; the specific Saury date is not.
- **NOT VERIFIED: the aluminum for the new firing pins coming from Japanese aircraft wreckage.** See above.
- **NOT VERIFIED: paint on the exploder screws.**
- **UNRESOLVED CONFLICT (documented above, do not average): the Nimitz order date, 24 June 1943 versus the official history's July 24.** Recommend 24 June.
- **UNRESOLVED CONFLICT: when the Southwest Pacific command gave up the magnetic exploder.** December 1943 (Milford), 20 January 1944 (Blair via Wikipedia, ordered by Kinkaid), March 1944 (Bowling), spring 1944 (Rowland and Boyd). Recommend "over the winter of 1943 to 1944."
- **UNRESOLVED CONFLICT: the 1926 test result, one shot that sank the hulk (Rowland and Boyd) versus two shots, the first a miss underneath (Wildenberg and Polmar; Alpher).** Recommend the two-shot version, with the disagreement noted.
- **UNRESOLVED: Tang's loss date, 24 October (official losses volume) versus 25 October (NHHC H-gram, O'Kane).** Recommend "the night of 24 October 1944."
- **I did not obtain Clay Blair's *Silent Victory* or Rowland and Boyd in print.** Rowland and Boyd was read in full via the HyperWar transcription at ibiblio, which contains obvious OCR errors (including the "900 feet" figure and several garbled words). **A QA pass against a clean copy of Rowland and Boyd is advisable before any direct quotation from it goes into narration.**
