# Research Report: Midway, the Five Fateful Minutes (Part A: the battle and the record)

## One-paragraph summary

The popular image of 4 June 1942 is wrong in a specific, documentable way. The story that American dive bombers caught four Japanese carriers with flight decks packed full of fueled and armed strike aircraft, five minutes from launching a war-winning counterstrike, originates with Mitsuo Fuchida's 1951 book and has no support in the Japanese operational record. The Japanese air group records (kodochosho), the official Japanese war history Senshi Sosho, and photographs taken by attacking B-17s all show the opposite: Kido Butai's anti-ship strike was still down in the hangars, the flight decks held mostly combat air patrol fighters, and Nagumo was not five minutes from launching but at least forty-five, because spotting and launching a deckload physically takes that long and his decks had been occupied all morning cycling defensive fighters against a nearly continuous stream of American attacks. Critically, this correction does not make the carriers less doomed, it explains why they died so fast. Aircraft in an enclosed Japanese hangar, fueled, with ordnance stacked loose rather than struck below and with avgas lines charged, is the single worst place to absorb a 1,000-pound bomb. The US Navy's own director of naval history, Samuel J. Cox at NHHC, states the corrected version flatly and credits Parshall and Tully for the numbers he uses, so this is not a fringe revision, it is the current official American position.

## The core correction: what was actually on those decks

This is the episode. It holds up, and it holds up from more than one direction.

### What the popular version claims
American dive bombers arrived at 1020 to find four Japanese flight decks jammed with fueled, armed strike aircraft, engines turning, five minutes from launching a strike that would have destroyed the American carriers. The image comes from Fuchida's *Midway: The Battle that Doomed Japan*, published in Japan in 1951 and in the United States in 1955, and was amplified by the 1976 film.

### The documentary basis for the correction

Parshall and Tully rebuilt the Japanese side from three source classes that earlier English-language accounts did not use systematically:

1. **The carrier air group records, the kodochosho.** The ships' logs were destroyed after the war, but the air group records survived. These record every launch and recovery with times, which lets you reconstruct what the flight deck was physically doing at any given moment.
2. **Senshi Sosho**, the official Japanese war history compiled by the War History Section of the Japanese Defense Agency. The Midway volume, *Midowei Kaisen*, was published in **1971**. Parshall and Tully note that Fuchida's account has been overturned in Japan for more than twenty years while remaining authoritative in the West.
3. **A technical understanding of Japanese carrier design and doctrine**, drawing on Evans and Peattie's *Kaigun* (1997) and Peattie's *Sunburst*.

For contrast, the three sources that formed the backbone of every prior Western account were the captured Nagumo Report (taken on Saipan in 1944), the postwar USSBS interviews, and Fuchida's book. [All of the above from the published *Shattered Sword* Introduction, loaded as PDF]

### The evidence itself

**A. Senshi Sosho says it outright.** Parshall, Dickson and Tully report that the official Japanese history explicitly states that at the time of the attack every Japanese carrier had its attack aircraft in the hangars, and the only aircraft on deck were CAP fighters, or in *Soryu*'s case strike-force escort fighters being launched to reinforce the CAP. [*Naval War College Review*, Vol. 54 No. 3, Summer 2001, loaded via Wayback]

**B. The B-17 photographs.** Between 0800 and 0820, while Nagumo was making his most consequential decisions, attacking B-17s photographed the formation. The images of *Soryu*, *Hiryu* and *Akagi* show no strike planes on deck, only a handful of fighters. This is physical evidence, not testimony. [NWC Review, loaded]

**C. The spotting arithmetic.** Parshall, Dickson and Tully break the spotting cycle into timed tasks, sourced to Japanese material:
- Bringing aircraft up from the hangar: sequential, about **one minute per plane**
- Spotting, unfolding wings, chocking wheels: mostly parallel, but **five sequential minutes** added
- Warming up engines: sequential, **fifteen minutes minimum for the entire force**
- Arming dive bombers: largely concurrent with warm-up, but **up to twenty minutes**
- Final pilot briefings: **five sequential minutes minimum**
- Crews to planes and preflight: **five minutes**
- The launch itself: **fifteen to thirty seconds per plane**

Their conclusion: spotting a **twenty-one-plane strike** takes around **forty minutes**, plus **five to ten minutes** to launch, plus another **five to ten minutes** if the spot includes dive bombers that must be armed on deck. Therefore Nagumo needed an unbroken **forty-five-minute window on all four flight decks at once**. [NWC Review, loaded]

**D. The *Akagi* CAP log shows he never got that window.** The full 4 June sequence appears in the timeline above. The load-bearing entry is **1010, recover three CAP fighters**, fifteen minutes before *Akagi* was fatally bombed at 1025. Fifteen minutes is not enough to bring a twenty-plane strike up, spot it, brief the pilots and warm the engines. And recovery operations require a clear flight deck aft, so aircraft cannot be spotted aft while other aircraft are landing, nor generally during fighter takeoffs. Every CAP launch and recovery all morning was a wall against spotting a strike. [NWC Review, loaded]

**E. Japanese carrier practice put the aircraft in the hangar in the first place.** Unlike the US Navy, the Japanese did not use permanent deck parks. Temporary deck parks existed only as transitional overflow forward of the crash barrier during recovery. Most fueling happened in the hangars, and except for arming dive bombers, aircraft were serviced in the hangars. So the conventional assumption that the anti-ship strike was brought up and spotted immediately after Tomonaga launched runs contrary to normal Japanese operating pattern. [NWC Review, loaded]

### The bottom line as the authors state it

At the moment *Akagi*, *Kaga* and *Soryu* were struck, their flight decks were **more empty than occupied**. There were aircraft on deck, but most were CAP fighters, not attack aircraft, and there were fewer aircraft on deck than is generally supposed. The authors are careful to frame this not as a revision but as a restatement of a record that official Japanese sources had already carried for about thirty years. [NWC Review, loaded]

### Independent American official confirmation

The US Navy's own history command says the same thing without hedging. NHHC states that contrary to most early accounts the Japanese flight decks were not packed with aircraft about to take off, that the strike aircraft were still below in the hangars, that fighters were cycling on and off the flight deck to deal with the stream of torpedo bomber raids, that the hangar aircraft were fully fueled, and that aboard *Kaga* and *Akagi* some torpedo bombers carried torpedoes and some still had bombs. NHHC also states that at 1020 the Japanese were still at least 45 minutes from launching a full counterstrike, the same position they had been in at 0700, and that this was 45 minutes and not five minutes as in early accounts. [NHHC H-006-1 and H-006-4, loaded]

**Caveat to state honestly**: NHHC credits *Shattered Sword* as its source for casualty numbers, and Cox names it as probably the most accurate book on Midway he has read. So NHHC is a very high-authority endorsement, but on some figures it is a downstream user of Parshall and Tully rather than a fully independent check. The *independent* legs of the argument are Senshi Sosho (1971, predating Parshall and Tully by decades) and the B-17 photographs.

### One nuance the writer must not flatten

"Empty decks" is not the claim, and overstating it would be its own myth. There **were** aircraft on the flight decks: CAP fighters, and on *Soryu*, escort fighters being launched. The *Soryu* TROM describes explosions among fueled and armed aircraft on both flight and hangar decks. The precise, defensible claim is: **no full strike was spotted, the attack aircraft were in the hangars, and the aircraft on deck were predominantly fighters.**

## Why the carriers died: hangars, ordnance, fuel, damage control

This is the payoff. The corrected picture makes the carriers *more* vulnerable, not less.

**1. Japanese hangars were fully enclosed. American hangars were not.**
Parshall, Dickson and Tully: US carrier hangars could be opened to the elements by rolling up metal screens along their sides. Japanese hangars were fully enclosed by storerooms, workshops and crew spaces, with no natural ventilation. [NWC Review, loaded] NHHC calls this a major design flaw, noting each Japanese carrier had an upper and a lower hangar deck, both enclosed. [NHHC H-006-3, loaded] The consequence: a bomb detonating inside a sealed steel box full of avgas vapor produces overpressure that has nowhere to go. An American open-sided hangar vents.

**2. Fueling happened in the hangar, and engines could not be warmed there.**
Because the hangars were sealed, Japanese aircraft engines were never warmed up below, in direct contrast to American practice where aircraft were commonly warmed in the hangar, brought up and launched immediately. Meanwhile most Japanese fueling *did* occur in the hangar. So at 1020 the hangars held fueled aircraft and charged fuel lines. [NWC Review, loaded]

**3. Ordnance was stacked loose in the hangars rather than struck below.**
This is the direct consequence of the rearm-and-counter-rearm. The *Kaga* TROM describes explosions among armed and fueled torpedo planes on the hangar decks, aggravated by unstored ordnance, starting a raging aviation gasoline fire. [combinedfleet.com *Kaga* TROM, loaded] NHHC's version: hangar aircraft fully fueled, with some torpedo bombers carrying torpedoes and some still carrying bombs. [NHHC H-006-4, loaded]

**4. Japanese damage control equipment and doctrine were inadequate to the situation.**
- *Kaga*: a single water main supplying firefighting water was destroyed when bombs exploded near it. The plan for hangar fires depended on pulling heavy fire curtains to isolate compartments, which is useless when fires break out in several places at once. Bomb hits also knocked out the fire suppression system and killed most of the damage control personnel. [search-confirmed only, via Pacific War Online Encyclopedia and related; **corroborate before airing specific mechanical detail**]
- Decapitation compounded it. One of the first bombs to hit *Kaga* was a direct hit on the bridge, killing Captain Jisaku Okada along with the executive officer, navigator and gunnery officer, which destroyed the ship's ability to coordinate firefighting. [NHHC H-006-4, loaded; combinedfleet.com *Kaga* TROM, loaded]
- *Soryu*'s dead included **242 engineers**. [combinedfleet.com *Soryu* TROM, loaded]

**5. The American contrast is documented in a primary source.**
*Yorktown*'s own action report states that when radar detected the incoming attack, plane fueling was stopped, an 8,000-gallon auxiliary avgas tank on the stern was dropped over the side, **fuel lines were drained and filled with CO2 at 20 pounds pressure**, and the gasoline tank compartments had already been filled with CO2 and secured. The report further states the belief that surrounding the gasoline tanks with CO2 prevented ignition when a bomb exploded on the fourth deck adjacent to the forward gasoline stowage. *Yorktown* did this twice on 4 June, draining and re-securing the system a second time at about 1550 when the next raid appeared. [USS *Yorktown* Action Report, HyperWar/ibiblio, loaded]

This is the cleanest possible A/B comparison for the episode: same day, same battle, bombs into a carrier. One navy had purged its fuel lines with inert gas as a matter of hard-won routine after losing *Lexington* at Coral Sea. The other had fueled aircraft and loose ordnance sitting in a sealed hangar.

**6. Japan did not learn the lesson, because they covered up the defeat.**
NHHC notes that the need to conceal the result caused many lessons to be lost. The new carrier *Taiho* incorporated an armored flight deck but not the damage control lessons, and was later destroyed by the same kind of aviation fuel line rupture that had doomed *Lexington* at Coral Sea and contributed to the four losses at Midway. [NHHC H-006-4, loaded]

## The torpedo squadrons

**VT-8** (*Hornet*), LCDR John C. Waldron. 15 TBD Devastators, 30 pilots and gunners. All 15 aircraft shot down, entirely by Japanese fighters, none by shipboard AA. **One survivor: Ensign George Gay.** Waldron had briefed that if only one plane were left, that man should go in and get a hit; not one pilot deviated from the attack course. [NHHC H-006-1 and H-006-3, loaded]

**VT-6** (*Enterprise*), LCDR Eugene Lindsey. 14 TBDs. Lindsey shot down by nine freshly launched Zeros just as VT-6 was closing the hammer and anvil on *Kaga*. Nine TBDs shot down, five torpedoes missed, VT-6 shot down one Zero. Four TBDs recovered aboard *Enterprise*; one ditched and its two-man crew drifted **17 days** before rescue. [NHHC H-006-3, loaded]

**VT-3** (*Yorktown*), LCDR Lance Massey. 12 TBDs, and unlike the others it **had** fighter escort, six Wildcats under Jimmy Thach. It made no difference. Massey was among the first shot down. **10 TBDs lost**, no hits, and one Zero downed by *Hiryu*'s own AA fire. Only one man initially survived: Ensign Wesley Osmus, who was then captured and killed. Thach, fighting for survival against roughly 30 Zeros, improvised the beam defense maneuver that became the Thach Weave, and shot down three Zeros. [NHHC H-006-3, loaded; DANFS *Osmus* (DE-701), loaded]

**Also flying torpedoes that morning**: 6 TBFs of the VT-8 Midway detachment under Lt. Langston Fieberling, and 4 USAAF B-26s under Capt. James Collins. **16 of the 18 TBF aircrew were killed.** Ensign Albert "Bert" Earnest brought his aircraft home with the instrument panel, hydraulics and control surfaces shot away, his turret gunner dead and his tunnel gunner unconscious, flying on trim tab alone, hit by nine 20mm rounds and at least 69 7.7mm rounds. He received two Navy Crosses. The Midway B-26 mission was the first and last time US Air Force aircraft ever attempted a torpedo attack. [NHHC H-006-3 and H-072-1, loaded]

### George Gay: what is reliable and what is not

**Reliable and verifiable**: Gay flew the last VT-8 aircraft, attacked *Soryu*, missed, was shot down by five Zeros, was wounded by two spent machine gun rounds and a 20mm flash burn to his left leg, hid under a seat cushion to avoid being picked up by a passing Japanese destroyer, and was rescued by a PBY roughly 30 hours later on the afternoon of 5 June. His own NHHC interview corrects a distortion that had already grown around him: he saw five Zeros shot down by the squadron's combined fire, which had been misreported as his own gunner shooting down five. [NHHC "Recollections of Lieutenant George Gay," loaded; NHHC H-006-1 and H-006-3, loaded]

**Not reliable, and demonstrably wrong against the Japanese record**: Gay's account of what he watched from the water. In the NHHC interview he says *Akagi* sank just after dark on 4 June after Japanese cruisers raked her with fire, and that *Kaga* and *Soryu* burned all night and were finished off the following morning by American submarines. The Japanese records show *Soryu* was scuttled by *Isokaze* at about 1913 and *Kaga* by *Hagikaze* at about 1925 on 4 June, and *Akagi* by four destroyers at about 0520 on 5 June. No American submarine sank any of them. [Compare NHHC Gay interview, loaded, against combinedfleet.com TROMs, loaded, and NHHC H-006-4, loaded]

**Gay is also, importantly, a source of the packed-deck image.** In the same interview he describes flying down the length of a carrier deck where aircraft were being rearmed and refueled, with gas hoses scattered everywhere. He attacked at about 0920, an hour before the dive bombers, and Japanese practice put refueling in the hangars. [NHHC Gay interview, loaded; NWC Review on hangar fueling, loaded]

Independent scholarly assessment agrees that Gay's contemporaneous statements are the reliable ones and that his later versions of what he saw are controversial among Midway historians, noting he had become a national hero, toured the country and appeared on the cover of *Life* between the battle and his later accounts. [USNI *Naval History*, June 2017, "Torpedo Eight's Celebrated Survivor," **search-confirmed only, USNI blocked my fetch**]

**Treat this gently on air.** The man was burned, bleeding, half-blinded by salt water, and alone in the ocean for thirty hours. Getting the ship names wrong is not dishonesty.

### Does "the torpedo squadrons pulled the CAP down and cleared the way" hold up?

**No, not in that form.** Parshall and Tully list it as one of the eight myths: that VT-8's sacrifice was not in vain because it pulled the Japanese CAP down to sea level and thereby allowed the dive bombers to attack at 1020. [*Shattered Sword* Introduction, loaded]

Their reasoning as reported: the Zero's performance meant the CAP could have regained altitude within minutes; the real causes of the failed intercept were poor Japanese fighter discipline and the absence of any real fighter direction system. [search-confirmed only]

NHHC's corroborating detail on the mechanism: the Japanese had no radar, unreliable aircraft radios and essentially no shipboard fighter direction, so once airborne the fighters were on their own and had a strong tendency toward what Cox describes using the Japanese term for little kids' soccer, everyone chasing the ball. The dive bombers went unseen because of cloud cover, because attention was fixed on the low-altitude fight, and because the CAP was strung out horizontally. Roughly 45 Zeros were airborne and enough could have reached altitude. [NHHC H-006-4, loaded]

**But the torpedo squadrons did matter, just by a different mechanism, and the episode should say so.** NHHC states plainly that the sacrifice was not in vain: the successive attacks, strung out over two and a half hours and including the Marine dive bombers and the B-17s, forced the Japanese carriers to launch and recover fighters continuously while maneuvering violently, and the result was that the carriers could not spot their decks for a counterstrike. VT-6's contribution is described in exactly these terms. [NHHC H-006-1 and H-006-3, loaded]

So the correction is not "their deaths were pointless." It is "their deaths mattered for a reason nobody put on a movie poster." That is a better beat than the myth.

## Wade McClusky and the destroyer *Arashi*

**Verified.** At 0955, with fuel critical and facing a choice between turning back to *Enterprise* or diverting to Midway, McClusky sighted a lone Japanese ship transiting northeast at high speed. He assumed it was trying to rejoin the task force. It was the destroyer *Arashi*, left behind to depth-charge USS *Nautilus*. NHHC states this in two separate H-grams and calls *Arashi*'s high-speed transit fateful. [NHHC H-006-1, H-006-3 and H-006-4, loaded]

**Scholarly qualifications to note:**
1. McClusky was only in that position because Nagumo had aggressively turned northeast at the first report of US surface ships, so McClusky overshot to the south. The chain of luck runs back through a Japanese decision. [NHHC H-006-4, loaded]
2. *Nautilus* was itself only there because LCDR William Brockman had been drawn by smoke from the earlier Midway-based attacks. [NHHC H-006-3, loaded]
3. NHHC also documents that McClusky then made a doctrinal error, directing the lead squadron onto the near target so that both squadrons dove on *Kaga* and none on *Akagi*. Cox flags a live historiographical dispute over whether McClusky or Best caused the confusion, and dryly notes that since Best is the hero of the 2019 film, McClusky takes the fall. Only Best's break to *Akagi* fixed it. [NHHC H-006-4, loaded]
4. NHHC states that had *Akagi* survived, her air group alone had enough combat power to have seriously damaged or sunk all three US carriers. [NHHC H-006-4, loaded]

## The codebreaking setup

**Verified, with a correction the episode should use.**

Station Hypo was the shorthand for the Combat Intelligence Unit, in the basement of the 14th Naval District headquarters at Pearl Harbor. Rochefort was officer in charge. He was **not** the codebreaker; the senior codebreaker at Hypo was LCDR Carter Ham. Neither Rochefort nor Layton was formally an intelligence officer, because no such specialty existed. [NHHC H-006-2, loaded]

**Scale of what codebreaking actually delivered**: at best the US was intercepting about **60 percent** of Japanese naval traffic, analyzing about **40 percent**, and actually breaking and reading only **10 to 15 percent**, often only fragments. JN-25B changed to JN-25C a week before Midway. Nimitz did not have a copy of the Japanese operations order. [NHHC H-006-2, loaded]

**The AF fresh water ruse, exactly as documented**: the idea came from **Lt. Jasper Holmes**, one of Rochefort's staff. Rochefort had a message sent **via secure underwater cable** to Midway instructing the base to broadcast a phony message **in the clear** saying its fresh water making capability was broken. The Japanese intercepted it, retransmitted the information, and Hypo intercepted and broke that, confirming AF meant Midway. [NHHC H-006-2, loaded; National WWII Museum, loaded, independently]

**The correction**: NHHC states that contrary to many books and movies, the AF gambit was run to convince **Washington**, not Nimitz. Nimitz and Layton were already convinced. Washington's OP-20G and War Plans, analyzing the same intelligence, were not, and some there dismissed the result as too good to be true or a Japanese deception. King came around only after ordering an assessment directly from Rochefort. [NHHC H-006-2, loaded]

## Absurd / ironic beats (all real, all sourced)

1. **The Japanese wargamed this exact defeat and cancelled it.** In the pre-battle war games, the Japanese officer playing the American side did essentially what Nimitz did, with results remarkably close to what actually happened. The umpire ruled his actions impossible and the Japanese losses were resurrected. [NHHC H-006-1, loaded]
2. **The order and the counter-order.** 0715, rearm 107 aircraft with land bombs. 0745, prepare to attack fleet units, leave torpedoes on whatever has not been changed yet. 0820, stop, put the torpedoes back. [NHHC H-006-1 and H-006-3, loaded]
3. **The scout plane that launched late, flew short, and buried the lede.** *Tone* No. 4 launched late for reasons still unknown, cut its prescribed route short, and its 0740 report said ten enemy surface ships without mentioning whether a carrier was present. Nagumo's demand for clarification was so urgent it went out **in the clear** and was intercepted by American radio intelligence. [NHHC H-006-2 and H-006-3, loaded]
4. **The late launch was a lucky break.** NHHC states that had *Tone* No. 4 launched on time and flown the prescribed route, it would have **completely missed** the American task groups. Meanwhile *Chikuma*'s No. 1 scout launched on time, flew the correct line, and saw nothing at 0615 when it should have seen TF-17. [NHHC H-006-2, loaded]
5. **Yorktown's three months in three days.** The shipyard estimated about three months of overhaul. Nimitz gave them 72 hours. More than 1,400 tradesmen worked around the clock, and Oahu ran rolling blackouts across the island to supply electricity for the welding. She sailed on the morning of 30 May with shipyard workers still aboard. [NHHC *Yorktown* page, loaded; DVIDS/Pearl Harbor Naval Shipyard, loaded]
6. **The *Hornet* Air Group's perfect score.** Except for Waldron's squadron, which was annihilated, none of *Hornet*'s morning strike even sighted the enemy. All ten VF-8 fighters ran out of fuel and ditched, two pilots never recovered. For the whole of 4 June the *Hornet* Air Group scored zero hits. [NHHC H-006-4, loaded]
7. **The bombers that never hit anything but were still useful.** Over the day the B-17s dropped more than 320 bombs on Japanese ships without a single hit, and on 5 June more than 50 dive bombers plus 9 B-17s dropped about 90 bombs on the lone destroyer *Tanikaze* and missed every one. But a wildly maneuvering carrier cannot conduct flight operations, so the misses contributed to the cascading delay. [NHHC H-006-1, H-006-3 and H-006-4, loaded]
8. **The *Nautilus* torpedo that became a flotation device.** Brockman fired four torpedoes at the burning, dead-in-the-water *Kaga*. All malfunctioned. The one that hit failed to explode, and its buoyant after body served as a float for swimming Japanese sailors. [NHHC H-006-1, loaded]
9. **Rochefort's reward.** Recalled to Washington on a pretext, never to return to Hypo, and given command of a floating dry dock. Nimitz's recommendation for a Distinguished Service Medal was denied by King on the recommendation of Rochefort's own Washington chain of command, which then took credit for breaking the code and predicting Midway. He received the medal posthumously from President Reagan in **1985**. [NHHC H-006-2, loaded]
10. **The two headlines.** The *New York Times* banner read that US Army fliers blasted two Japanese fleets at Midway. The *Japan Times* in Tokyo announced an epochal Japanese naval victory. The Army fliers hit nothing. [NHHC H-006-1, loaded]
11. **Japan lied to its own prime minister.** Yamamoto and senior navy leadership told the truth only to Emperor Hirohito. Tojo and the army leadership were kept in the dark, and when the army did find out it largely concluded the navy had it coming. To maintain the deception, nobody was relieved of command. Nagumo kept Kido Butai until after Santa Cruz in October 1942. [NHHC H-006-4, loaded]
12. **The friendly fire on *Hornet*.** A damaged *Yorktown* fighter with a wounded pilot made a hard landing on *Hornet*, and its un-safed machine guns sprayed the flight deck, killing Lt. Royal Ingersoll II, son of the Atlantic Fleet commander in chief, plus four Marines, and wounding 20 others. [NHHC H-006-4, loaded]
13. **The Bruno Gaido story before Midway.** On 1 February 1942, with a damaged Japanese bomber deliberately aiming to crash into *Enterprise*, Gaido jumped from his battle station into the rear seat of a **parked** SBD and opened fire. The bomber's wingtip cut the tail off the aircraft he was sitting in and spun it around. He kept firing. Then he grabbed the plane's fire bottle, put out the burning gasoline on the flight deck, and disappeared into the ship because he was worried about getting in trouble for leaving his post. Halsey had him found and spot-promoted him two grades. [NHHC H-068-2, loaded]

## Human-cost beats (play straight)

**Flagging these explicitly. No jokes anywhere near this section.**

**The torpedo squadrons.** Of the 99 men in the 42 torpedo planes lost, three survived. VT-8 launched 30 men from *Hornet* and one came back. [NHHC H-006-1, loaded]

**Ensign Wesley Frank Osmus, VT-3.** Born 2 September 1918 in Chicago. Flew the last plane in VT-3's formation, was shot down on the run-in, and bailed out of a burning Devastator; his radio-gunner **ARM3c Benjamin R. Dodson** was either already dead or unable to get out. Osmus was picked up by the destroyer *Arashi*, which was running independently to rejoin Kido Butai after the unsuccessful hunt for *Nautilus*. He was injured. He was subjected to duress and it yielded information about the composition of the American carrier task groups. DANFS states that sometime later, most likely on the night of 4 June, he was murdered by his captors. He received a posthumous Navy Cross, and DE-701 was named for him. [DANFS *Osmus* (DE-701), NHHC, loaded; NHHC H-006-3, loaded]

*Additional detail, lower confidence*: accounts naming *Arashi*'s commanding officer, Cdr. Kosaku Ariga, as having ordered the execution, and describing Osmus holding onto the rail and being struck with a fire axe, appear in secondary sources including Wikipedia and forum discussion of the war crimes investigation. **I could not verify this in a Tier 1 source.** Either use the DANFS wording, which is grave enough, or verify further before airing the axe detail.

**Ensign Frank Woodrow O'Flaherty and AMM1c Bruno Peter Gaido, VS-6.** Their SBD had been holed in the wing and was losing fuel after the fight with the Zeros that broke away from *Hiryu*'s strike. They ditched in the open sea and were picked up by the destroyer *Makigumo*. They were interrogated and possibly tortured. Cox's assessment is that they gave up plausible but phony information; neither had ever been to Midway, so neither could have known what was on it. By 15 June the Japanese decided they were no longer of use and orders came to dispose of them. According to Japanese accounts the destroyer's captain asked for volunteers to execute them and the crew initially refused. In the dead of night weights were tied to both and they were thrown over the side to drown. Japanese accounts state both met their end with stoic and dignified defiance. Neither man's fate was known in the United States until after the war. No responsible Japanese officer survived the war, so there was no prosecution. They were declared missing on 4 July 1942 and presumed dead on 5 June 1943. O'Flaherty received a posthumous Navy Cross and had DE-340 named for him. Gaido received a posthumous Distinguished Flying Cross. **No ship has ever been named for Bruno Gaido.** [NHHC H-068-2, loaded]

*Correction the episode can make*: the 2019 film depicts Gaido's death on 4 June. It happened on 15 June. NHHC says so directly. [NHHC H-068-2, loaded]

**Japanese sailors in the burning hangars.** Over 3,000 Japanese sailors died, most of them while trying to save their ships, including over 700 aircraft technicians and maintainers. *Soryu* lost 242 engineers. *Akagi*'s crew fought a nine-hour battle against the fires. [NHHC H-006-1, loaded; combinedfleet.com TROMs, loaded]

**The captains.** Captain Jisaku Okada of *Kaga* was killed on the bridge by one of the first bombs, along with the executive officer, navigator and gunnery officer. Captain Ryusaku Yanagimoto of *Soryu*, highly popular with his crew, refused to leave; his men tried to physically remove him and he stared them down, and he was aboard when she was torpedoed. Captain Taijiro Aoki of *Akagi* also intended to go down with his ship and stayed aboard alone for hours while the scuttling was delayed, until some of his crew went back aboard to talk him out of it, which they finally did. [NHHC H-006-4, loaded]

**Hiryu's last night.** RADM Yamaguchi and Captain Tomeo Kaku elected to go down with the ship after a ceremony on the flight deck with several hundred surviving crewmen in formation, with speeches, toasts and songs, followed by an orderly abandon ship. After she was torpedoed, roughly **30 to 40 men trapped in the engineering spaces made their way topside**, and the destroyer on-scene commander left them behind. Nagumo sent *Tanikaze* back for them and she found nothing. They got into a cutter. **34 of them endured until 17 June**, when they were rescued and taken prisoner by USS *Ballard*. [NHHC H-006-4, loaded]

**Hammann.** Blown in half by one of *I-168*'s torpedoes. Sailors were seen trying to reach the depth charge racks on the stern to disarm them, but she sank too fast. When the depth charges detonated, virtually every man from *Hammann* who was in the water was killed, and the shock broke legs and ankles of the damage control parties working on *Yorktown*. **81 of her 251-man crew** were lost. [NHHC H-006-1 and H-006-4, loaded]

**Yorktown.** The first Japanese dive bomber to attack was hit by the two quad 1.1-inch mounts aft of the island, and released its bomb before breaking up. It hit those exact mounts and killed almost their entire crews, **19 men**. [NHHC H-006-4, loaded]

**What Japan did to its own survivors.** The Imperial Navy went to great lengths to isolate the Midway survivors, especially the wounded, who were treated as disgraced losers. All were barred from writing to or visiting their families and were shipped out to the far reaches of the empire, where the great majority died. [NHHC H-006-4, loaded]

## Myth vs record (corrections)

**1. The five fateful minutes.**
Popular: Japanese flight decks were packed with fueled, armed strike aircraft five minutes from launch.
Actual: The strike aircraft were in the hangars. The decks held mostly CAP fighters and were more empty than occupied. Nagumo was at least 45 minutes from launching, the same position he had been in at 0700. Senshi Sosho has said so since 1971; B-17 photographs from 0800 to 0820 show no strike planes on deck.
[Parshall/Dickson/Tully, *Naval War College Review* 54:3, 2001, loaded; NHHC H-006-1 and H-006-4, loaded; *Shattered Sword* Introduction, loaded]

**2. Fuchida is a reliable witness.**
Popular: Fuchida's *Midway: The Battle that Doomed Japan* is the authoritative Japanese account.
Actual: Parshall and Tully call it irretrievably flawed, and say his errors are not omissions but fundamental and willful distortions bearing on Nagumo's intelligence estimates, his search plan, Japanese flight deck operations and the nature of the dive bomber attack. His account has been overturned in Japan for over twenty years while remaining authoritative in the West. (Part B is covering Fuchida the man; noted here only because it is the source of the deck myth.)
[*Shattered Sword* Introduction, loaded]

**3. The torpedo squadrons pulled the CAP down to sea level and cleared the way.**
Popular: VT-8's annihilation was redeemed because it dragged the Zeros to wave-top height.
Actual: Listed by Parshall and Tully as a myth. The Zero could regain altitude in minutes. The real causes were poor Japanese fighter discipline, no radar, unreliable radios and no fighter direction. **But** the torpedo attacks did matter, by forcing continuous CAP launch and recovery plus violent evasive maneuvering, which is precisely what prevented the Japanese from spotting a strike.
[*Shattered Sword* Introduction, loaded; NHHC H-006-1, H-006-3 and H-006-4, loaded; the Zero-climb reasoning is search-confirmed only]

**4. The late *Tone* No. 4 scout doomed Nagumo.**
Popular: If only the scout had launched on time, Japan wins.
Actual: Listed by Parshall and Tully as a myth. NHHC states that on-time and on-route, *Tone* No. 4 would have missed the American task groups entirely. *Chikuma*'s No. 1 launched on time, flew the right line, and saw nothing anyway.
[*Shattered Sword* Introduction, loaded; NHHC H-006-2, loaded]

**5. The rearming decision lost the battle.**
Popular: If Nagumo had not ordered the switch to land bombs, he could have struck immediately.
Actual: Listed by Parshall and Tully as a myth. Parshall, Dickson and Tully argue the outcome hinged neither on when Nagumo got the *Tone* message nor on how fast the armorers worked, but on what the flight decks were doing. NHHC adds that Nagumo was already in extremis from the moment he launched on Midway without knowing where the US carriers were.
[NWC Review, loaded; *Shattered Sword* Introduction, loaded; NHHC H-006-3, loaded]

**6. Japan lost the cream of its naval aviators at Midway.**
Popular: Midway destroyed Japan's irreplaceable veteran aircrew.
Actual: Listed by Parshall and Tully as a myth. NHHC, using their figures, gives **110 aircrew lost, 36 on the carriers and 74 in the air**, most from *Hiryu*, and states that most Japanese pilots were rescued. The deeper loss was institutional: **over 700 aircraft technicians and maintainers** and, above all, the four carriers themselves. Most Pearl Harbor veteran pilots survived Midway and died later over the Solomons, where Japan lost over 1,000 aircraft.
[*Shattered Sword* Introduction, loaded; NHHC H-006-1, loaded]

**7. The Americans won against overwhelming odds.**
Popular: A miracle, a hopelessly outnumbered underdog victory.
Actual: Listed by Parshall and Tully as a myth. At the point of contact it was 4 Japanese carriers with 248 aircraft against 3 American carriers with 233 aircraft plus 127 on Midway, giving the US 360 aircraft to Japan's 248. NHHC: not a miracle, and in dive bombers, the decisive weapon, the two sides were at rough parity. Japan's enormous overall force commitment was distributed so that nothing but the four carriers could affect the outcome.
[*Shattered Sword* Introduction, loaded; NHHC H-006-1, loaded]

**8. Nagumo was indecisive.**
Popular: He dithered.
Actual: NHHC states he moved aggressively and in accordance with Japanese doctrine, and that his extreme offensive mentality is arguably what cost him the battle. His most damaging move was turning **toward** the reported US ships rather than opening the range.
[NHHC H-006-1 and H-006-3, loaded]

**9. The AF ruse was run to convince Nimitz.**
Popular: Rochefort had to prove AF meant Midway to a skeptical Nimitz.
Actual: Nimitz and Layton were already convinced. The gambit was aimed at Washington, where OP-20G and War Plans were analyzing the same intelligence and refusing to accept the conclusion.
[NHHC H-006-2, loaded]

**10. American submarines finished off the Japanese carriers.**
Popular: This appears in George Gay's own 1943 account.
Actual: All four carriers were scuttled by Japanese destroyers. The single *Nautilus* torpedo that hit *Kaga* failed to explode.
[NHHC H-006-1 and H-006-4, loaded; combinedfleet.com TROMs, loaded; compare NHHC Gay interview, loaded]

**11. Bruno Gaido was executed on 4 June.**
Popular: As depicted in the 2019 film *Midway*.
Actual: O'Flaherty and Gaido were killed on **15 June 1942**, from the destroyer *Makigumo*.
[NHHC H-068-2, loaded]

**12. Torpedo Squadron 8's sacrifice was the whole story of the torpedo attacks.**
Popular: VT-8 alone.
Actual: VT-6 and VT-3 were destroyed in the same way for the same result. NHHC notes VT-3 and VT-6 showed equal valor and simply did not get the publicity. Add the Midway-based TBF and B-26 crews, and the Marine SBD and SB2U crews.
[NHHC H-006-1 and H-006-3, loaded]
