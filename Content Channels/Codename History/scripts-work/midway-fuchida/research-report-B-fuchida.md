# Research Report: Midway, the Five Fateful Minutes (Part B: Fuchida and the myth)

## One-paragraph summary

Mitsuo Fuchida led the air attack on Pearl Harbor on 7 December 1941 and, at Midway six months later, flew nothing at all. He had undergone an emergency appendectomy aboard *Akagi* days before the battle and watched the 4 June dive bomber attack from the carrier's bridge, breaking both ankles during the evacuation. In 1955 the United States Naval Institute published his and Masatake Okumiya's *Midway: The Battle That Doomed Japan*, and simultaneously ran an excerpt in *Proceedings* titled "Five Fateful Minutes at Midway." In it Fuchida wrote that at 1020 Nagumo ordered the launch, that *Akagi*'s decks held a fully spotted strike with engines warming, that "within five minutes all her planes would be launched," and that the Japanese were "caught flatfooted in the most vulnerable condition possible, decks loaded with planes armed and fueled for an attack." That image became the accepted Western account of the most important naval battle in American history for roughly fifty years. It was wrong. Japan's own official war history (*Senshi Sōshō* vol. 43, 1971) and the carriers' air unit records (*kōdōchōshos*) show no attack aircraft spotted on the Japanese flight decks at 1020, only combat air patrol fighters, and *Akagi* was recovering CAP fighters about fifteen minutes before it was bombed, which required a clear deck aft. Jonathan Parshall and Anthony Tully laid this out in *Shattered Sword* (Potomac Books, 2005), built on their earlier *Naval War College Review* piece "Doctrine Matters" (Summer 2001), and Parshall then named three specific Fuchida fabrications in "Reflecting on Fuchida, or 'A Tale of Three Whoppers'" (*Naval War College Review*, Spring 2010, vol. 63 no. 2, pp. 127-138). The correction is now mainstream, but it drew a full published rebuttal from screenwriter Martin Bennett (*NWCR* Winter 2013, vol. 66 no. 1, pp. 110-125) and a reply from Parshall (*NWCR* Spring 2013, "In My View," pp. 136-138), and some parts of the case (especially the *Missouri* claim) rest on absence of evidence rather than positive disproof. That distinction matters and the script must keep it.

## The account: what Fuchida actually claimed

The load-bearing passage. This is the exact text from **"Five Fateful Minutes at Midway," *U.S. Naval Institute Proceedings*, June 1955, vol. 81/6/628**, which I loaded directly, and it matches the book text Parshall quotes (*Midway*, pp. 155-56):

> "Preparations for a counter-strike against the enemy had continued on board our four carriers throughout the enemy torpedo attacks. One after another, planes were hoisted from the hangar and quickly arranged on the flight deck. There was no time to lose. At 1020 Admiral Nagumo gave the order to launch when ready. On *Akagi*'s flight deck all planes were in position with engines warming up. The big ship began turning into the wind. Within five minutes all her planes would be launched. **Five minutes! Who would have dreamed that the tide of battle would shift completely in that brief interval of time?** ... At 1024 the order to start launching came from the bridge by voice-tube. The Air Officer flapped a white flag, and the first Zero fighter gathered speed and whizzed off the deck. At that instant a lookout screamed: 'Hell-divers!' I looked up to see three black enemy planes plummeting toward our ship."

And the sentence that did the real damage, a few paragraphs later:

> "**We had been caught flatfooted in the most vulnerable condition possible, decks loaded with planes armed and fueled for an attack.**"

Then his own account of his injuries, which is worth having verbatim because the script will use it:

> "In my condition this was no easy task. Helped by some sailors, I managed to get out of the bridge window and slid down the already smoldering rope to the gun deck. There I was still ten feet above the flight deck. The connecting monkey ladder was red hot, as was the iron plate on which I stood. There was nothing to do but jump, which I did. At the same moment another explosion occurred in the hangar, and the resultant blast sent me sprawling ... Returning to consciousness, I struggled to rise to my feet, but **both of my ankles were broken**. Crewmen finally came to my assistance and took me to the anchor deck ... There I was strapped into a bamboo stretcher and lowered to a boat."

Three things to note for the script. First, "in my condition" is the only trace of the appendectomy in the passage. He does not explain it there. Second, he places himself **on the bridge**, not in a sickbay, at 1024. Third, the "both of my ankles were broken" detail comes from Fuchida himself, so it is his testimony, not an independent finding. No historian I found disputes the broken ankles.

The USNI framing also matters: the *Proceedings* piece carried a note reading "This article is an excerpt from the book ... published this month by the U.S. Naval Institute." The professional journal of the American naval officer corps ran the excerpt as a promotional launch for its own book.

## The three whoppers (Parshall), each explained and sourced

All three are named by Parshall himself in **"Reflecting on Fuchida, or 'A Tale of Three Whoppers,'" *Naval War College Review*, Spring 2010, vol. 63 no. 2, art. 10, pp. 127-138** (full PDF loaded). His framing statement, up front:

> "It is doubtful that any one person has had a more deleterious long-term impact on the study of the Pacific War than Mitsuo Fuchida. Because of his misstatements, the American study of the Japanese side of such battles as Pearl Harbor and Midway (particularly the latter) was probably set back by decades. His untruths also demonstrate the tremendous power of **self-serving ideas that may be wrong, but subtly support national self-images**, particularly when carried forward by the mass media."

And, disarmingly: "It is true that in this article I hope to bury Fuchida, not to praise him. Yet it is equally true that as a student of the battle I would have loved to have had a beer with him, too."

### Whopper 1: "The Tale of the Missing Tank-Farm Attack"

**The claim.** Quoted in Prange's *At Dawn We Slept* (p. 541), Fuchida said that on his return flight to *Akagi* on 7 December he "mentally earmarked for destruction the fuel-tank farms, the vast repair and maintenance facilities, and perhaps a ship or two bypassed that morning for priority targets," and that on landing he pressed vigorously for a follow-up strike at those targets, becoming "bitter and angry" when Nagumo turned for home. Dramatized in *Tora! Tora! Tora!* (1970).

**Parshall's verdict:** "In fact, it would appear that none of these events ever took place."

**The evidence, four strands, all sourced in Parshall's notes:**
1. Willmott, Tohmatsu and Johnson (*Pearl Harbor*, 2001, p. 64) establish the actual Japanese target priority list: land-based airpower, aircraft carriers, battleships/cruisers/other warships, merchant shipping, port facilities, land installations. Fuel tanks were at the bottom. Parshall: "had he actually done so, this would have represented a complete renunciation of all his prior naval training and indoctrination."
2. Kusaka, Nagumo's chief of staff, makes no mention of Fuchida's protestations in his own postwar account, and dismisses the later fuel-tank criticism as "afterthoughts of poor strategists" (*The Pearl Harbor Papers*, p. 162).
3. **Genda explicitly denied it.** He acknowledged in his memoirs that he was aware of the *Tora! Tora! Tora!* scene "but explicitly denied that such an incident had actually taken place or that any such proposal had been put forward by Fuchida."
4. In his 1945 American interrogation Fuchida was asked point-blank why there had been no follow-up attack, and answered that the degradation of American airpower on Oahu was unknown, and that eight damaged or destroyed battleships constituted success. **He made no mention of the fuel tanks.** By 1963 he was telling Prange a very different story.

I independently loaded **USSBS Interrogation Nav No. 6, Tokyo, 18 October 1945**, in which Fuchida describes his Pearl Harbor status simply as "I was an air observer" and there is no mention of fuel tanks, a third wave, or any argument with Nagumo. This corroborates the pattern even though it is not the same interrogation Willmott cites.

Parshall's closing line on this one is the most quotable in the article: **"Fuchida was never slow to detect the sort of tales his audiences liked to hear."**

He also documents a *later* mutation, from a Battle of Midway Roundtable post in 2009 relaying secondhand that Fuchida had privately said the Japanese did not bomb the oil tanks "because they planned to use them after they invaded Oahu." Parshall calls this "a truly incredible misstatement," citing John Stephan's *Hawaii under the Rising Sun* to show there were no concrete invasion plans, and noting that "a mere air group commander like Fuchida almost certainly would not have been privy to their details on 7 December."

### Whopper 2: "The Tale of the Fallacious Five Minutes"

**The claim.** The passage quoted in full above.

**Parshall's verdict:** "**Thus, Fuchida's entire rendition of the climax of the most important naval battle in American history was a lie. The Japanese were nowhere near ready to counterattack at this time.**"

**The evidence:**
- Japanese carriers were attacked no fewer than five times by nine separate American aircraft groups that morning, keeping flight decks occupied with combat air patrol cycling. Spotting a strike took around forty-five minutes.
- *Akagi*'s own air group records show it was **recovering CAP fighters about fifteen minutes before it was bombed**. Recovery requires an empty deck aft, which means no reserve strike was spotted.
- **"The official Japanese war history on the battle, *Senshi Sōshō*, explicitly states that at the time of the American attack there were no attack aircraft on the Japanese flight decks, only combat air patrol fighters."**
- The single Zero "whizzing off *Akagi*'s flight deck" in Fuchida's set-piece can be shown from *Akagi*'s own air group records to have been a **CAP fighter** launched to fight off the ongoing American attacks. Parshall: "**We even know the pilot's name.**"

Parshall's assessment of the damage: "it essentially paralyzed the American study of this pivotal battle for the better part of fifty years. Fuchida's tale was in English, while the operational records that belied it were in handwritten Japanese stored on microfilms." And: "**Intriguingly, Fuchida's reputation as a reliable witness was demolished in Japan as soon as the *Senshi Sōshō* volume on the battle came out in 1971.**" Also: "his word was accepted essentially as holy writ until 2005."

The technical foundation is in the earlier article, **Parshall, Dickson and Tully, "Doctrine Matters," *NWCR* Summer 2001** (loaded), which states it plainly:

> "To put the matter succinctly, at the time *Akagi*, *Kaga*, and *Soryu* were struck, **their flight decks were more empty than occupied**. This is almost the reverse of the standard view, which has the Japanese flight decks packed with strike aircraft awaiting takeoff. There were aircraft on deck, but most were CAP fighters, not attack aircraft."

and

> "*Akagi*'s 1010 CAP recovery dictates that there cannot have been many strike planes on its deck when it was fatally bombed at 1025. Fifteen minutes would not have been sufficient time to bring its twenty-plane strike to the flight deck, let alone spot them, brief the pilots, and warm up engines. Thus, **the common belief that the American dive-bomber attack found the Japanese flight decks practically chock-a-block with strike planes revved up and waiting to take off is clearly untrue.**"

That article also contains the line the channel should probably borrow for its tone, because it is the debunkers' own restraint: "**Though potentially startling, this is less a 'revision' than a correction and careful restating of the existing historical record.**" And: "official Japanese sources on the battle have been aware of this for some thirty years."

### Whopper 3: "The Tale of the Privileged Observer"

**The claim.** In Prange's *God's Samurai* (pp. 174-75), Fuchida says he arranged transport for the Japanese surrender delegation and went out with a group of army and navy liaison officers, watching the 2 September 1945 ceremony aboard USS *Missouri* from an upper deck. In his memoir *For That One Day*: "Since I was not an official attaché, I was watching the signing ceremony from the upper deck along with the crews of the USS *Missouri*."

**Parshall's verdict:** "**Thus, by any reasonable measure of proof, Fuchida was not aboard the USS *Missouri* for the ceremony.**"

**The evidence.** This one is different in kind from the other two, and Parshall says so. He calls it "in some ways ... the most egregious of the three, because unlike his tales from the Kidô Butai there were literally thousands of potential American witnesses." He also concedes the methodological problem outright: "proving a negative is oftentimes a lot harder than proving a positive."

The chain: a photograph surfaced of an unidentified Japanese man in tropical military dress with a camera aboard *Missouri*, not a delegation member. **Martin Bennett**, then researching a Fuchida screenplay, wrote to the Battleship Missouri Memorial. Curator Michael Weidenbach returned the oral history of *Missouri*'s captain, Stuart Murray, which identified the man:

> "The Japanese were allowed to have a newsreel photographer ... he was assigned a position on the 40mm gun platform on the starboard wing of the verandah deck. Two Marines had been assigned him to keep an eye on him because I felt there was a possibility he might try to pull a fancy trick with his camera or something and be a hero or a kamikaze by taking with him some of the central people. So these two Marines each had a hand on his leg and put him in his place and told him to stay there ... they had their other hand on the butt of their Colt .45 ... there was no question that he got the word."

Parshall adds that a Russian photographer who moved to get a better view was physically tackled by a Marine and escorted back. Weidenbach's stated position: if Fuchida had been aboard "in any capacity whatsoever, his presence would have been noted, and his placement would have been noted in the official records ... and would have been strictly monitored and recorded."

**Do not overstate this one.** Parshall's own conclusion is a standard-of-proof judgment, not a document proving absence.

## Fuchida's other claims, verified one by one

**1. That he personally confronted or advised Nagumo about a third wave at Pearl Harbor to hit the fuel tank farm and submarine base.**
**Verdict: rejected by named historians, with a real dissent.** Parshall (*NWCR* 2010): "it would appear that none of these events ever took place." Genda denied it. Kusaka's account does not contain it. Zimm, in *Attack on Pearl Harbor* (2011), independently calls the heated third-strike discussion aboard *Akagi* "a fabrication of Commander Fuchida Mitsuo" (search-confirmed). Willmott and Tohmatsu (2001) reportedly call Fuchida's version "blatant and shameless self-advertisement" about "an episode which never took place" (this exact phrasing is search-confirmed via Wikipedia's citation to Willmott and Tohmatsu; I did not load the book).
**The dissent, which the script must acknowledge:** Bennett (*NWCR* Winter 2013) shows that Prange's own footnotes in *God's Samurai* date the fuel-tank remark to an interview of **4 March 1948**, not 1963, which if correct undercuts Parshall's "his story changed by 1963" chronology. Bennett also notes Fuchida never claimed a heated argument, only that he "strongly recommended" a further attack (*Midway*, p. 57), and that Capt. Jisaku Okada of *Kaga* independently recommended adding fuel tanks and dock facilities to the target list. Parshall's published reply (Spring 2013) does not concede the 1948 date; he says "a careful review of Fuchida's statements given in several interviews from 1945 to 1963 reveals a different picture. Fuchida's account changed dramatically over that time."
**Honest framing for the script:** the *argument on the bridge* is denied by the only other man in the room who wrote about it, and both Parshall and Zimm call it invented. Whether Fuchida ever privately thought about the oil tanks is less settled than the popular version of the debunking suggests.

**2. That Nimitz later told Fuchida the third wave would have extended the war.**
**Verdict: NOT FOUND as a Fuchida-Nimitz conversation.** What is real is a widely repeated Nimitz remark that destroying the fuel storage "would have prolonged the war another two years." I could not trace that remark to a primary document or to a named historian's citation in any Tier 1 source, and I found **no** source at all documenting Nimitz saying it *to Fuchida*. Fuchida did meet former enemies including Nimitz on postwar American tours (Tier 2, from popular biographies and his memoir), but the specific conversation is unsupported. **Mark this NOT FOUND. Do not put it in the script as fact, and if the script mentions it, it must be flagged as an untraceable popular attribution.**

**3. That he was present at the Japanese surrender aboard USS *Missouri*.**
**Verdict: uncorroborated, and judged false by Parshall. Not positively disproven.** Parshall (2013 reply): "no one has ever produced a corroborating account placing Fuchida at this event. The only 'evidence' we have comes from Fuchida himself." Bennett's counter is real and should be stated: Weidenbach told him in January 2012 that "There is no single roster of all the individuals that were aboard that day ... there appears to be no record made of their accompanying staff members." Bennett also offered a photographic identification of a mustached Asian man on an upper deck; Parshall's reply states that photograph "was analyzed by the curator of the Battleship Missouri Memorial and shown to be that of an American sailor." Bennett further cites a 1950 journal entry by Pocket Testament League secretary Glenn Wagner recording Fuchida saying "First to step on Battleship Missouri, lit cigarette and was grabbed by a US Marine."
**Correct label: unverifiable, with the weight of professional opinion against it.**

**4. That he visited Hiroshima immediately after the bombing and that his companions died of radiation while he did not.**
**Verdict: not challenged by any historian I found. Treat as Fuchida's own uncorroborated account, not as an exposed lie.** The standard version: he was in Hiroshima for a conference shortly before 6 August 1945, and returned on 7 August with a damage assessment party; many members of that party later died of radiation sickness while he showed no symptoms. Parshall's article does not mention Hiroshima. Bennett's rebuttal does not mention it. Zimm's *Naval History* article does not mention it. Note also that the versions vary between "many members" and "all members except Fuchida," which is itself a sign of a story that has grown in retelling. **The script must not list this among the debunked claims. There is no published debunking of it.**

**5. Anything about the Doolittle Raid or Japanese wartime atrocities.**
**Verdict: mostly NOT FOUND as a documented Fuchida fabrication.** What is documented:
- Fuchida was called to testify at Japanese war crimes trials, believed them to be victors' justice, and set out to interview returned Japanese POWs to prove Americans had treated prisoners equally badly. That project is what led him to Kanegasaki and Peggy Covell. This is the standard account, resting largely on Prange's *God's Samurai* and Fuchida's own memoir, so it is Fuchida-sourced. (Tier 2 corroboration only.)
- The Doolittle connection runs through **Jacob DeShazer**, a Doolittle Raider POW, whose tract reached Fuchida in 1948. That is separate from any Fuchida claim about the raid itself.
- I found **no** Parshall or Zimm challenge to any Fuchida statement about the Doolittle Raid or about atrocities. Mark NOT FOUND.

**6. Bonus, and important: the Pearl Harbor signal flares.**
**Verdict: a named, credentialed, Tier 1 accusation of lying, published by USNI itself.** In "Commander Fuchida's Decision" (*Naval History*, December 2016, vol. 30 no. 6), Cdr. Alan D. Zimm USN (Ret.) argues that Fuchida's famous story of accidentally firing a second "Black Dragon" flare, causing Takahashi's dive bombers to attack out of sequence, is untrue. His reasoning: Fuchida told the Strategic Bombing Survey the interval between flares was "about 10 seconds," which is not enough time to observe non-response, reload a flare pistol and fire again; the earliest track charts, probably supplied by Fuchida himself, contradict his sequence; and American ground sightings timed against the 0755 "Prep" bugle and 0800 colors show the torpedo bombers held north for five extra minutes, which only makes sense under the no-surprise plan. Zimm's conclusion: "**All this adds up to one thing: Fuchida was not telling the truth when he claimed that he intended to signal for a surprise attack.**" His proposed motive: Fuchida ordered the wrong attack plan and shifted blame onto Takahashi, who had been killed at Coral Sea and could not answer.

**7. Bonus, three more from Parshall (combinedfleet.com/ThreeMoreWhoppers.htm, announced in his Spring 2013 *NWCR* reply).**
These are self-published on Parshall's own site, not peer-reviewed, so they are a step below the *NWCR* material, but they are annotated with Japanese sources and Parshall staked his professional reputation on them in a Navy journal. Summarized:
- **Colombo, 5 April 1942.** Fuchida claimed he spotted and directed the destruction of twelve Swordfish. *Hiryu*'s records show *Hiryu*'s fighters made the kill at 0732; there were six Swordfish, not twelve; and Fuchida approached from a direction that made the sighting impossible.
- **HMS *Hermes*, 9 April 1942.** Fuchida claimed he landed, watched a Blenheim attack at 1045, took off again and witnessed *Hermes* sinking at 1055. *Hermes* was 155 nautical miles away. *Akagi*'s records show one Fuchida mission that day. Parshall notes the story grew: the *Hermes* element is absent from *Midway* and appears later in *God's Samurai*.
- **The Borneo crash and jungle ordeal, March 1942.** Fuchida claimed flak holed his fuel tank during the Tjilatjap raid and he crash-landed in Borneo. *Akagi*'s *kōdōchōshos* mark all eighteen aircraft "OK" on return; Borneo was on the wrong side of Java and out of range; and Murata's biography records nothing. Parshall credits J. Michael Wenger and Don Kehn Jr.

## How the myth propagated into accepted history

The chain is documentable and each link has a source.

**Step 0, the institutional prior.** Before Fuchida, the American narrative was already shaped toward "incredible victory." The 1948 Bates Report from the Naval War College "lauded the excellence of American strategy and battle performance, while focusing on Japanese shortcomings," and Samuel Eliot Morison's *History of United States Naval Operations in World War II* (vol. IV, 1953), written with privileged access to official records, "mainly celebrated American achievements." Britain's official historian Stephen Roskill then leaned on Morison, compounding it. [John Francis, "Review Essay: The Battle of Midway," *The Northern Mariner / Le marin du nord*, XX no. 2, April 2010, pp. 199-207, loaded]

**Step 1, 1955, the United States Naval Institute.** USNI published the book and simultaneously excerpted it in *Proceedings* as "Five Fateful Minutes at Midway." The Northern Mariner review essay is blunt about what happened next: the official versions "relied heavily on two personal testimonies," George Gay's and Fuchida's, and asks the killer rhetorical question, "**What better corroboration could there be but the words of a former enemy?**" It then delivers the verdict: "**His book is now regarded as a 'nonsense work' designed to protect reputations.**"

**Step 2, 1967, Walter Lord.** *Incredible Victory*. Per the Northern Mariner, Lord's book "produced what Professor Isom has described as 'the standard American scenario of the battle,'" whose climax is the dive bombers arriving "just at the very moment their decks were crowded with loaded attack planes and unused ordnance." Lord did not read Japanese and *Senshi Sōshō* did not exist yet.

**Step 3, 1970 and 1976, the movies.** Parshall: Fuchida's tank-farm scene "was mirrored in the movie *Tora! Tora! Tora!* thereby passing into the American collective memory." On *Midway* (1976), the Northern Mariner: the film left audiences with "the by-now familiar characterization of the battle as an 'incredible victory'" and the image of Japanese decks "crowded with planes and loose bombs." Parshall's summary of the compounding effect: "Nor do many veterans get to see their personal versions of history enshrined in not one but two major motion pictures (*Tora! Tora! Tora!* and *Midway*), thereby ensuring that their distortions will be incorporated into the common wisdom of the most important conflict of the twentieth century."
*Caution:* Parshall states Prange and Fuchida were technical advisers on *Tora! Tora! Tora!*; **Bennett disputes this**, saying Genda and Prange advised and Fuchida did not, and that the "exceptional people get exceptional treatment" scene Parshall reads as character evidence is fictional. This is unresolved. Do not build a beat on it.

**Step 4, 1982, Prange.** *Miracle at Midway*, which Parshall calls "hands down, the most important English-language account of the battle, one whose details were subsequently incorporated into many other Western histories." Parshall notes plainly: "It did not help matters that Fuchida had become great friends with Gordon Prange." Prange had interviewed Fuchida for hundreds of hours from 1946 onward and was, at the time, the chief historian of MacArthur's occupation staff. The pipeline from Fuchida to American institutional memory ran through one very well-placed man.

**Step 5, 1992, canonization.** USNI reissued the book in its **Classics of Naval Literature** series. Thomas B. Buell's introduction, quoted verbatim by Bennett: "As one of the editors was Roger Pineau, the premier American expert on the Japanese navy in the war, there is good reason to believe that the data as to events is accurate. **I am not aware of any challenges to its assertions since this book was first published in 1955.**" That was written twenty-one years after *Senshi Sōshō* vol. 43 had already destroyed Fuchida's credibility in Japan. This is the single sharpest documented illustration of the language barrier at work.

**Step 6, the myth outliving the correction.** The Smithsonian's own magazine, in a feature published **8 November 2019** by Meilan Solly, fourteen years after *Shattered Sword* and in an article that quotes *Shattered Sword* elsewhere, still writes: "**In the ensuing confusion, sailors left unsecured ordnance, as well as fueled and armed aircraft, scattered across the four carriers' decks**," and later says *Kaga* and *Akagi* "exploded in flames from the excess ordnance and fuel onboard." That is a sourceable, dateable, high-prestige example of the packed-decks image still circulating as fact. (Loaded via Wayback.)

**Counter-example worth having.** The National WWII Museum's own "The Battle of Midway" article does **not** make the packed-decks claim, does not mention the five minutes, and does not mention Fuchida at all. The museum has also hosted Parshall lecturing on *Shattered Sword*. So the institutions have moved; popular and journalistic retellings lag.

**On US Navy institutional history and Naval Academy teaching specifically: PARTIAL.** I can document that the Naval War College published both the 1948 Bates Report and, later, the entire Parshall/Isom/Bennett debate in its own journal, and that Parshall is an adjunct lecturer at the Naval War College. I found **no** source documenting what was taught at the US Naval Academy in any specific era. **Mark that sub-claim NOT FOUND. Do not assert it.**

## Current state of scholarship, including pushback and what is still argued

**Settled, as near as anything in this field gets.** The flight decks of *Akagi*, *Kaga* and *Sōryū* were not packed with a spotted, fueled, armed strike force at 1020 on 4 June 1942. This rests on *Senshi Sōshō* vol. 43 (1971), the carriers' *kōdōchōshos*, and Japanese carrier doctrine. It was accepted in Japan from 1971. *Shattered Sword* (2005) won the John Lyman Book Award for Best Book in U.S. Naval History. Craig Symonds's *The Battle of Midway* (Oxford University Press, 2011) incorporates it. Parshall was consulted on Emmerich's 2019 *Midway*, which drops the image. The Northern Mariner's summary in 2010: "**Much of the credit for opening up the debate must go to Parshall and Tully, although they were not the first to identify the issues. To some extent, they have provided a target for others to shoot at and there has been a lot of shooting.**"

**The published pushback, in order.**

1. **Dallas W. Isom**, law professor, Willamette University. Argued a different reconstruction in *NWCR* Summer 2000, drew the Parshall/Dickson/Tully "Doctrine Matters" reply in Summer 2001, replied again in the same issue's "In My View," and published *Midway Inquest: Why the Japanese Lost the Battle of Midway* (Indiana University Press, 2007). Isom's positions relevant here: he holds that *Senshi Sōshō* "fudges" some entries, that "the entries in Nagumo's battle report showing that the rearming operation was ordered at 0715 and countermanded at 0745 were fabrications to put Nagumo in a better light," and that the Japanese official history is "fragmentary, often inconsistent, and inaccurate" (as quoted by Bennett from *Midway Inquest*, pp. 10 and 133). "Doctrine Matters" attacks Isom directly: "A major error in the Isom article is that it repeatedly misstates what aircraft actually were on the Japanese flight decks. In several places, Isom presumes that the Japanese antiship strike force was on the flight decks when it is demonstrable that those aircraft were still in their hangars." **Important nuance: Isom and Parshall/Tully are not on opposite sides of the Fuchida question. Isom is the man who first showed that the late launch of *Tone*'s No. 4 float plane was not the cause of the Japanese failure, a finding Parshall and Tully adopted. Their fight is over the reconstruction of the morning, not over whether Fuchida is reliable.**

2. **Cdr. Ken Hansen**, reviewing *Shattered Sword* in *The Northern Mariner*, granted the "good research and highly integrated logic" but criticized the book as "bottom-up analysis" that concentrates on tactics and technology without adequately covering command relationships and factional rivalry between Yamamoto and Nagumo.

3. **Martin Bennett**, *NWCR* Winter 2013, the only sustained published defense of Fuchida. His core arguments, stated fairly: Parshall announced a conclusion ("bury Fuchida") before the analysis; the tank-farm remark is documented to a 1948 Prange interview, not 1963; Genda could only ever have said he did not hear a proposal, not that none was made; Fuchida and Genda were eyewitnesses who lived on these carriers and are more reliable on timing than a modern historian's calculations, with **Genda testifying in a 1948 US military interview that *Akagi* was fifteen minutes from launching**; Japanese records were reconstructed after four carriers and their logs were lost; and Weidenbach's own statement that no complete *Missouri* roster exists means Fuchida's presence cannot be disproven. Bennett also personally interviewed men who worked with Fuchida postwar and describes "a humble, gentle man who never bragged about his part in the war but rather profusely apologized at every opportunity."

4. **Parshall's reply**, *NWCR* Spring 2013 "In My View," pp. 136-138. His central methodological point, and the best line in the whole exchange for the channel's purposes:

> "In essence, Mr. Bennett is saying that operational records must take a backseat to personal accounts. This is folly ... Consider the following example, wherein a hypothetical modern historian produces a book on the Battle of Midway. Based on Japanese aviator accounts, this author might claim that eyewitness accounts 'proved' that the Japanese struck the carrier *Yorktown* with five bombs during the battle. However, in the face of such 'eyewitness evidence,' more prudent naval historians would be well within their rights to say, 'Wait a minute! We have the *Yorktown*'s after-action report, which describes precisely where each of the three bombs hit her. She's an American ship, and they ought to know!' This same principle applies exactly in reverse to the Japanese unit records."

He also invokes the reviewers of his manuscript: John Lundstrom, Osamu Tagaya, and James Sawruk personally vetted *Shattered Sword*'s approach and conclusions, with Henry Sakaida and J. Michael Wenger named as part of the same research community.

**What honestly remains argued.**
- Whether Fuchida ever mentally noted the fuel tanks on 7 December. Bennett's 1948 citation is real and Parshall's reply does not directly rebut the date. The *argument on the bridge* is a separate matter and is denied by Genda.
- Whether Fuchida was aboard *Missouri*. Unresolvable on current evidence in the strict sense. Parshall wins on burden of proof; Bennett is right that no roster exists.
- The precise minute-by-minute reconstruction of the Japanese morning (Isom vs Parshall/Tully). This is my colleague agent's territory, but the script should not present the operational reconstruction as a solved problem.
- Fuchida's character and motives, which is explicitly amateur psychology by Parshall's own admission.

**Where the consensus is.** Fuchida is not usable as a standalone source. Parshall's own formulation in 2013 is the fair one and the script should adopt it rather than a harder line: "**If Fuchida is to be used at all, he absolutely must be used in conjunction with other, corroborating sources.**"

## Motive, documented analysis versus speculation (label clearly)

**Documented analysis by named historians:**
- **Parshall, on the "fates of war" function:** "Fuchida subtly shifted the causes for Japan's defeat away from individuals and more toward what might be termed a 'fates of war' explanation, which is more acceptable to Japanese societal sensibilities. This made sense in a book written immediately after the conflict by a former Japanese naval officer trying to salvage some honor from the wreckage of both a career and a lost war." He draws an explicit comparison to F. W. von Mellenthin's *Panzer Battles* (1956) and other Wehrmacht memoirs, which "helped orient the terms of study of the eastern front along essentially German lines for the better part of fifty years" and "implanted the myth of outsized German martial prowess."
- **Parshall, on why Americans bought it:** "Americans have always identified with tales of plucky underdogs prevailing against the odds. The story that Fuchida fed us was oriented along those lines ... With such a 'reliable' witness providing ready-made images for any screenplay, why would anyone think to look further into the (incredibly difficult and tedious) Japanese sources?"
- **Zimm, on the flares specifically:** blame deflection onto a dead man. Fuchida "possibly was deflecting the blame to 'that blockhead' Takahashi," who was killed at Coral Sea and could not defend himself.
- **The Northern Mariner review essay** describes Fuchida's book as "regarded as a 'nonsense work' designed to protect reputations," and separately notes that Japanese accounts of this era "were also influenced by a desire to remain on friendly terms with the victors of the conflict, who by then were the occupying power in the Japanese homeland."
- **Named third-party summary:** the assessment that Parshall and Tully "prove that he often lied as part of a larger agenda to refurbish the good name of the Japanese navy" appears in book-review literature (Tier 2, search-confirmed only).

**Explicitly labeled speculation, by Parshall himself.** He introduces his motive section with: "Here we must set aside strictly objective historical enquiry and venture into amateur psychology (a prospect that always makes me queasy)." His conclusion is that Fuchida "considered himself exceptional. Above all else, he wanted to be perceived as being intelligent and insightful, and if that meant depicting himself as armed with wisdom that could only have been developed in hindsight, so be it." **The scene he uses as evidence for this is from *Tora! Tora! Tora!*, a movie, and Bennett's rebuttal that it is fictional is correct on its face. Do not use this beat.**

**Also documented, and it is about the audience rather than about Fuchida:** Parshall's explanation of why the correction was so hard to land.

> "By his conversion to Christianity and ordination, Fuchida was vested with the aura that we typically confer on all clergy. We are taught in Sunday school that priests do not lie ... I have been called to task more than once by World War II veterans who express incredulity that a man of the cloth like Fuchida could have lied about his wartime experiences ... Furthermore, in the eyes of veterans, Fuchida, despite being an enemy during the war years, was still (as Bill Mauldin put it) a member of 'The Benevolent and Protective Brotherhood of Them What Has Been Shot At,' whereas historians of the postwar era are decidedly not."

That is a claim about credulity, not about fraud. See handling notes.

**Commercial storytelling as motive: NOT FOUND.** No source I read argues Fuchida invented for money.

## Absurd / ironic beats (all real, all sourced)

1. **The most quoted eyewitness to Midway flew nothing.** Fuchida did not take off on 4 June 1942. He had had an emergency appendectomy aboard *Akagi* days earlier. He described the carrier's flight deck from the bridge above it, and his description is contradicted by that same carrier's own air group records. [Fuchida's own 1955 text; Parshall 2010]
2. **The United States Naval Institute published it, excerpted it, and later canonized it.** USNI put out the book in 1955, ran "Five Fateful Minutes at Midway" in *Proceedings* the same month, and in 1992 added it to its **Classics of Naval Literature** series with an introduction stating "I am not aware of any challenges to its assertions since this book was first published in 1955." [*Proceedings* June 1955, loaded; Buell's introduction quoted in Bennett 2013]
3. **Japan knew by 1971. America found out in 2005.** *Senshi Sōshō* vol. 43 demolished Fuchida's Midway account thirty-four years before *Shattered Sword*. Parshall: "because of the difficulty of the source materials, most American historians were not even aware of the value of *Senshi Sōshō*, let alone what it said about Midway in particular, until around the turn of the twenty-first century." Buell's "no challenges" line was written twenty-one years after the challenge had been published, in Japanese.
4. **The single Zero.** The one aircraft in Fuchida's most famous sentence, the fighter that "whizzed off the deck" the instant the Hell-divers appeared, is in *Akagi*'s records as a **combat air patrol fighter**, launched to fight off the American attack, not the first plane of a counterstrike. Parshall: "We even know the pilot's name." The one concrete, verifiable detail in the scene is the detail that proves the scene wrong.
5. **The Navy journal that published the demolition is the Navy's own.** The *Naval War College Review* ran Parshall's "Three Whoppers," then Bennett's full-length rebuttal, then Parshall's reply, over three years. The professional US Navy institutions both built and dismantled the myth.
6. **The man who supplied the evidence against Fuchida became Fuchida's defender.** Martin Bennett, researching a Fuchida screenplay, wrote to the Battleship Missouri Memorial and obtained the Murray testimony. Parshall used it to demolish whopper number three, and credits Bennett by name in his footnotes. Bennett then wrote a sixteen-page *NWCR* article attacking Parshall's entire method. [Parshall 2010 n. 20; Bennett 2013]
7. **The Hollywood remake hired the debunker.** Parshall consulted on Emmerich's 2019 *Midway*, which drops the packed-decks image his own book destroyed. (Tier 2/3, flagged, corroborate before scripting.)
8. **The myth outlived its own obituary in the Smithsonian's magazine.** *Smithsonian*, 8 November 2019: "sailors left unsecured ordnance, as well as fueled and armed aircraft, scattered across the four carriers' decks," in an article that cites *Shattered Sword* by name a few paragraphs later.
9. **Fuchida's own first account is his cleanest.** In the October 1945 USSBS interrogation he described his Pearl Harbor role in three words, "I was an air observer," and said nothing about fuel tanks, third waves, or arguments with admirals. Tully's rule, quoted by Parshall: "witnesses' first accounts are often their best accounts."
10. **The 1976 movie accidentally started the correction.** The Northern Mariner: "Strangely, it appears that a major catalyst was Hollywood's film *Midway* (1976). This dramatization led many of the survivors to declare 'it wasn't like that,' which in turn prompted various studies as new evidence became available." The film that hardened the myth also provoked the people who could contradict it.

## Handling notes (what the script must NOT overclaim)

1. **Not a sickbed.** Fuchida was on *Akagi*'s **bridge** at 1024, per his own account. He was grounded and recovering from abdominal surgery, but "watched the battle from a sickbed" is wrong. The accurate and better line is that he watched from the island of a ship, days after being cut open, and was carried off it in a bamboo stretcher with two broken ankles.
2. **Do not use his Christian conversion as evidence he was a fraud.** It is a separate biographical fact. Parshall's argument about clerical aura is about **why audiences believed him**, not about why he lied, and Parshall never argues the conversion was insincere. Bennett produces first-hand witnesses describing a humble, apologetic man. Both can be true. Keep them in separate compartments and say so if the script gets near it.
3. **Ordination is disputed.** Parshall writes "conversion to Christianity and ordination." Bennett states flatly "He was never ordained, so Parshall's ideas surrounding that notion are false." *Christian History* magazine documents the Captain Fuchida Evangelistical Association and speaking tours but does not document ordination. **Say "evangelist," not "ordained minister" or "bishop."** One search snippet described him as a "Lutheran bishop"; that appears to be simply wrong and should never be repeated.
4. **Conversion date conflicts.** Wikipedia and *Christian History* give September 1949 (after reading the Bible); other sources give 14 April 1950. Bennett's cited Glenn Wagner journal places a Fuchida meeting on 14 April 1950. **Say "around 1949-1950" unless you can resolve it.**
5. **Do not say the whole book is fiction.** Parshall's target is a set of specific claims. His own closing position: "The fundamental goal of history rightly remains not the discrediting of Fuchida but rather the perfection of our collective knowledge about the events he witnessed." Also, the 1955 English text was translated and edited by Kawakami and Pineau, who by the Northern Mariner's account "felt a need to correct what they perceived as some errors," so it is not purely Fuchida's unfiltered voice.
6. **The *Missouri* claim is unproven, not disproven.** Parshall's own words are "by any reasonable measure of proof." Weidenbach's later statement that no complete roster exists is real and Bennett is entitled to it. Say "no one has ever produced any evidence he was there except his own word," which is exactly what Parshall says and is unimpeachable.
7. **Do not include the Nimitz-told-Fuchida conversation.** NOT FOUND.
8. **Do not present Hiroshima as a debunked whopper.** No historian has challenged it. If it appears at all, present it as his own account, uncorroborated.
9. **Do not present the Doolittle Raid or atrocities as areas of documented Fuchida fabrication.** NOT FOUND.
10. **Do not use the *Tora! Tora! Tora!* "exceptional people get exceptional treatment" scene** as evidence of Fuchida's character. It is a scripted line in a movie and Bennett's objection is sound.
11. **Do not claim anything about US Naval Academy curriculum.** NOT FOUND.
12. **Fuchida's rank.** He was a **Commander** at Pearl Harbor and at Midway, and a **Captain** by war's end (the October 1945 USSBS transcript lists him as Captain). Wikipedia's phrasing suggesting he was a captain from October 1941 conflicts with Zimm's and Parshall's usage. Use "Commander" for 1941-42.
13. **The human cost is not a joke and is not thin.** Fuchida's own 1955 text describes the *Akagi* hangar: "Stepping over fallen comrades, another damage control party would dash in to continue the struggle, only to be mowed down by the next explosion." Whatever the book gets wrong about the flight deck, the men who burned to death below it did burn to death. Play that straight. Japan lost 3,057 men at Midway; the US lost 362. [Smithsonian, loaded]
14. **The correction is the episode, and the correction has to be honest about its own limits.** Parshall himself does not claim total victory: "It is unlikely that Fuchida's legacy will be overturned any time soon, perhaps not even within my lifetime."
