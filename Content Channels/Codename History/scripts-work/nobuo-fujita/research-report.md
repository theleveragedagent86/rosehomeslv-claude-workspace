# Research Report: Nobuo Fujita and the Lookout Air Raid

## One-paragraph summary

On 9 September 1942, a Japanese B1-type submarine, I-25, surfaced roughly 25 to 33 miles off Cape Blanco on the southern Oregon coast, assembled a tiny two-seat floatplane from parts stored in a watertight deck hangar, and catapulted it into the pre-dawn dark. The pilot was Warrant Flying Officer Nobuo Fujita, and behind him sat Petty Officer Shoji Okuda. They carried two 168-pound thermite incendiary bombs and one goal: set the forests of the Pacific Northwest on fire and force America to pull resources back from the Pacific. They dropped both bombs near Wheeler Ridge on Mount Emily, about 10 miles from Brookings, Oregon. A Forest Service lookout named Howard "Razz" Gardner heard the engine, reported it, and later walked to the smoke with a small crew. They found a crater, a dozen or so small fires burning weakly in a rain-soaked forest, and bomb fragments with Japanese characters on them. They beat the fires out. Fujita went back on 29 September and dropped two more bombs near Port Orford. Those two have never been found. It was the only time in the war an enemy aircraft with a human being in it bombed the mainland United States, and it accomplished almost nothing. The story's real weight comes after: in May 1962, the Brookings Jaycees invited Fujita back for their Azalea Festival, the town split over it, and Fujita arrived carrying his family's 400-year-old katana, which he gave to the town. He spent the next thirty-five years tied to Brookings. He was made an honorary citizen days before he died in September 1997, and in October 1998 his daughter buried part of his ashes at the bomb site.

## Verified timeline

- **23 February 1942** - Japanese submarine I-17, under Cdr Nishino Kozo, shells the Ellwood oil field near Goleta, California, roughly 12 miles north of Santa Barbara. About 16 to 25 shells fired starting around 19:15. Damage limited to a pier and a derrick. First shelling of the North American mainland in the war. [California State Military Museum; Wikipedia "Bombardment of Ellwood"; American Oil & Gas Historical Society]
- **17 February 1942** - Fujita and Okuda fly the E14Y over Sydney Harbour on a pre-dawn reconnaissance flight, counting roughly 23 ships. Fujita flies further reconnaissance over Melbourne and Tasmania in early March 1942. [HistoryNet, "The Japanese Pilot Who Bombed Mainland America"]
- **18 April 1942** - Doolittle Raid on Tokyo. The Japanese retaliation logic that produced the Oregon plan is repeatedly tied to this. [HistoryNet; Stars and Stripes]
- **Late May 1942** - A faulty catapult valve prevents a planned E14Y launch near Dutch Harbor, Alaska. [HistoryNet]
- **20 June 1942** - I-25 torpedoes the freighter SS Fort Camosun off Cape Flattery. Ship survives. [Wikipedia "Japanese submarine I-25"; combinedfleet.com I-25 TROM]
- **21/22 June 1942** - I-25 surfaces about 11,000 yards off the mouth of the Columbia River and fires 17 rounds from her 5.5-inch (14 cm) deck gun at Battery Russell, Fort Stevens, Oregon. No casualties. Damage limited to a baseball backstop and telephone cables. Intended target was reportedly the US submarine base at Astoria. Described by US Army sources as the first foreign attack on a military installation on US soil since the War of 1812. [combinedfleet.com I-25 TROM; DVIDS, US Army, "The Bombing of Oregon: 80th Anniversary"; Wikipedia "Japanese submarine I-25"]
- **9 September 1942, 05:35** - Fujita's E14Y1 is catapulted from I-25, positioned roughly 25 to 33 miles off Cape Blanco. [combinedfleet.com I-25 TROM ("Fujita catapults off at 0535"); HistoryNet ("At 5:35 a.m. ... 33 miles off Cape Blanco")]
- **9 September 1942, ~06:00 to 06:30** - Fujita flies inland at around 500 feet and releases two incendiary bombs over the Mount Emily / Wheeler Ridge area, roughly 10 miles (16 km) east/northeast of Brookings. [Wikipedia "Nobuo Fujita" (06:00); McKenzie River Reflections ("approximately 6:00-6:30 A.M."); HistoryNet]
- **9 September 1942, 06:24** - Forest Service fire warden Howard "Razz" Gardner, at the Mount Emily lookout, reports an unidentified aircraft. He relays to Aircraft Warning System operator Rita Ganong at Gold Beach headquarters. Gardner described the engine as sounding like "a Model A Ford backfiring," and said he saw what looked like "a small suitcase falling from the airplane." [HistoryNet, "Japanese Bomb the Continental U.S. West Coast" (6:24 a.m.); McKenzie River Reflections; Pacific Eagles]
- **9 September 1942, around midday** - Gardner spots smoke and the fire is reported. Gardner, 18-year-old forestry student Keith V. Johnson from the nearby Bear Wallow lookout, and a crew of roughly half a dozen Forest Service workers reach the site. They find a small impact crater, a little over a dozen small fires burning fitfully, and bomb fragments engraved with Japanese writing. The fires are beaten out. [HistoryNet (smoke spotted at noon); McKenzie River Reflections; Wikipedia "Lookout Air Raids"]
- **10 September 1942** - An A-29 Hudson from McChord Field spots I-25 on the surface and attacks. Tagami crash-dives; depth charges cause minor damage. The submarine settles on the bottom off Port Orford until dark. [Pacific Eagles, "The Career of the I-25 Part Two"; Cape Blanco Heritage Society]
- **10 September 1942** - The story runs in US newspapers. Lt Gen John L. DeWitt, Western Defense Command, announces: "The Western Defense Command is investigating the circumstances surrounding the discovery on Sept. 9 of fragments of what appears to have been an incendiary bomb. These fragments were found by personnel of the United States Forestry Service near Mt. Emily nine miles northeast of Brookings, Or." Coverage is then suppressed under wartime press censorship. [Los Angeles Times, Sept 1942, "Jap Incendiary Sets Forest Fire," cited in Wikipedia "Lookout Air Raids"; Wikipedia "Lookout Air Raids"]
- **29 September 1942, before dawn** - Second sortie, launched from I-25 roughly 50 miles west of Cape Blanco. Fujita and Okuda fly east and drop two more incendiaries in the Grassy Knob area near Port Orford, about 50 miles north of Brookings. Fujita believed he started a fire. [combinedfleet.com I-25 TROM; HistoryNet; EBSCO Research Starters "Oregon bombing"]
- **29 September 1942, 05:22** - A work crew remodeling the Grassy Knob lookout, about 7 miles east of Port Orford, hears the aircraft. A firefighting patrol goes out after daylight and searches for two days. It finds no smoke and no bomb debris. **Neither bomb from the second raid has ever been found.** [HistoryNet, "Japanese Bomb the Continental U.S. West Coast"; Wikipedia "Lookout Air Raids"]
- **4 October 1942** - I-25 torpedoes the 6,653-ton armed tanker SS Camden at 43-43N, 124-54W. One crewman killed. [combinedfleet.com I-25 TROM; Pacific Eagles]
- **6 October 1942** - I-25 torpedoes the 7,038-ton armed tanker SS Larry Doheny at 42-20N, 125-02W. Six deaths. [combinedfleet.com I-25 TROM] *(Wikipedia "Japanese submarine I-25" dates the Doheny attack 5 October. See Numbers section.)*
- **11 October 1942** - I-25 fires her last torpedo at what Tagami takes to be an American submarine. It is the Soviet minelayer submarine L-16, under Cdr Dmitri F. Gussarov, transiting from Petropavlovsk via Dutch Harbor toward San Francisco. All 56 aboard are lost, including a US liaison officer. Japan and the USSR were not at war. [combinedfleet.com I-25 TROM; Pacific Eagles; Wikipedia "Japanese submarine I-25"]
- **3 September 1943** - I-25 is lost with all 100 hands in the New Hebrides / Espiritu Santo area. Attribution is uncertain; combinedfleet credits USS PATTERSON's depth charge attack of 25 August 1943 as the probable cause; other sources credit US destroyers on 3 September. [combinedfleet.com I-25 TROM; Wikipedia "Japanese submarine I-25"]
- **5 May 1945** - A Japanese Fu-Go balloon bomb kills six civilians near Bly, Oregon: Elsye Mitchell (26, pregnant) and five children, Eddie Engen (13), Jay Gifford (13), Sherman Shoemaker (11), Dick Patzke (14), and Joan "Sis" Patzke (13). They were the only civilians killed by enemy weapons on the US mainland in the war. **PLAY STRAIGHT.** [Smithsonian Magazine; HistoryLink.org File 10692; Oregon Encyclopedia "Balloon Bombs"]
- **June 1961** - Fujita's own first-person account, "I Bombed the U.S.A.," is published in US Naval Institute *Proceedings*, Vol. 87/6/700. *(Confirmed to exist via USNI listing; full text paywalled, see Gaps.)*
- **1961/1962** - Brookings Jaycees, planning the Azalea Festival, decide to find and invite the unknown pilot. Named Jaycees include Doug Peterson (who had the idea), Doyle Rausch, and Bill McChesney. [Offbeat Oregon History, citing McCash (2005)]
- **24 to 25 May 1962** - Fujita and his family visit Brookings for the Azalea Festival, twenty years after the raid. Fujita rides in the Azalea Festival parade, receives the key to the city, attends a crab feast, and is flown over Mount Emily in a Piper. At a banquet he presents the town his family's 400-year-old katana. [HistoryNet, "The Japanese Pilot Who Bombed Mainland America" (May 24-25, 1962); "Samurai in the Oregon Sky" documentary site (May 24-25, 1962); Oregon Encyclopedia "Brookings" by Jeff LaLande ("arrived in May of 1962," grand marshal)] *(Conflicting date, see Myth vs record.)*
- **1985** - Fujita makes good on a long-standing promise and hosts three Brookings-Harbor High School students in Japan at his own expense, despite having gone through a business bankruptcy. He receives a US flag flown over the Capitol and a letter from a Reagan aide expressing "admiration for your kindness and generosity." [Wikipedia "Nobuo Fujita," citing AP, "Japanese Pilot Who Bombed Oregon Keeps Promise"; Stars and Stripes]
- **1990** - Returns to Brookings for the Azalea Festival. [Offbeat Oregon History; Wikipedia "Nobuo Fujita"]
- **1992** - On the 50th anniversary, plants a redwood seedling as a "peace tree" at the bomb site. [Oregon Travel Information Council historical marker text; City of Brookings official site; "Samurai in the Oregon Sky"]
- **1995** - Returns again. The katana is moved from City Hall to a new display case in the Chetco Community Public Library; Fujita had helped raise funds toward the library. [Wikipedia "Nobuo Fujita"; Stars and Stripes]
- **Mid-September 1997** - Brookings names Fujita an honorary citizen and ambassador of international goodwill while he is hospitalized with terminal lung cancer in Tsuchiura, Ibaraki Prefecture. Sources say "days" or "about one week" before his death. [Wikipedia "Nobuo Fujita"; HistoryNet ("just one week before his death"); City of Brookings]
- **30 September 1997** - Fujita dies in Tsuchiura, Ibaraki, Japan, aged 85. [Wikipedia "Nobuo Fujita"; Find a Grave memorial 19244912; NYT obituary published 3 October 1997] *(Stars and Stripes gives 2 October 1997. See Numbers section.)*
- **3 October 1997** - Nicholas D. Kristof's obituary, "Nobuo Fujita, 85, Is Dead; Only Foe to Bomb America," runs in the *New York Times*. [New York Times, cited in Wikipedia "Lookout Air Raids" and "Nobuo Fujita"]
- **October 1998** - Fujita's daughter, Yoriko Asakura, brings part of his ashes from Japan and buries them at the bomb site on Mount Emily. She is quoted saying part of her father's soul would forever be flying over Mount Emily. [Wikipedia "Nobuo Fujita"; Offbeat Oregon History; World War II Database]
- **6 July 2006** - The site is listed on the National Register of Historic Places as the Wheeler Ridge Japanese Bombing Site, NRHP reference number 06000589, Curry County, Oregon. Criterion A (Event), significance level National, areas of significance Military and Social History, significant year 1942. [NPS National Register; noehill.com NRHP listing 06000589; LandmarkHunter]
- **October 2008** - The Forest Service dedicates the Bombsite Trail (Trail 1118), a one-mile hike each way, about 19 miles east of Brookings. [Patty Burel, "Trail Dedication at Japanese Bombing Site Set," *FS Today*, US Forest Service, 19 September 2008; Larry Bingham, *The Oregonian*, 2 October 2008]

## Key figures

- **Nobuo Fujita (1911 to 30 September 1997)** - Warrant Flying Officer, Imperial Japanese Navy. Born in Bungotakada, Oita Prefecture. Served 1932 to 1945. Age 30 or 31 at the time of the raids. Flew the Yokosuka E14Y1 from I-25, including reconnaissance over Sydney, Melbourne, Tasmania, and Alaska. Pilot of both Oregon raids. Sources broadly agree he proposed the idea of arming submarine floatplanes for offensive strikes. Final rank listed as Sub-Lieutenant, a post-war demobilization promotion. Ran a hardware store in Ibaraki after the war, later worked at a wire manufacturing company, and at one point went through bankruptcy. [Wikipedia "Nobuo Fujita"; HistoryNet; Archives West finding aid to the William McCash Research Collection]
- **Shoji Okuda (Okuda Shoji)** - Petty Officer, IJN. Fujita's observer, navigator, rear gunner, and bombardier on both Oregon raids and on the earlier Australian reconnaissance flights. [Wikipedia "Lookout Air Raids"; HistoryNet; EBSCO]
- **Meiji Tagami (Tagami Meiji)** - Lieutenant Commander, later Commander, IJN. Commanding officer of I-25 from her commissioning on 15 October 1941 to 7 July 1943. In command for Fort Stevens, both Oregon air raids, the tanker attacks, and the L-16 sinking. [combinedfleet.com I-25 TROM; HistoryNet ("Captain Meiji Tagami")] *(Some sources render this "Akiji Tagami" or "Mieji Tagami." See Myth vs record.)*
- **Tasuo Tsukudo** - Lieutenant, IJN. Executive officer of I-25. [HistoryNet, "Japanese Bomb the Continental U.S. West Coast"] *(Single-source; spelling unverified elsewhere.)*
- **Howard "Razz" Gardner** - US Forest Service fire lookout/fire warden, Mount Emily lookout, Siskiyou National Forest. Heard and reported the aircraft, spotted the smoke, and helped extinguish the fire. Twenty years later he opposed the 1962 visit and was, by multiple accounts, put in jail for its duration. [Wikipedia "Lookout Air Raids"; HistoryNet; McKenzie River Reflections]
- **Keith V. Johnson** - US Forest Service fire lookout, Bear Wallow lookout tower, age 18, a forestry student. Dispatched to the fire; helped Gardner control it. [Wikipedia "Lookout Air Raids"; HistoryNet]
- **Rita Ganong** - Aircraft Warning System operator at Gold Beach headquarters, took Gardner's aircraft report. [McKenzie River Reflections]
- **Louis Amort** - One of the Forest Service men who collected unexploded bomb fragments and carried them to town in a paper sack or blanket, reportedly in a glove box, over rough terrain. [McKenzie River Reflections; Offbeat Oregon History, "During WWII, man brought home Japanese bomb in the glovebox"]
- **Lt Gen John L. DeWitt** - Commander, Western Defense Command. Issued the 10 September 1942 public statement confirming the bomb fragments. [Los Angeles Times, September 1942]
- **Yoriko Asakura** - Fujita's daughter. Buried part of his ashes at the bomb site in October 1998. Also the source, in at least one account, for the statement that her father intended seppuku. [Wikipedia "Nobuo Fujita"; Stars and Stripes]
- **Doug Peterson, Doyle Rausch, Bill McChesney** - Brookings Jaycees who drove the 1962 invitation. Peterson is credited with the idea; Rausch had heard the plane as a child. [Offbeat Oregon History, citing McCash (2005)]
- **Nicholas D. Kristof** - New York Times correspondent, author of the 3 October 1997 obituary that is the most commonly cited source for the seppuku claim.
- **William McCash** - Author of *Bombs Over Brookings: The World War II Bombings of Curry County, Oregon, and the Postwar Friendship Between Brookings and the Japanese Pilot, Nobuo Fujita* (Corvallis, OR, 2005). His research collection is held at Oregon State University (Archives West ark:/80444/xv36954). This is the definitive book-length treatment.
- **Ilana Sol** - Director of the documentary *Samurai in the Oregon Sky*, based on interviews with Brookings residents and the Fujita family.

## Numbers (high-risk, source each)

- **Bomb weight: 76 kg / 168 lb each, two per sortie, four total.** [Wikipedia "Lookout Air Raids" (76 kg / 168 lb); Stars and Stripes ("two 168-pound thermite firebombs"); HistoryNet ("168-pound"); DVIDS/US Army and EBSCO both say "170-pound," which is the same figure rounded] **Do NOT use 154 kg / 340 lb as a single-bomb weight.** The Wikipedia "Nobuo Fujita" article's "two 154 kg (340 lb) incendiary bombs" is the *combined* payload mislabeled as per-bomb. 76 kg x 2 = 152 kg = ~335 lb.
- **Bomb contents: approximately 520 thermite firing elements/pellets per bomb, burning at about 2,700 degrees Fahrenheit, dispersing over more than 100 yards.** [HistoryNet, "Japanese Bomb the Continental U.S. West Coast" (520 elements, 2,700 F, 100+ yard dispersal); HistoryNet, "The Japanese Pilot Who Bombed Mainland America" (520 thermite pellets)]
- **Launch time, 9 September 1942: 05:35.** [combinedfleet.com I-25 TROM; HistoryNet]
- **Distance offshore at launch: 25 to 33 miles off Cape Blanco.** Sources vary. [Pacific Eagles (25 miles); HistoryNet (33 miles)] Second sortie launched about 50 miles off Cape Blanco. [Stars and Stripes; HistoryNet]
- **Bomb impact location: Wheeler Ridge, Mount Emily, roughly 10 miles (16 km) east/northeast of Brookings.** Sources give 9, 10, and 16 km. DeWitt's 1942 statement said "nine miles northeast of Brookings." The NRHP/trailhead is 19 road miles east of Brookings, which is driving distance, not straight-line. [Los Angeles Times 1942; Wikipedia "Lookout Air Raids" (16 km / 9.9 mi); noehill NRHP listing (19 miles by road)]
- **Crater: about one foot deep.** [Wikipedia "Lookout Air Raids"]
- **Fires found: "a little over a dozen" small fires around the crater.** [McKenzie River Reflections]
- **Fewer than half the thermite pellets ignited, because of recent rain.** [HistoryNet, "The Japanese Pilot Who Bombed Mainland America"]
- **Second sortie: aircraft heard at 05:22 by a work crew at Grassy Knob lookout, 7 miles east of Port Orford. Two-day search found nothing. Both bombs never found.** [HistoryNet; Wikipedia "Lookout Air Raids"]
- **Aircraft assembly: the E14Y broke into roughly 12 components and could be assembled by a seven-man aircrew in about 15 minutes.** [HistoryNet, "The Japanese Pilot Who Bombed Mainland America"] *(Widely repeated figures elsewhere range from ~15 to ~45 minutes depending on crew and conditions. Treat 15 minutes as the well-drilled best case.)*
- **E14Y1 performance: max speed 246 km/h (153 mph) at sea level, cruise around 90 mph, range 881 km (547 miles), service ceiling 5,240 m, one rear-facing 7.7 mm machine gun, normal bomb load 60 kg (higher when flown single-crew).** [Wikipedia "Yokosuka E14Y"; Stars and Stripes (90 mph cruise)] *Note the tension: the raids carried 2 x 76 kg, well above the 60 kg listed normal load. Treat the 60 kg spec as the standard rating, not a hard ceiling.*
- **Flight duration over America: "barely 30 minutes in American airspace."** [Pacific Eagles] The children's book title *Thirty Minutes Over Oregon* reflects this.
- **I-25 specifications: B1-type (I-15 class), 2,584 long tons surfaced / 3,654 submerged, 108.7 m long, 9.3 m beam, 23.5 knots surfaced / 8 knots submerged, 14,000 nautical mile range, one E14Y floatplane stored disassembled in a watertight hangar forward of the conning tower, one 14 cm (5.5 in) deck gun.** [Wikipedia "Japanese submarine I-25"]
- **Fort Stevens: 17 rounds fired, no casualties.** [combinedfleet.com I-25 TROM; DVIDS/US Army]
- **Ellwood: 16 to 25 shells, about 20 minutes, no casualties.** [California State Military Museum; Wikipedia "Bombardment of Ellwood"] *Sources give 16, 17, and about 25. The most commonly cited is 16 to 17 hits observed out of roughly 25 fired.*
- **L-16: all 56 aboard lost (55 Soviet crew plus one US liaison officer, Sergey Mikhailoff; some counts give 55 Russians, one naturalized American, and one American officer).** [combinedfleet.com I-25 TROM; Pacific Eagles]
- **SS Camden: 6,653 tons, 1 killed. SS Larry Doheny: 7,038 tons, 6 killed.** [combinedfleet.com I-25 TROM] **PLAY STRAIGHT.**
- **I-25 lost with all 100 hands.** [combinedfleet.com I-25 TROM; Wikipedia "Japanese submarine I-25"] **PLAY STRAIGHT.**
- **Library donation: $1,000 to the Brookings library to buy books about Japan for children.** [The Engines of Our Ingenuity No. 3135, University of Houston; Stars and Stripes] *(Two sources; the $ figure would benefit from a third.)*
- **Students hosted in Japan: three Brookings-Harbor High School students, 1985, at his own expense.** [Wikipedia "Nobuo Fujita," citing AP; Stars and Stripes]
- **Opposition ad signers: 141 (Offbeat Oregon, citing McCash 2005) vs "over 100" (multiple secondary retellings).** Use "more than 100" or cite 141 to McCash specifically. See Myth vs record.
- **Fu-Go: over 9,300 balloons launched Nov 1944 to Apr 1945; roughly 300 found or observed in North America; Japanese planners expected about 10 percent (~900) to arrive. Six killed at Bly, Oregon, 5 May 1945, the only enemy-caused deaths on the US mainland in the war.** [Wikipedia "Fu-Go balloon bomb"; Smithsonian Magazine (~9,000 built, ~1,000 estimated to have reached North America); HistoryLink.org] *(Note the launched-vs-built and reached-vs-found distinction. Smithsonian's ~1,000 is an estimate of arrivals; ~300 is the count of ones actually found.)* **PLAY STRAIGHT.**
- **Fujita's death: 30 September 1997, age 85.** [Wikipedia; Find a Grave; NYT obit dated 3 October 1997] *(Stars and Stripes says 2 October 1997. Minority position. Use 30 September.)*

## Absurd / comedic beats (all real)

1. **The aircraft carrier was a tube.** I-25 carried her air force disassembled into about a dozen pieces in a watertight hangar on the deck. To go to war, the crew had to surface, unbolt the hangar, and physically build the airplane on the deck in the dark, in the open ocean, in enemy waters, in roughly 15 minutes. Then catapult it. Then take it apart again on return. [Wikipedia "Japanese submarine I-25"; Wikipedia "Yokosuka E14Y"; HistoryNet]
2. **The plane cruised at about 90 mph.** The entire aerial threat to the American homeland moved slower than modern highway traffic and carried a single rear-facing machine gun. [Stars and Stripes; Wikipedia "Yokosuka E14Y"]
3. **A fire lookout identified the invasion of America by ear, and the sound he reached for was a car.** Razz Gardner said the engine sounded like "a Model A Ford backfiring." [Pacific Eagles; McKenzie River Reflections]
4. **He watched the bomb fall and described it as luggage.** Gardner reported seeing "what looked like a small suitcase falling from the airplane." [McKenzie River Reflections]
5. **The great forest fire was put out by roughly six men with hand tools.** No air tankers, no cavalry. Gardner, an 18-year-old forestry student, and a handful of Forest Service workers walked in and beat out about a dozen small fires. [McKenzie River Reflections; Wikipedia "Lookout Air Raids"]
6. **The crater was a foot deep.** [Wikipedia "Lookout Air Raids"]
7. **Fewer than half the thermite pellets even lit,** because the forest Japan had chosen specifically for its flammability was soaking wet. [HistoryNet]
8. **A Forest Service man carried live bomb fragments to town in a paper sack, or a blanket in the glove box, over rough mountain road.** [McKenzie River Reflections; Offbeat Oregon History]
9. **The second raid is a mystery to this day, in the least dramatic way possible.** Two 168-pound incendiary bombs were dropped on the United States on 29 September 1942 and nobody has ever found either one. A patrol searched for two days and came back with nothing, not even smoke. [HistoryNet; Wikipedia "Lookout Air Raids"]
10. **The one thing the raid definitely accomplished was getting the submarine bombed.** The day after the "attack on America," an A-29 Hudson caught I-25 on the surface and depth-charged her into the mud off Port Orford. [Pacific Eagles]
11. **America's chief eyewitness to the only enemy air raid on the mainland got locked up so he wouldn't ruin the parade.** Twenty years later, Razz Gardner, the man who put the fire out, was reportedly put in jail for the duration of Fujita's 1962 visit. [HistoryNet; Roadside America; multiple retellings, all tracing to local newspaper accounts] *(Flag as "reportedly." See Gaps.)*
12. **The opposition ad's own argument was accidentally the best case for the invitation.** The full-page ad in the *Brookings-Harbor Pilot* complained that "Fujita's only claim to fame is that he is the only (Japanese) pilot who has bombed the mainland of the United States by airplane," which is, in fact, exactly why anyone cared. [Offbeat Oregon History, citing McCash 2005]
13. **Teenagers flew a Japanese flag for the occasion and the town's older residents took it down.** [Stars and Stripes]
14. **They flew the man who bombed Mount Emily back over Mount Emily.** In a Piper. For fun. [HistoryNet]
15. **He kept the sword for 20 years planning to gut himself with it, and instead ended up in a parade eating crab.** [HistoryNet; Oregon Coast Magazine]

## Human-cost beats (play straight, no jokes)

- **No one was killed or injured in either Lookout Air Raid.** That is the fact the whole comedy rests on, and it should be stated plainly.
- **SS Camden, 4 October 1942: one crewman killed. SS Larry Doheny, 6 October 1942: six killed.** Same submarine, same patrol, three and four weeks after the raids. The I-25 was not harmless. [combinedfleet.com I-25 TROM]
- **Soviet submarine L-16, 11 October 1942: all 56 aboard lost,** killed by a country their own was not at war with, in a case of mistaken identity. [combinedfleet.com; Pacific Eagles]
- **I-25 herself: lost with all 100 hands in 1943.** Fujita and Okuda had both been reassigned by then. Every man Fujita had served with on that boat died. [combinedfleet.com I-25 TROM]
- **Bly, Oregon, 5 May 1945.** Elsye Mitchell, 26 and pregnant, and five children, Eddie Engen, 13, Jay Gifford, 13, Sherman Shoemaker, 11, Dick Patzke, 14, and Joan Patzke, 13, were killed by a Japanese Fu-Go balloon bomb on a Sunday school picnic on Gearhart Mountain. They were the only civilians killed by enemy weapons on the US mainland during the Second World War. Pastor Archie Mitchell, Elsye's husband, was parking the car and shouted a warning that came too late. [Smithsonian Magazine; HistoryLink.org; Oregon Encyclopedia] **No jokes anywhere near this beat.**
- **The Ellwood shelling of February 1942 fed the West Coast invasion panic and is cited as a contributing factor in the decision to intern Japanese Americans.** [California State Military Museum; Wikipedia "Bombardment of Ellwood"] Handle with care and without a punchline.
- **Fujita's remorse was real and lifelong.** He went bankrupt and still paid to bring three American teenagers to Japan because he had promised. He was dying of lung cancer when Brookings made him a citizen. Play the last act completely straight.

## Myth vs record (corrections)

1. **Popular telling: Fujita started a massive forest fire / devastated Oregon woodland.**
   **Actual: he started one small fire that a handful of Forest Service men walked to and beat out by hand, plus a second raid whose bombs were never even located.** Total damage: a one-foot crater, about a dozen small fires, and some scorched brush. No structures, no injuries, no deaths. [Wikipedia "Lookout Air Raids"; McKenzie River Reflections; HistoryNet]

2. **Popular telling: "Fujita was the only person to bomb the mainland United States."**
   **Actual: he flew the only manned aerial bombing of the continental US by an enemy aircraft.** That is the defensible phrasing. Competing incidents that were NOT manned aerial bombings: Ellwood (Feb 1942) and Fort Stevens (June 1942) were naval gunfire from surfaced submarines, not aircraft. The Fu-Go balloon bombs (1944-45) were unmanned, and they, not Fujita, caused the only enemy-inflicted civilian deaths on the mainland. Note also that Fujita himself flew *two* raids, which is why the Oregon Encyclopedia describes the September 9 attack as "the first and one of only two enemy aerial bombings of the U.S. mainland." [Stars and Stripes; Wikipedia "Attacks on the United States (1900-1945)"; Oregon Encyclopedia "Brookings" by Jeff LaLande]

3. **Popular telling: Fujita was a "fighter pilot" or a "bomber pilot."**
   **Actual: he was a Warrant Flying Officer, a warrant-rank naval aviator flying a two-seat reconnaissance floatplane.** The E14Y1 was a scout aircraft, not a bomber; the raids were an improvised offensive use of a reconnaissance platform, an idea Fujita himself is credited with proposing. Several outlets, including the KTVL piece and DVIDS, loosely call him a "fighter pilot" or describe the E14Y as "a modified Zero seaplane." Both are wrong. The E14Y was a purpose-built Yokosuka design, not a Zero derivative. [Wikipedia "Yokosuka E14Y"; Wikipedia "Nobuo Fujita"; HistoryNet]

4. **Bomb weight: 340 lb per bomb is wrong.**
   **Actual: 168 lb (76 kg) per bomb, two per sortie.** The "154 kg (340 lb)" figure on the Wikipedia "Nobuo Fujita" page is the combined load presented as a per-bomb figure. [Wikipedia "Lookout Air Raids"; Stars and Stripes; HistoryNet]

5. **The I-25's commander's name.**
   **Most reliable form: Tagami Meiji (Meiji Tagami), Lieutenant Commander, later Commander.** This is the form used in the combinedfleet.com tabular record of movement, which is compiled from IJN records. "Akiji Tagami" appears in Wikipedia's "Lookout Air Raids" article and gets copied widely; "Mieji Tagami" appears in the McKenzie River Reflections piece. Treat Meiji Tagami as correct and note that Akiji is a common misrendering. [combinedfleet.com I-25 TROM; HistoryNet]

6. **The seppuku claim: real, but attribute it, don't assert it.**
   The claim that Fujita brought the sword intending to commit seppuku if Brookings received him with hostility is genuinely reported, but it is a claim about intent from decades later, not a documented act. Wikipedia attributes it to Kristof's NYT obituary of 3 October 1997. Stars and Stripes attributes it to his daughter. Oregon Coast Magazine attributes it to Fujita himself ("Fujita said he had brought the sword... intending to commit seppuku"). Notably, the two most detailed narrative accounts, HistoryNet's "The Japanese Pilot Who Bombed Mainland America" and the *Samurai in the Oregon Sky* documentary summary, do not mention seppuku at all. **Correct script language: "According to his New York Times obituary, he had brought the sword intending to use it on himself if the town turned on him."** Do not state it as an unqualified fact. [NYT via Wikipedia; Stars and Stripes; Oregon Coast Magazine]

7. **The date of the 1962 visit.**
   **Most defensible: 24 to 25 May 1962, the Azalea Festival, Memorial Day weekend.** Supported by HistoryNet's detailed account, the *Samurai in the Oregon Sky* documentary, and Jeff LaLande's Oregon Encyclopedia entry on Brookings ("arrived in May of 1962"). A competing claim that the sword ceremony happened on 9 September 1962, the raid's 20th anniversary, appears on Roadside America and EBSCO. The Azalea Festival is a Memorial Day event, and the Jaycees invited him specifically as festival grand marshal, which favors May. **Safest script phrasing: "In the spring of 1962" or "twenty years later, in May 1962."** [HistoryNet; samuraiintheoregonsky.com; Oregon Encyclopedia "Brookings"]

8. **"The town welcomed him with open arms" is too clean.**
   **Actual: the town split hard.** A full-page ad ran in the *Brookings-Harbor Pilot* signed by 141 residents (McCash's figure; secondary retellings say "over 100"). There were rumors of threatening letters from Brookings and Bandon residents. Some accounts say people threatened to shoot Fujita and the sponsoring Jaycees. A local veterans group compared inviting him to inviting Fidel Castro or building a monument to John Wilkes Booth. Fujita himself feared being beaten up or having eggs thrown at him. The warm reception is the ending, not the premise. [Offbeat Oregon History citing McCash 2005; HistoryNet; Oregon Coast Magazine]

9. **The federal role.**
   The Japanese government sought and received State Department assurances that Fujita would not be arrested or tried as a war criminal before he would travel. Governor Mark O. Hatfield and President John F. Kennedy both publicly backed the visit. Kennedy is credited with congratulating the town on promoting international friendship. Do not overstate this as the State Department "arranging" the trip; it was the Jaycees' idea. [Stars and Stripes; HistoryNet; Wikipedia "Nobuo Fujita"; samuraiintheoregonsky.com]

10. **The sword's location.**
    **Actual: the katana is displayed at the Chetco Community Public Library, 405 Alder Street, Brookings, Oregon.** It was originally kept at City Hall and was moved to the library's new display case around 1995, with Fujita present. Do not say it is at City Hall. [City of Brookings official website; Wikipedia "Nobuo Fujita"]

11. **The "peace tree."**
    **Actual: a redwood seedling, planted by Fujita at the bomb site in 1992 on the 50th anniversary.** Not an azalea, not a cherry tree. [Oregon Travel Information Council historical marker; City of Brookings; samuraiintheoregonsky.com]

## Gaps (NOT FOUND)

- **NOT FOUND: the verbatim text of Nicholas D. Kristof's NYT obituary of 3 October 1997.** nytimes.com and web.archive.org both blocked fetching. The seppuku attribution is confirmed only through secondary sources citing that obituary (Wikipedia's footnote 4, Atlas Obscura, and others). If exact quotation matters, the obituary needs to be pulled from a library database or the NYT archive directly. Do not put quotation marks around any specific obituary wording in the script.
- **NOT FOUND: the full text of Nobuo Fujita, "I Bombed the U.S.A.," US Naval Institute *Proceedings*, June 1961, Vol. 87/6/700.** Confirmed to exist via USNI's own listings; the article is paywalled and returned HTTP 403. This is the single best primary source for Fujita's first-person account and should be obtained if the script quotes him about the mission itself.
- **NOT FOUND: the full NRHP nomination form for the Wheeler Ridge Japanese Bombing Site (ref 06000589).** Only the summary listing data was retrievable. The full nomination is in NPGallery.
- **NOT FOUND: the name of the Brookings mayor or official who physically received the sword in 1962.** Sources say "the Mayor" or "city officials" without naming anyone.
- **NOT FOUND: independent primary confirmation that Razz Gardner was jailed during the 1962 visit.** It is reported in HistoryNet, Roadside America, and other retellings, all of which appear to trace to local newspaper accounts and to McCash's book. Treat as "reportedly."
- **NOT FOUND: an exact date for the honorary citizenship proclamation in September 1997.** Sources say only "days before" or "about a week before" his 30 September death.
- **NOT FOUND: a precise, independently sourced figure for how much money Fujita helped raise for the Chetco library building fund.** The $1,000 book donation is sourced twice; the broader building-fund contribution is described qualitatively only.
- **NOT FOUND: any documented casualty or injury from either Lookout Air Raid.** This is a confirmed negative, not a gap, but state it as such.
- **CONFLICT UNRESOLVED: Fujita's death date, 30 September 1997 (majority, including Wikipedia and Find a Grave) vs 2 October 1997 (Stars and Stripes).** Use 30 September.
- **CONFLICT UNRESOLVED: SS Larry Doheny sunk 5 October (Wikipedia) vs 6 October 1942 (combinedfleet).** Use 6 October, citing combinedfleet, which works from the IJN record.
- **CONFLICT UNRESOLVED: the exact clock time of the second sortie.** combinedfleet's TROM says "Fujita's plane is launched by catapult at 2107 (I)" on 29 September, while HistoryNet and the Grassy Knob witnesses put the flight in the small hours before dawn (aircraft heard 05:22). The 2107 figure is likely a different time-zone convention or a same-operation entry logged on the 28th. Do not give a launch clock time for the second raid in the script; say "before dawn."
