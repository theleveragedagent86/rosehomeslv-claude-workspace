# Research Report: Operation Postmaster

## One-paragraph summary

On the night of 14 January 1942, a small British special-forces team seized three Axis vessels from Santa Isabel harbour on the neutral Spanish island of Fernando Po (now Bioko, Equatorial Guinea) in the Gulf of Guinea. The Small Scale Raiding Force (No. 62 Commando), an arm of the Special Operations Executive under Brigadier Colin Gubbins, was led on the water by Major Gustavus "Gus" March-Phillipps, with Captain Geoffrey Appleyard, Captain Graham Hayes, and the Danish volunteer Anders Lassen among the raiders. Onshore SOE agent Richard Lippett lured the Axis ships' officers to a dinner party. While they dined, the raiders arrived on two tugs, Vulcan and Nuneaton, blew the anchor cables of the Italian liner Duchessa d'Aosta and the German tug Likomba with explosive charges, and towed the vessels out to sea. The whole harbour phase took about thirty minutes, with no casualties to the raiding party and twenty-nine prisoners taken. Because Fernando Po was neutral Spanish territory and Britain did not want to push Franco's Spain toward the Axis, the British government publicly denied any involvement and staged an at-sea "interception" by the corvette HMS Violet to sell the cover story that the ships had escaped and been caught in international waters. Three of the men in this near-bloodless heist would die in the war: March-Phillipps at Operation Aquatint in September 1942, Graham Hayes executed by the Germans in 1943, and Anders Lassen killed at Lake Comacchio in 1945 and awarded a posthumous Victoria Cross.

## Verified timeline
- August 1941 - The Maid Honor Force / SSRF sails the Brixham trawler Maid Honor from Britain to West Africa. [Wikipedia "Operation Postmaster"; Thomas Harder, thomasharder.dk]
- 14 January 1942 (night) - The raid on Santa Isabel harbour, Fernando Po. Tugs positioned around 23:15 and 23:30; harbour phase about 30 minutes. [Wikipedia "Operation Postmaster"; Spotter Up]
- 15 January 1942 (at sea) - March-Phillipps sets watches and guards over the 29 prisoners; Vulcan is "captured" at the rendezvous by HMS Violet as the staged cover. [Wikipedia "Operation Postmaster"]
- Arrival at Lagos - Escorted by HMS Violet, the Duchessa d'Aosta and Vulcan make a "triumphant entrance" into Lagos. [Wikipedia "Operation Postmaster"]
- 17 January 1942 - Richard Lippett, still on the island, is taken in for questioning by the Spanish authorities. [Wikipedia "Operation Postmaster"]
- 12/13 September 1942 - Operation Aquatint at Sainte-Honorine-des-Pertes, Normandy; March-Phillipps is killed. [Wikipedia "Operation Aquatint"; Wikipedia "Graham Hayes"]
- 13 July 1943 - Graham Hayes executed by firing squad at Fresnes prison after nine months' solitary confinement. [Wikipedia "Graham Hayes"; tracesofwar.com]
- 8/9 April 1945 - Anders Lassen mortally wounded at Lake Comacchio, Italy (Operation Roast). [Wikipedia "Anders Lassen"; London Gazette VC citation via royalmarineshistory.com]
- 28 December 1945 - Lassen's posthumous Victoria Cross presented to his parents by King George VI at Buckingham Palace. [Thomas Harder, thomasharder.dk; Wikipedia "Anders Lassen"]

## Key figures
- Maj. Gustavus "Gus" March-Phillipps - commander of the SSRF, led the raid on the water; later killed at Operation Aquatint, Sept 1942. [Wikipedia "Operation Postmaster"; Wikipedia "Operation Aquatint"]
- Capt. Geoffrey Appleyard - SSRF officer on the raid. [Wikipedia "Operation Postmaster"]
- Capt. Graham Hayes - third founding member of the SSRF; captured after Operation Aquatint, held at Fresnes, executed 13 July 1943. [Wikipedia "Graham Hayes"]
- Anders Lassen - Danish volunteer, one of the five who sailed the Maid Honor; commissioned in the field and given an immediate Military Cross for Postmaster; later a major, killed at Lake Comacchio 1945, posthumous Victoria Cross. [Wikipedia "Anders Lassen"; Thomas Harder]
- Richard Lippett - SOE agent onshore who arranged the dinner-party diversion. [Wikipedia "Operation Postmaster"; Thomas Harder]
- Leonard Guise - SOE agent involved in the operation. [Wikipedia "Operation Postmaster"]
- Brigadier Colin Gubbins - senior SOE figure. [Wikipedia "Operation Postmaster"]
- Ian Fleming - credited with crafting the cover story that let the British government deny involvement. [Spotter Up]

## Numbers (high-risk, source each)
- Date: night of 14 January 1942. [Wikipedia; Spotter Up; Thomas Harder]
- Harbour phase duration: about 30 minutes. [Wikipedia; Spotter Up]
- Prisoners: 29 taken. Of these, 27 Italian men were transferred to an internment camp in Nigeria. [Wikipedia "Operation Postmaster"]
- Dinner party attendance: 12 Italian officers and 2 German officers (from Likomba). [Wikipedia "Operation Postmaster"]
- Raiding force: roughly 32 men. Spotter Up gives 4 SOE, 11 SSRF commandos, 17 local recruits. Thomas Harder describes "some 40 men." Present as "around three dozen" and note the range. [Spotter Up; Thomas Harder; Wikipedia]
- Casualties to the raiding party: none. [Wikipedia; Spotter Up; Thomas Harder]
- Duchessa d'Aosta tonnage: 7,872 GRT, built 1921 (Wikipedia). A separate summary cited ~7,651 GRT and other sources reference figures near 8,500. Present 7,872 GRT as best-sourced and note sources conflict. [Wikipedia "Operation Postmaster"; general ship references]
- Ships seized: Italian liner Duchessa d'Aosta, German tug Likomba, and the barge/launch Bibundi. [Wikipedia "Operation Postmaster"]
- Towing tugs: Vulcan and Nuneaton, provided via the Governor of Nigeria. [Wikipedia; Spotter Up]
- Cover ship: HMS Violet, a Flower-class corvette. [Wikipedia "Operation Postmaster"]

## Absurd / comedic beats (all real)
- The entire snatch was set up by inviting the enemy officers to a dinner party ashore; they went. [Wikipedia; Thomas Harder]
- The officers were reportedly seated with their backs to the windows facing the harbour so they could not see their own ships being stolen. [Thomas Harder]
- The raiders blew the anchor cables with charges and simply towed the ships away with tugs; it was a theft, not a battle. [Wikipedia; Spotter Up]
- Not a single shot was fired during the operation, per the biographer's account. [Thomas Harder]
- The whole harbour job took about thirty minutes. [Wikipedia; Spotter Up]
- The British then staged a fake at-sea "capture" of their own stolen ship by HMS Violet, then sailed into Lagos to a cheering crowd. [Wikipedia]
- Free French caps were reportedly left behind to muddy attribution; speculation blamed the British, Americans, or anti-Falangist Spanish rebels. [Spotter Up]
- Britain flatly denied the whole thing to protect relations with neutral Spain. [Spotter Up; Wikipedia]

## Human-cost beats (play straight)
- Maj. Gus March-Phillipps was killed at Operation Aquatint on the Normandy coast, 12/13 September 1942. [Wikipedia "Operation Aquatint"; Wikipedia "Graham Hayes"]
- Capt. Graham Hayes escaped Aquatint into Spain, was handed to the Germans, held nine months in solitary at Fresnes prison, and executed by firing squad on 13 July 1943. [Wikipedia "Graham Hayes"; tracesofwar.com]
- Anders Lassen was mortally wounded leading a raid at Lake Comacchio, Italy, 8/9 April 1945, and awarded a posthumous Victoria Cross; he was the only non-Commonwealth recipient of the VC in WW2. [Wikipedia "Anders Lassen"; royalmarineshistory.com]

## Myth vs record (corrections)
- Popular telling (and the 2024 film "The Ministry of Ungentlemanly Warfare"): a gun-blazing commando battle. / Actual: a near-bloodless deniable theft. No casualties to the raiders; the biographer states not a shot was fired in the operation. The drama, gun-fights, and enlarged body count are Hollywood embellishment, not the record. Treat the film as pop-culture context only, NOT a source. [Thomas Harder; Wikipedia]
- Popular framing: "commandos captured the ships in a raid." / More precise: they were lured-and-towed, with the crews' officers absent at a dinner party and the anchor cables blown, then a staged interception sold the escape cover story. [Wikipedia; Thomas Harder]
