# Research Report: Operation Tracer (Gibraltar "Stay Behind Cave")

Compiled Wave 1. Every significant claim below carries its sources inline. Anything unverified is marked NOT FOUND or flagged SINGLE SOURCE. No em-dashes used.

---

## One-paragraph summary

In the summer of 1941, with Gibraltar looking like Hitler's next objective, the Director of Naval Intelligence, Rear Admiral John Henry Godfrey, ordered a contingency built into the Rock itself. If Gibraltar fell, a small Royal Navy team would be sealed inside a concealed chamber near Lord Airey's Battery, roughly 1,350 feet up, and would spend a year or more watching the harbour through a slit in the rock and reporting Axis shipping by clandestine wireless. The plan was codenamed Tracer. It began as five men for one year and grew to six: one executive officer, two Royal Naval Volunteer Reserve surgeons, and three telegraphists. There was no exit. The entrance was to be bricked up behind them. A January 1942 meeting, attended among others by Godfrey's personal assistant Lieutenant Commander Ian Fleming, formally recommended that any man who died inside be embalmed and cemented up. The team deployed to Gibraltar under cover on 1 August 1942 and waited. Gibraltar never fell. On 24 August 1943, a week after the Axis were driven out of Sicily, the Director of Naval Intelligence signalled for one last wireless exercise, then ordered the chambers blocked and the stores handed out. The cave sat sealed and empty inside the Rock until cavers broke into it at the turn of 1996 to 1997. One of the volunteers, Surgeon Lieutenant Commander Bruce Cooper, stayed silent for over sixty years, went public in February 2007 aged 92, and returned to Gibraltar in October 2008 to confirm the site was the one built for him. He died in December 2010, aged 96. The chamber is now a listed Gibraltar heritage monument, reference HLMP2/036, open to a strict quota of 30 visitors a year.

## The two flagged sourcing problems, resolved

### 1. ADM 223/464 and the "single official document" claim

**RESOLVED, and the popular framing is an overstatement that needs correcting.**

What I verified directly in The National Archives Discovery catalogue API (not from secondary sources):

- **ADM 223/464 is real, is at TNA Kew, and its own official catalogue description does name Operation Tracer.** Verbatim scope and content field returned by the Discovery API for catalogue id C4123354: *"History of Naval Intelligence and the Naval Intelligence Department 1939-1945 including raids on the Scharnhorst, Gneisenau and Graf Spee. Also Operation Ruthless (capture of German Enigma cypher machine) planned by Commander Ian Fleming, Operation Husky, Operation Mincemeat, Operation Tracer, Operation Torch (landings in North Africa), Room 40 and the raid on St Nazaire."* Covering dates **1942-1950**. Legal status: Public Record. Closure status: **O**, meaning open. Department ADM. Held by The National Archives, Kew.
  [TNA Discovery API, record C4123354 / ADM 223/464]

- **A catalogue keyword search of TNA Discovery for "Operation Tracer" returns 9 hits, and ADM 223/464 is the only one that is this Operation Tracer.** The others are unrelated (25-pounder tracer ammunition, SA80 tracer rounds, a plutonium tracer method, and so on).
  [TNA Discovery API search, query "Operation Tracer"]

- **A search of Discovery for "Stay Behind Cave" returns nothing relevant at all** (4 hits, all unrelated: a Ladakh map, two Elizabethan Irish state papers, a service record).
  [TNA Discovery API search, query "Stay Behind Cave"]

**The correction to make on air:** the claim that ADM 223/464 is "the single official document in which this operation is mentioned" originates with the researcher Nick Metcalfe writing at Frontline Ulster, and it has been copied outward from there and from Wikipedia. It is defensible as a statement about the *catalogue*, and it is true that ADM 223/464 pages 268 to 270 is the only known surviving *narrative account* of Tracer. It is **not** true that no other paperwork ever existed or that nothing else survives. The record itself and researchers list several other Tracer documents that are referenced but not located:
  - Commander Geoffrey Birley's November 1941 feasibility report on the Rock, described in the official history as *"an elaborate one with appendices on the details of construction, camouflage, sanitary arrangements, wireless requirements, stores and crew."* Not located. [Frontline Ulster, quoting ADM 223/464]
  - Lieutenant Colonel John Kyme Cordeaux's SIS feasibility report of 27 December 1941. Not located.
  - Captain Humphry Sandwith's communications report of 11 January 1942. Not located.
  - A progress report of 15 March 1942, reference **NID 001107/42**. Not located, and described by Frontline Ulster as "likely lost to time." Wikipedia treats the same reference number (rendered "#1001107/42") as the Tracer manual and describes its contents, which is a discrepancy, see Myth vs record below.
  - The printed **Tracer manual**, disguised as an Arctic expedition manual. A radio-history summary of the Naval Intelligence files states plainly that the Operation Tracer Manual "remains undiscovered." [jproc.ca radio story, Tracer]

- **Bonus, and a genuinely useful confusion to head off: there were TWO Operation TRACERs.** TNA holds **ADM 223/457**, "Operation TRACER [Malta Convoys]: flying Hawker Hurricanes to Malta, accompanied by Lockheed Hudsons, off HMS Ark Royal and HMS Victorious coming from Gibraltar," covering dates **10 to 16 June 1941**. Completely different operation, same codename, also involving Gibraltar. Verified in the TNA Discovery catalogue. [TNA Discovery API, ADM 223/457]

- **The authorship is solid.** The three-page top secret summary titled TRACER at ADM 223/464 pages 268 to 270 is initialled C.L.M. and is the work of **Charles Langbridge Morgan (1894 to 1958)**, the novelist and drama critic who wrote the post-war official history of the Naval Intelligence Division. Morgan had no role in Tracer itself, which is why the summary reads as a neutral digest of files he was handed. [Frontline Ulster; Wikipedia; TNA catalogue confirms ADM 223 is the NID intelligence reports and papers series]

- **Minor correction:** secondary sources including Frontline Ulster describe ADM 223/464 as the official history "1939-1942." The TNA catalogue itself says **1939-1945**, with covering dates 1942-1950. Use the TNA wording.

### 2. The volunteers: exactly who is named and who is not

**Six men. Three of the six are named in the record. Three are not, and never have been.**

| Leading Signalman / telegraphist | UNKNOWN | **NOT NAMED.** |
| Leading Signalman / telegraphist | UNKNOWN | **NOT NAMED.** |
| Leading Signalman / telegraphist | UNKNOWN | **NOT NAMED.** |

The Government of Gibraltar Ministry for Heritage listing states it directly: *"The names of the three radio operators are unknown but the name of the replacement officer is known. His name was 'Windy' Gale, a native of Kent."* Wikipedia agrees: "The names of the three enlisted men who were wireless operators are unknown."

**So: two men are named with full confidence (Cooper, Milner). One is known only by a nickname and a county (Gale). Three are entirely unidentified.** Do not let a script imply a complete roster of six. There is also a **fourth named-but-rejected** man, **Lieutenant White RNVR**, proposed as officer in charge at the 17 February 1942 meeting and apparently not selected. He was never a team member.

## Verified timeline

**Strategic context**

- **Why Gibraltar mattered.** Gibraltar has been British since the Treaty of Utrecht in 1713. It is the western gate of the Mediterranean. Britain's Mediterranean position rested on holding both ends, Gibraltar and the Suez Canal, with Malta in the middle. Lose Gibraltar and the Royal Navy loses the western Mediterranean, the Malta convoys become impossible, and Britain is cut from the Empire route. That is precisely what Führer Directive No. 18 said Felix was for. Gibraltar was also physically tiny, about 2.6 square miles, and overlooked from Spain, so nothing built above ground could be hidden. This is why everything went underground. [Wikipedia, Operation Felix; Frontline Ulster; Wikipedia, Tunnels of Gibraltar]
- **1940. The civilians were removed.** To convert Gibraltar into a full fortress, the British evacuated the great majority of the civilian population, roughly **16,700 people**, to Madeira, Jamaica, Northern Ireland and mainland Britain, with about 13,500 shipped to Casablanca in early June 1940 before that route collapsed. About 13,000 Gibraltarians spent the war in London, under the Blitz and later the V1s. Most did not return until 1943. Only those with essential jobs stayed. **This is the context for Tracer: the Rock had been emptied of its own people and filled with soldiers and tunnels, and the plan was that if it fell, six men would stay behind inside it.** [Wikipedia, Evacuation of the Gibraltarian civilian population during World War II; Historic UK]
- **June 1940.** France falls. Britain is alone in the Mediterranean. Spain shifts from neutrality to "non-belligerence." Germany's Peripheral Strategy aims to cut Britain from the Empire, and Gibraltar is the western hinge. [Wikipedia, Operation Felix; Wikipedia, Operation Tracer]
- **12 July 1940.** OKW sets up a special planning group for a Gibraltar operation. [Wikipedia, Operation Felix]
- **22 July 1940.** Admiral Wilhelm Canaris travels to Madrid for talks with Franco and War Minister Juan Vigón. [Wikipedia, Operation Felix]
- **24 August 1940.** Hitler approves a general plan to seize Gibraltar. [Wikipedia, Operation Felix]
- **23 October 1940.** Hitler meets Franco at **Hendaye** and proposes Spanish entry into the war by January 1941. Franco does not commit. [Wikipedia, Operation Felix; Warfare History Network]
- **12 November 1940.** **Führer Directive No. 18** issued. Verbatim: *"the aim of German intervention in the Iberian Peninsula (code name Felix) will be to drive the English out of the Western Mediterranean."* [Wikipedia, Operation Felix; Grey Dynamics; BMMHS]
- **5 December 1940.** Hitler decides to request Franco's permission for German troops to cross Spain on **10 January 1941**. [Wikipedia, Operation Felix]
- **7 December 1940.** Canaris meets Franco again. **Franco refuses**, citing Spain's economic collapse and civil war damage. Felix is shelved. [Wikipedia, Operation Felix; Grey Dynamics]
- **February 1941.** OKW advises the operation is "out of the question for the time being." Planning nevertheless continues in various forms (Felix-Heinrich submitted 10 March 1941, then Isabella and Ilona/Gisela) as late as 1944, which is exactly why the British could not treat the threat as dead. [Wikipedia, Operation Felix]

**Operation Tracer**

- **Late summer / August 1941.** Conception. Rear Admiral John Henry Godfrey, DNI, decides to build a covert observation post at Gibraltar that survives the Rock's capture. [ADM 223/464 via Frontline Ulster; Gov of Gibraltar HLMP2/036; Wikipedia]
- **November 1941.** Commander Geoffrey Birley, Senior Officer (Intelligence) Gibraltar, with Chief Engineer Colonel H. M. Fordham RE and his deputy Lieutenant Colonel R. A. Hay RE, reconnoitres the Rock and identifies an existing tunnelled shelter at **Lord Airey's Battery**. Birley writes an elaborate feasibility report. [ADM 223/464 via Frontline Ulster]
- **November / December 1941.** Lord Horder consulted on medical and dietary matters. [Frontline Ulster]
- **1941.** Plasterer **Dennis Woods** plasters the main chamber ceiling and walls and signs his name in pencil beside a doorway, dated 1941. [Gibraltar Heritage Journal Vol 1 Iss 1 (2002) via Frontline Ulster; Gov of Gibraltar HLMP2/036]
- **27 December 1941.** Lieutenant Colonel John Kyme Cordeaux RM, the NID representative inside MI6/SIS, issues a detailed feasibility report. [ADM 223/464 via Frontline Ulster]
- **Late December 1941.** Construction/fit-out under way. [Wikipedia]
- **11 January 1942.** Captain Humphry Sandwith, Deputy Director Signals Division (Y), head of NID 9, reports on Tracer communications. [ADM 223/464 via Frontline Ulster]
- **25 January 1942.** Meeting to discuss the Horder and Levick report. Attended by **Horder, Levick, John Cordeaux and Ian Fleming**. Outcomes: team fixed at **six** (one officer leader, two doctors, three telegraphists); recommendations on exercise, food, alcohol, tobacco, ventilation, sanitation; and the recommendation that **any man who died be embalmed and cemented**. A psychological-suitability rehearsal is proposed, initially suggested for Scotland. [ADM 223/464 via Frontline Ulster; Wikipedia]
- **15 February 1942.** Target completion date for the radio corridor, with communications trials to follow immediately. [ADM 223/464 via Frontline Ulster]
- **17 February 1942.** Second meeting, held at Godfrey's private flat, **36 Curzon Street, Mayfair**, deliberately not at Whitehall. Lieutenant White RNVR proposed as officer in charge. Levick to attend the rehearsals. Recruitment formally begins: one officer, two doctors, three RN telegraphists. Lord Horder to recruit the doctors; Admiral Sir William Whitworth, Second Sea Lord, to provide the officer and the telegraphists. [ADM 223/464 via Frontline Ulster; Wikipedia; Gov of Gibraltar HLMP2/036]
- **1 March 1942.** Godfrey reports on Tracer to the First Sea Lord. [ADM 223/464 via Frontline Ulster]
- **15 March 1942.** Progress report issued, reference NID 001107/42. Not located. [Frontline Ulster]
- **30 April 1942.** Two surgeons and three signalmen selected. **Still no leader.** [ADM 223/464 via Frontline Ulster; Wikipedia]
- **May 1942.** Provisions prepared at the Admiralty. Arrangements begin for equivalent operations at **Colombo and Trincomalee**. [Wikipedia]
- **16 May 1942.** Training begins at **Shotley**, the Royal Navy shore training establishment (HMS Ganges), Shotley Gate, **Suffolk**, near Harwich and Ipswich. [Frontline Ulster; codenames.info. See Myth vs record for the location dispute.]
- **Mid-July 1942.** Anticipated deployment date. [Frontline Ulster]
- **1 August 1942.** The Tracer team deploys to Gibraltar, under the new Senior Officer (Intelligence) Gibraltar, **Commander James Grenville Pyke-Nott**. Each man is given a cover job. Cooper's cover was **dockyard physician and censor of soldiers' letters**. [ADM 223/464 via Frontline Ulster; Wikipedia; Telegraph obituary as summarised in secondary sources]
- **End August 1942.** Construction almost complete, provisions in place. A Tracer manual is printed, disguised as the manual for an **Arctic expedition**. The DNI begins preparing a "shadow" Tracer team. [Wikipedia, citing ADM 223/464]
- **May 1943.** Allied armies complete the capture of North Africa. [Wikipedia]
- **10 July 1943.** Operation Husky, the Allied invasion of Sicily, begins on the night of 9/10 July. [IWM, Operation Husky; US Naval History and Heritage Command]
- **17 August 1943.** The six-week Sicilian campaign ends. US 3rd Division enters Messina at 10:00, about 50 minutes ahead of the British. The last Axis forces are off the island. The threat to Gibraltar is now negligible. [IWM; US Naval History and Heritage Command; Wikipedia]
- **24 August 1943.** The Director of Naval Intelligence sends a **one-time-pad** signal to Gibraltar ordering one final wireless exercise, then the blocking up of the chambers and the distribution of the stores. Tracer is over. [ADM 223/464 via Frontline Ulster; Wikipedia; Gov of Gibraltar HLMP2/036]

**Rediscovery and after**

- **26 December 1996 (Boxing Day) or December 1997.** Members of the **Gibraltar Caving Group** (the Caves and Cliffs Section of the Gibraltar Ornithological and Natural History Society, founded 1976; now the Fortress of Gibraltar Group) feel an unexplained draught from behind rusting corrugated iron lining in the Lord Airey's shelter tunnel, pull back the sheeting, find a brick wall, and break through into the chambers. **Sources conflict on the year, see Myth vs record.** Wikipedia's Gibraltar Caving Group article names four members: **Richard Durrell, Jean Paul Latin, Mark Ainsworth and Ian Bramble**. Partial corroboration that these are real Gibraltar cavers: Durrell, Latin and Ainsworth are credited contributors on a peer-reviewed study of methane in Gibraltar karst air published in *Earth and Planetary Science Letters* (ScienceDirect, S0012821X13002495). Their specific attribution to the Tracer break-in, however, rests on Wikipedia alone. They had been searching for about two and a half years, reasoning that the post had to be high on the Rock to see both the Bay and the Mediterranean, which is how they eliminated Coptic Cave and concentrated on the Lord Airey system. [Wikipedia, Gibraltar Caving Group; Gov of Gibraltar HLMP2/036; Forces News; Frontline Ulster]
- **They kept the location secret for three months while they researched it.** [Wikipedia, citing the 1998 documentary]
- **1998.** A 28-minute documentary, *Operation Tracer - Stay Behind Cave*, is filmed inside the cave. During filming the team turns the brass tap over the wash pit, fed from the 10,000 gallon tank. The water runs black at first, then clear. The film was published publicly in April 2012. [Wikipedia, citing the documentary]
- **September 1998.** **Dennis Woods** returns to Gibraltar for the first time in more than fifty years, identifies himself as having worked on the construction, and is invited by the Gibraltar Museum to tour the chambers. He confirms he plastered the main chamber including the ceiling, for sound insulation. He says the site was known during construction as **"Braithwaite's Cave."** He also states there were **two other Tracer teams in Gibraltar**, his being the main one, and that Tracer units, by then called "Shadow" units, operated during the **Suez Crisis**. [Gov of Gibraltar HLMP2/036; Gibraltar Heritage Journal Vol 1 Iss 1 (2002) via Frontline Ulster; Wikipedia]
- **November 2006.** Bruce Cooper, then 91 or 92, meets **Jim Crone** of discovergibraltar.com and **Sergeant Major Pete Jackson MBE** of the Royal Gibraltar Regiment, senior tunnel guide, and relates his memories of Tracer for the first time. [Gov of Gibraltar HLMP2/036; Wikipedia, Bruce Cooper]
- **4 February 2007.** The *Independent on Sunday* runs Anne Penketh's article. Cooper, aged 92, has broken a silence he had kept since 1941. The *New Zealand Herald* runs it the next day under the headline "He volunteered to be buried alive," 5 February 2007. [thepoormouth.blogspot.com contemporaneous 4 Feb 2007 post summarising the Independent piece; Wikipedia ref "herald"]
- **15 October 2008.** Cooper, aged 93, returns to Gibraltar and **confirms that the Stay Behind Cave found by the cavers is the chamber built for him and his five colleagues.** The visit was organised by documentary filmmaker Martin Nuza. [Gov of Gibraltar HLMP2/036; Wikipedia, Bruce Cooper]
- **3 December 2010.** Cooper dies, aged 96, shortly after his 96th birthday. [Wikipedia, Bruce Cooper]
- **4 January 2011.** *The Daily Telegraph* publishes his obituary, "Surgeon Lieutenant-Commander Bruce Cooper." [cited by Frontline Ulster with date]
- **2011.** The story features on BBC One's *The One Show*. [Wikipedia]
- **June 2012.** The **Earl and Countess of Wessex** (Prince Edward and Sophie) tour the Stay Behind Cave during Gibraltar's Diamond Jubilee celebrations. [Wikipedia]
- **August 2020.** The BBC Travel Show films inside; the Gibraltar National Museum notes "up to 30 people a year can visit this secret location." [BBC Travel via Gibraltar National Museum news, 14 August 2020]

## Key figures

- **Rear Admiral (later Vice Admiral) John Henry Godfrey RN (1888 to 1971)**, Director of Naval Intelligence February 1939 to November 1942. Conceived Tracer, drove it at every level of planning, held the meetings at his own flat rather than the Admiralty, and finally ordered it shut down. Widely held to be Ian Fleming's model for "M." [Frontline Ulster; Wikipedia; Gov of Gibraltar HLMP2/036; his papers are at Churchill Archives Centre, Cambridge]
- **Lieutenant Commander Ian Lancaster Fleming RNVR**, personal assistant to Godfrey, designation **17F**, based in Room 39 at the Admiralty. **Documented attendee of the 25 January 1942 Tracer meeting** per ADM 223/464. That is the extent of his verified role. See Myth vs record.
- **Surgeon Commander George Murray Levick RN (1876 to 1956)**, naval surgeon and Antarctic explorer, veteran of Scott's *Terra Nova* expedition of 1910 to 1913. **Called out of retirement.** He advised on the selection of volunteers, survival, psychology, diet, clothing, exercise, recreation, alcohol, tobacco, ventilation, sanitation, and **the handling and disposal of dead bodies**. He personally recruited Bruce Cooper. He lived with the team during their rehearsals, tracking ships.
  **Why the Admiralty picked him, and this is the strongest human parallel in the story:** in February 1912, Levick and five others of the Terra Nova Northern Party (Victor Campbell, Raymond Priestley, George Abbott, Harry Dickason, Frank Browning) were cut off by pack ice on **Inexpressible Island**. In March 1912 they dug a snow cave roughly **12 ft by 9 ft**, too low to stand up in, and lived in it through the Antarctic winter on scavenged seal and penguin, then walked about 200 miles to Cape Evans, reaching it on 7 November 1912. **All six survived roughly seven months underground.** So the man the Royal Navy called out of retirement to advise on sealing **six men** into a chamber in the Rock of Gibraltar was a man who had already survived exactly that, with exactly that number, in ice. Levick's own words on the place: *"it seemed probable that hell itself would be paved something after the style of Inexpressible Island."* He was **64** when the Navy recalled him for Tracer and gave him the rank of Surgeon Commander.
  One further Levick detail, useful and human, with a caveat. The earliest Gibraltar account reports that Levick *"recalled how he used to hallucinate about walking down a street full of sweet shops, but it was always in the afternoon, and it was half day and early closing."* That is the man the Admiralty put in charge of the psychology of six men going into a hole in a rock. **Caveat:** the same passage says Levick spent those seven months "in an igloo, alone," which is wrong. He was one of six. Use the hallucination, correct the "alone." [Neill Rush, aboutourrock.com, via Wayback; corrected against Wikipedia, Inexpressible Island]
  [Gov of Gibraltar HLMP2/036; codenames.info; Frontline Ulster; Wikipedia, George Murray Levick; Wikipedia, Inexpressible Island; CoolAntarctica, Terra Nova Northern Party; Bonhams lot description of Levick's autograph manuscript on the Northern Party's ice cave]
- **Thomas Jeeves Horder, 1st Baron Horder (1871 to 1955)**, consultant physician to monarchs and prime ministers. Advised on diet and provisions and was tasked with recruiting the two doctors. [Gov of Gibraltar HLMP2/036; Frontline Ulster]
- **Colonel Richard Gambier-Parry (1894 to 1965)**, head of **Section VIII, Secret Intelligence Service**, responsible for the worldwide SIS communications network. Advised on the radio plan and proposed training a wireless technician immediately and sending one of his own men to run trials at Gibraltar with equipment SIS already had there. [Frontline Ulster quoting ADM 223/464; Gov of Gibraltar HLMP2/036]
- **Captain Humphry Sandwith RN**, Deputy Director Signals Division (Y), head of NID 9, the naval Y-service. Reported on Tracer communications. His involvement indicates NID 9 at **RAF/HMS Flowerdown, Hampshire** would have received the Tracer transmissions. [Frontline Ulster]
- **Lieutenant Colonel John Kyme Cordeaux RM**, the Naval Intelligence Division's representative inside MI6/SIS. Issued the SIS feasibility report of 27 December 1941 and attended the 25 January 1942 meeting. **This is the documented MI6 involvement.** [Frontline Ulster quoting ADM 223/464]
- **Admiral Sir William Whitworth**, Second Sea Lord and Chief of Naval Personnel 1941 to 1944. Asked to supply the executive officer and the three telegraphists. [Frontline Ulster]
- **Commander Geoffrey Birley RN**, Senior Officer (Intelligence) Gibraltar during conception. Ran the reconnaissance and picked the site.
- **Commander James Grenville Pyke-Nott RN**, Senior Officer (Intelligence) Gibraltar during the team's deployment.
- **Colonel H. M. Fordham RE**, Chief Engineer Gibraltar 19 Sept 1940 to 14 Dec 1941, and **Lieutenant Colonel Robert Alistair Hay RE**, Deputy Chief Engineer 19 June 1941 to 16 Jan 1944. [britishmilitaryhistory.co.uk Gibraltar Command personnel list, via Frontline Ulster]
- **Charles Langbridge Morgan (1894 to 1958)**, novelist and critic, wrote the post-war official NID history including the three-page TRACER summary. Had no role in the operation itself.
- **Surgeon Lieutenant (later Lieutenant Commander) Bruce Cooper RNVR (1914 to 2010)**, volunteer, and the single most important human source for this story. See below.
- **Surgeon Lieutenant Arthur Milner RNVR**, volunteer. Civilian GP in Morecambe before the war. Reluctant to join the Navy because of seasickness, and was assured he would never have to serve at sea, which is grimly funny given what he actually signed up for.
- **"Windy" Gale**, executive officer and team leader, from Kent. Replaced the original choice.
- **Dennis Woods**, civilian, plastered the main chamber in 1941 and signed his name. Confirmed the site in September 1998.

## The chamber, described

Location: inside the existing tunnelled shelter at **Lord Airey's Battery**, near the southern end of the Upper Rock Nature Reserve, about 1,350 ft up. Coordinates given by Wikipedia as 36 deg 07 min 29 sec N, 5 deg 20 min 36 sec W. TNA **WO 227/30** records that a tunnel called **Lord Airey's Tunnel** was completed in 1941, which matches both the time and the place. [Frontline Ulster]

Layout, from the survey work at the site:
- A camouflaged entrance behind corrugated iron and brick, giving into a rock-cut entrance hall.
- A **second, older entrance permanently sealed with shuttered concrete**, with construction leftovers still stacked against it including **metal tins of cement**, which are thought to be the cement stored for cementing up the dead.
- A patch of **loose soil floor** outside the main chamber door. Two purposes have been proposed: silent footfall, and grave-digging.
- The **main chamber**: plastered walls and ceiling, cork tile floor in two patterns, square tiles around the periphery (thought to mark storage areas) and narrow tiles in herringbone in the centre. The plaster reportedly had **horse dung mixed into it to deaden noise**, per BFBS Forces News. A tap and sump in the corner fed by the 10,000 gallon tank behind the southern wall.
- A **corridor and concrete stairway** up to the toilets, the radio room, and the eastern opening.
- The **radio room**: the only room with clear evidence of actual use. Ceiling of tarred paper and wire mesh over timber planks. Remains of a bench, a shelf, wiring, ceramic and glass insulators, a knife-edge switch, a bakelite light switch, a light bulb fitting, a four-blade cast aluminium belt-driven fan with vibration dampers, ducting, a modified bicycle frame with a sprocket, and hand cranks.
- The **eastern observation post**: the stairs simply stop at a half-height wall with no platform. The aperture is large enough for a man to climb out onto a ledge.
- The **western observation post**: reached up a side tunnel, narrowing, with a step and a carved-out recess so an observer could sit back far enough to use binoculars. The slit is angled toward the harbour.

**Two important as-built realities that the popular telling misses:**
1. **There is effectively only one true observation slit**, the western one, barely 20 mm high. [Frontline Ulster site survey]
2. **The main chamber has no ventilation at all.** With both doors closed there is no air path. There are also no light fittings, no fixtures, no witness marks from furniture, and no cooking or heating provision anywhere in it. The researcher who surveyed it concluded the main chamber was probably **stores**, and that the men would more likely have slept in the upper corridor near the west slit. [Frontline Ulster]

## The absurd and comedic beats, all real and sourced

1. **The chamber was 5,760 cubic feet, and the Admiralty's own top secret summary wrote 57,600.** Off by a factor of ten in the one document that survives. [ADM 223/464 sentence 15]
2. **The first team leader was replaced because he objected to eating dinner with the ratings.** Verbatim from the Government of Gibraltar's official heritage listing: *"the leader soon had to be replaced after he expressed his displeasure at having to dine with the ratings who were to serve as radio operators."* A man was fine with being bricked into a mountain for a year. He was not fine with the seating plan. [Gov of Gibraltar HLMP2/036; Wikipedia sources it to the Telegraph obituary]
3. **The replacement leader's name, as far as history records it, is "Windy" Gale.** [Gov of Gibraltar HLMP2/036]
4. **Doctor number two, Arthur Milner, only joined the Navy after being reassured he would never have to go to sea.** He was then assigned to a hole in a rock with no exit. [Wikipedia]
5. **The Tracer manual was printed disguised as the manual for an Arctic expedition.** [Wikipedia, citing ADM 223/464]
6. **The Admiralty's survival consultant was a man whose qualification was having survived a winter trapped in a snow cave in Antarctica.** Levick was pulled out of retirement specifically for this. [Gov of Gibraltar HLMP2/036; codenames.info]
7. **The most secret operation in the Mediterranean was planned in a flat in Mayfair because Whitehall was considered too leaky.** 36 Curzon Street. [Wikipedia; Frontline Ulster]
8. **The power plan for a year-long intelligence post inside a mountain was a man on a bicycle.** The bicycle's chain was swapped for a leather belt so the enemy would not hear it. Recharging all three batteries to only 50 per cent from the standard Battery Charger No. 2 would take over 72 hours of pedalling. [ADM 223/464 sentence 24; Gov of Gibraltar HLMP2/036; Frontline Ulster calculation]
9. **The aerial plan was to shove an 18-foot metal rod out of a hole in a cliff face and then pull it back in.** Frontline Ulster's assessment is that doing this in daylight "would have likely attracted some attention to the occupant's activities." [ADM 223/464 sentences 25 to 26; Frontline Ulster]
10. **The Rock's prevailing wind, the Levanter, regularly wraps the top of the Rock in thick low cloud between May and October.** On those days the observation post would have seen nothing at all. [Frontline Ulster, using Gibraltar wind data]
11. **The site's construction nickname was "Braithwaite's Cave."** Wikipedia says it honoured a Major J. A. Braithwaite who died in a tunnelling explosion. The surveyor at Frontline Ulster instead identified the 10,000 gallon water tank as a **sectional Braithwaite tank**, a standard British military product. If the second explanation is right, the whole complex was named after a brand of water tank. **Both are in play, see Gaps.**
12. **Two toilets, for six men, purely for redundancy.** If one broke there was no plumber coming. [Forces News; Frontline Ulster]
13. **There were TWO Operation TRACERs, both involving Gibraltar.** The other one, ADM 223/457, was a June 1941 Malta convoy operation flying Hurricanes off Ark Royal and Victorious. [TNA Discovery, ADM 223/457]
14. **The cavers who found it turned the tap in 1998 and the fifty-year-old water tank still ran, black at first, then clear.** [Wikipedia, citing the 1998 documentary]
15. **The 1997/98 explorers punched a hole through a suspicious patch of plaster at the north end of the main chamber to see what was hidden behind it. It was bedrock.** [Frontline Ulster]
16. **On the Rock itself, the plan was not remembered as Operation Tracer. Locals and servicemen called it "Operation Monkey."** [Neill Rush, aboutourrock.com, via Wayback]
17. **When the cavers sat down inside to write up their notes at the western slit, the sunlight coming through the loophole acted as a pinhole camera and projected a live image of the Bay of Gibraltar onto their paper.** The observation post accidentally worked without anyone looking through it. [Neill Rush, aboutourrock.com, via Wayback]
18. **The water system still works.** In 1997 the cavers turned the brass tap and eventually got clear, cold water. They once left it running for six hours, and by the next day the 10,000 gallon tank had refilled itself from the rock. [Neill Rush, aboutourrock.com, via Wayback]

## Human-cost beats, play these completely straight

**Nobody died in Operation Tracer.** The cost here is what six men agreed to, not what happened to them.

1. **There was no way out, and they were told that up front.** The plan was for the men to go in and for the entrance passage to be bricked up behind them, with loose bricks stored on site for exactly that. The men did not hold the key. Release was contingent on the defeat of Germany. The earliest Gibraltar account puts it flatly: *"Potential volunteers were told that if accepted they would be bricked up in the cave and that they would have no way out."* [Wikipedia; Gov of Gibraltar HLMP2/036; Neill Rush, aboutourrock.com, via Wayback]

2. **They were told it would be about a year, and that it could be much longer.** The archive says a year. The men understood it as a floor, not a ceiling. [ADM 223/464 sentence 9; Gov of Gibraltar HLMP2/036]

3. **A protocol existed for their own bodies, and they knew it.** The 25 January 1942 meeting formally recommended *"the disposal by embalming and cementing up of anyone who died."* Levick, the survival consultant, briefed them on "the handling of dead bodies" as one of his listed subjects, alongside diet and exercise. Tins of cement were stored in the entrance chamber and are still there. Six men were told, in advance, what the survivors would do with them. [ADM 223/464, quoted phrase via Frontline Ulster; Gov of Gibraltar HLMP2/036 for Levick's brief]

4. **The screening was psychological.** The January 1942 meeting proposed a rehearsal specifically to assess psychological suitability, and Levick, whose expertise was small groups trapped in confined spaces for months, lived alongside the team through those rehearsals. This was not a paperwork exercise. Somebody sat down and asked whether these particular men would survive each other. [ADM 223/464 via Frontline Ulster; Gov of Gibraltar HLMP2/036]

5. **Bruce Cooper was told nothing about the mission until after he had agreed to it.** He was summoned to see Surgeon Commander Levick and told only that they were looking for a doctor "to do something special." He said yes first and found out second. [Independent on Sunday, 4 Feb 2007, as summarised contemporaneously; Wikipedia]

6. **He then recruited his friend.** Asked to name a second doctor, Cooper suggested Arthur Milner, a civilian GP in Morecambe he had trained with at Durham. Cooper is the reason Milner was in that chamber's plan at all. [Wikipedia]

7. **They lived in Gibraltar under cover for a year, doing ordinary jobs, waiting to be sealed in.** Cooper's cover was dockyard physician and letter censor. Every day from 1 August 1942 was a day on which the order might come. [Wikipedia; Frontline Ulster; Telegraph obituary via secondary summary]

8. **The physical reality of the place is worse than the plan sounds.** Gibraltar's tunnel humidity ran 90 to 95 per cent. The official tunnelling report noted that furniture stored in unlined underground huts "has grown fungus," and that in fully occupied chambers under siege conditions "the body heat of occupants produces an intolerable condition within a few hours." The Tracer main chamber has **no ventilation whatsoever** when its doors are closed, no heating, no light fittings, and no cooking facility. The sewage from the two toilets drained into a crevice in the corner of the room they were meant to live in. [TNA WO 227/30 via Frontline Ulster; Frontline Ulster site survey]

9. **They never got to tell anyone.** Cooper kept the secret for **sixty-five years**, from 1941 until November 2006. He first spoke publicly in February 2007, aged 92. He was the last one left. [Gov of Gibraltar HLMP2/036; Independent on Sunday 4 Feb 2007; Wikipedia]

10. **A death is attached to the construction, if the Wikipedia account is right.** Major J. A. Braithwaite is said to have led the men who built the facility and to have been killed by an accidental explosion while tunnelling, with the site named Braithwaite's Cave in his honour. **This is single-source and I could not independently verify it.** Treat with care, see Gaps.

11. **Cooper stood in the chamber again on 15 October 2008, aged 93.** He did not recognise it at first. Then he did, and confirmed it was the place. He died two years later, on 3 December 2010, aged 96. [Gov of Gibraltar HLMP2/036; Frontline Ulster; Wikipedia]

## The pre-Cooper Gibraltar account (recovered from the Wayback Machine)

This matters because it is the state of local knowledge **before Bruce Cooper came forward in November 2006**, and it is the source Wikipedia leans on for several claims. Retrieved from the Internet Archive capture of 19 May 2012 of **Neill Rush, "Operation Tracer - Stay Behind Cave," aboutourrock.com** (site now defunct). Treat as Tier 2 local reporting, valuable but uncited, and demonstrably wrong in places.

What it adds:

- **The local Gibraltarian name for the plan was "Operation Monkey."** Verbatim: *"Recollections and rumours from locals and servicemen about 'Operation Tracer' or 'Operation Monkey' (as it was locally known,) have persisted through the years about this potentially suicidal World War Two plan."* Excellent, and I found this nowhere else.
- **The four cavers, named and dated to 1997**: *"One day, back in 1997, the members of the Gibraltar Caving Group, Mark Ainsworth, Ian Bramble, Richard Durrell and Jean-Paul Latin were high in the tunnels when they noticed a draught coming from one of the tunnels walls."* This is the origin of the four names and it says **1997**, not 1996. It also confirms the three-month secrecy.
- **What the volunteers were told, stated plainly**: *"Potential volunteers were told that if accepted they would be bricked up in the cave and that they would have no way out."*
- **Levick was 64** when the Navy recalled him and gave him the rank of Surgeon Commander.
- **The water tank still worked in 1997.** The tap ran thick sludge, then clear cool water. On one occasion the group left it running for six hours and *"the following day the tank was found to be full again,"* refilled from a catchment high in the Rock.
- **A real, small, beautiful detail:** while the group were trying to write notes at the western observation post, light through the slit fell on the paper and, acting as a pinhole camera, projected an image of the Bay of Gibraltar onto it.
- **Independent documentary thread for the two doctors' names.** Written before Cooper went public, the article says the men's names are not known, but that *"Surgeon Lieutenant's Cooper and Milner of the RNVR could have possibly been involved, as they are listed as being attached to HMS Cormorant on the orders of the First Sea Lord."* HMS Cormorant was the Royal Navy shore establishment at Gibraltar. That is a naval posting record pointing at Cooper and Milner **years before Cooper confirmed it himself**, which is meaningful corroboration.
- **It confirms Shotley, Suffolk** as the training location: *"Others were trained at the Naval School in Shotley, Suffolk before being sent to Gibraltar with 'Proper Jobs' as a cover."*
- **It is the origin of the Major Braithwaite story**: *"also sometimes referred to as Braithwaite's Cave after Major Braithwaite who led the team building the complex, and who in fact died when a tunneling charge went off accidentally."* Still uncited, still unverified, but now I know where the claim entered the record and that it was tunnel-tour lore told to visitors for years before the cave was found.

Where this article is demonstrably **wrong**, and worth knowing so the writer does not repeat it:
- It gives the team as *"an Officer, a Surgeon and four Signalers."* The archive says one officer, **two** doctors, **three** telegraphists.
- It says Levick *"spent seven months in an igloo, alone."* He was not alone. He was one of **six** men in an ice cave on Inexpressible Island. The "seven months" is right.
- It attributes the stand-down to *"the pressure on the new 'Eastern Front' and the redeployment of the German Forces."* The record ties it to North Africa and Sicily.
- It states *"No manuals or papers on the operation have ever been found, indeed if they ever existed."* Written before ADM 223/464 was widely known.

**The lime bath.** This article records the tunnel-tour version of the death protocol: *"It was said that a bath of lime would be installed, so that if any of the servicemen died they would be placed in the bath, which would dissolve their remains and eliminate any smell. When the final occupant of the cave met the same fate it would not really matter."* **This is tour-guide lore, not the record.** The archive says embalming and cementing up. The article itself notes that the location shown to tourists was the wrong one and would not have had the required field of view. Use the lime bath only as an example of the legend that grew around the story, never as fact.

**The origin of the "self-heating soup" and "appendix and tonsils removed" claims.** Both appear here, in this pre-2006 local article, and both are unsourced: *"all who would have had their appendix and tonsils removed before going into the cave"* and *"food would have consisted of something like chemically heated soups, dried rations and powdered foods."* Note carefully that this article does **not** attribute either to Ian Fleming, and it hedges both with "would have" and "something like." History Hit later hardened the soup into a Fleming invention. Both claims remain unverified.

## Myth vs record (corrections, each sourced)

**1. "Supplies for seven years."**
- Popular telling: the chamber was stocked with seven years of provisions.
- Actual: the surviving Admiralty summary, ADM 223/464, specifies stores and water to maintain the party **for a year**. The seven-year figure appears in the Government of Gibraltar's heritage listing and in Wikipedia but is **not traceable to the archival record**. The researcher who transcribed and analysed ADM 223/464 states directly: *"I have not found anything about 7 years in the records,"* and notes the sheer tonnage that seven years of rations for six men would require. The Telegraph obituary of Cooper, drawing on Cooper himself, says **"enough supplies for a year."**
- **Best handling on air:** the plan was for a year, with the men told it could run longer. If the seven-year figure is used, attribute it as a later claim, possibly originating in Cooper's oral testimony, and say plainly that the surviving document says one year.
- [ADM 223/464 sentence 9; Frontline Ulster; Gov of Gibraltar HLMP2/036; Telegraph obituary]

**2. "Ian Fleming built it / the James Bond author's secret Gibraltar hideout."**
- Popular telling: headlines such as History Hit's "The Secret Gibraltar Hideout Built by Bond author Ian Fleming."
- Actual: Fleming was Godfrey's personal assistant, designation **17F**, in Room 39. His **only documented connection to Tracer** is attendance at the meeting of **25 January 1942** to discuss the Horder and Levick report, recorded in ADM 223/464. He did not conceive it, did not run it, and did not build it. Godfrey did. The National WWII Museum's account of Fleming's naval intelligence career lists Mincemeat, 30 Assault Unit and T-Force, and **does not mention Tracer or Gibraltar at all**.
- **Also correct the task brief's own premise:** Tracer was not a **Section 17M** operation. **NID 17M was Ewen Montagu's section**, named for Montagu, housed in Room 13, handling naval Ultra material, and it is where Operation Mincemeat came from. Fleming was 17F. Do not conflate them.
- [ADM 223/464 via Frontline Ulster; National WWII Museum; Wikipedia, Ewen Montagu; History Hit for the myth itself]

**3. "Fleming invented gadgets for it, including self-heating soup," and "all the volunteers had their tonsils and appendixes removed."**
- **UNVERIFIED, and now traced to its origin.** Both claims appear in the pre-2006 local Gibraltar article by Neill Rush at aboutourrock.com, uncited and hedged ("all who **would have** had their appendix and tonsils removed", food "**would have consisted of something like** chemically heated soups"). Neither appears in ADM 223/464 as transcribed. Crucially, **the original article does not attribute either to Ian Fleming.** History Hit later converted the hedged soup into a hard claim that Fleming personally "devised a number of Bond-worthy gadgets, such as self-heating soup." That attribution appears to be an invention downstream of the source. **Do not use without flagging as unverified, and never credit the soup to Fleming.**
- [aboutourrock.com via Wayback Machine, 19 May 2012 capture; History Hit for the hardened Fleming version]

**4. "The single official document mentioning Operation Tracer."**
- See the detailed treatment above. Defensible as a statement about surviving narrative accounts and about the TNA catalogue. **Not** defensible as a claim that nothing else was ever written. At least five other Tracer documents are referenced inside the record itself and are simply unlocated. Say "the only surviving account," not "the only document ever written."

**5. The discovery year: 1996 or 1997?**
- **Genuine, unresolved conflict.** Wikipedia's *Gibraltar Caving Group* article and BFBS Forces News say **December 1996**, Forces News specifying **Boxing Day, 26 December 1996**. The Government of Gibraltar's official listing, Wikipedia's *Operation Tracer* article headline text, and virtually all secondary retellings say **1997**. Frontline Ulster says **Christmas 1997**. Wikipedia's Operation Tracer article is internally inconsistent, saying "in late 1996" in one sentence and "December 1997" three paragraphs later. **The earliest local Gibraltar account, Neill Rush at aboutourrock.com, which is the source that names all four cavers, says 1997.** That tilts the balance toward 1997, or toward a Boxing Day 1996 first break-in followed by the real exploration in 1997.
- **Best handling:** say the cavers broke in over the Christmas period at the turn of 1996 into 1997, that they kept it quiet for about three months while they researched it, and that the site is officially dated to 1997 by the Government of Gibraltar. Do not assert a single day.

**6. "They stumbled on it by accident."**
- Popular telling: a chance gust of wind on a Levanter day led them to it.
- The draught is in every account including the Government of Gibraltar's. But the surveyor who has been inside and read the archive material argues the break-in was targeted, not accidental: the group had already narrowed the search over two and a half years by reasoning the post must be high enough to see both the Bay and the Mediterranean, they had eliminated Coptic Cave, they had concentrated on the Lord Airey system, and archive material had by then been released at the Public Record Office. His words: *"I believe the caving group had worked out from the released archive materials where to search. There does not seem anything meticulous about their search."*
- **Handling:** the draught is real. "Pure accident" is not. They knew roughly where to look.
- [Gov of Gibraltar HLMP2/036; Gibraltar Heritage Journal Vol 1 Iss 1 2002; Frontline Ulster]

**7. Cooper's cloak-and-dagger hotel routine.**
- Popular telling, from the Telegraph obituary: Cooper entered the Rock Hotel by the front door in RNVR surgeon-lieutenant's uniform and left by the back door dressed as an Army sergeant, then vanished into a tunnel to train.
- Partially corrected: there genuinely was access to the military tunnel system from the rear of the Rock Hotel, but that entrance led into the **Brigade Headquarters system, nowhere near the Tracer cave.** The likelier and duller explanation offered by the surveyor is that Cooper wore khaki battledress to avoid being saluted constantly as a naval officer on his way to the underground hospital, and there is a press photograph of him apparently taken there. **Use with a caveat, or skip.**
- [Telegraph obituary 4 Jan 2011; Frontline Ulster analysis]

**8. "Training at Romney Marsh."**
- Wikipedia says the team rehearsed at **Romney Marsh**, southern England, after initial training. Frontline Ulster's timeline says training began **16 May 1942 at Shotley**, though it misplaces Shotley in "Sussex." codenames.info says **Shotley naval school in Suffolk**. Shotley Gate, Suffolk, is the site of **HMS Ganges**, the RN shore training establishment, near Harwich and the Stour and Orwell estuaries. The Government of Gibraltar listing says Levick lived with the team during exercises **"tracking ships along the Thames Estuary,"** which fits Shotley and does not fit Romney Marsh.
- **Best handling:** Shotley, Suffolk, is the better-supported location for the training. Note that Wikipedia's Romney Marsh claim exists. Frontline Ulster's "Sussex" is simply an error.
- [Wikipedia; Frontline Ulster; codenames.info; Wikipedia, RNTE Shotley / HMS Ganges; Gov of Gibraltar HLMP2/036]

**9. "Allied forces invaded Sicily on 17 August 1943."**
- The Government of Gibraltar's own heritage listing says *"On August 17, 1943, Allied forces invaded Sicily and captured the island."* **That is wrong.** Operation Husky **began** on the night of 9/10 July 1943. **17 August 1943 is the day the six-week campaign ended**, when the US 3rd Division entered Messina. The listing has conflated the start and the end. Wikipedia's Operation Tracer article gets it right.
- [IWM, Operation Husky; US Naval History and Heritage Command; Gov of Gibraltar HLMP2/036 for the error]

**10. "Two observation posts with wide slits."**
- Planned: two apertures, each 12 in x 6 in. As built: **one true concealed slit, on the west, about 15 cm x 1.5 cm**, and on the east a larger natural opening onto a ledge. The plan and the reality diverged significantly.
- [ADM 223/464 sentence 17 vs Gov of Gibraltar HLMP2/036 and Frontline Ulster site survey]

**11. "The men would have been buried under the floor of the main chamber."**
- The record says only *"the disposal by embalming and cementing up of anyone who died."* The soil floor in the entrance passage is a **speculation** about where that would have happened, not a documented instruction. Wikipedia phrases it carefully: "the entrance passage had loose soil, to facilitate burials if needed." The surveyor argues the soil was more plausibly for silent footfall and that embalmed bodies would have been encased in concrete elsewhere, possibly in coffins.
- **Handling:** embalming and cementing is documented. The exact location is not. Say so.
- [ADM 223/464 via Frontline Ulster; Wikipedia; Frontline Ulster analysis]

**12. "Dennis Woods, the telegraph operator."**
- He is described in different sources as a **telegraph operator** and as the **plasterer** who plastered the main chamber and signed his name. Both descriptions circulate. Frontline Ulster flags the inconsistency and doubts he was employed in Gibraltar as both. Attribute carefully: what is documented is that he worked on the construction, plastered the main chamber, signed and dated it 1941, and returned in September 1998 to confirm it.
- [Gov of Gibraltar HLMP2/036; Gibraltar Heritage Journal 2002; War History Online; Frontline Ulster]

## Why it never happened

Two answers, and both are needed.

**The immediate answer:** Tracer was stood down on **24 August 1943**, when the Director of Naval Intelligence signalled Gibraltar by one-time pad to run one final wireless exercise, block up the chambers, and distribute the stores. The trigger was the collapse of the Mediterranean threat: North Africa was completely in Allied hands by May 1943, and the last Axis forces were driven out of Sicily on **17 August 1943**, one week before the signal. [ADM 223/464 via Frontline Ulster; Wikipedia; Gov of Gibraltar HLMP2/036; IWM for the Sicily dates]

**The underlying answer:** the Germans never came, because Franco never let them. Hitler could not take Gibraltar without crossing Spain, and at Hendaye on **23 October 1940** and again through Canaris on **7 December 1940** Franco declined. Operation Felix went from directive to staff study to shelf. [Wikipedia, Operation Felix]

**Was the chamber ever occupied?** Not as an operational post. No. But it was **not completely untouched**: the radio room is the only room in the complex with clear evidence of use, consistent with the communications trials Gambier-Parry pushed for from early 1942 and with the final wireless exercise ordered on 24 August 1943. The surveyor's conclusion: *"Perhaps the only occupied part of the cave as we find it was the radio room where the initial and final communication tests were carried out."* He also concludes the complex was probably **never fully furnished and stocked** ready to go at a moment's notice, which cuts against the "sealed with seven years of food waiting inside" image. [Frontline Ulster]

## Related stay-behind schemes

- **Within Tracer itself.** ADM 223/464 states the design was *"to establish first at Gibraltar and, if possible, in other places such as Malta and Aden, secret observation posts which could be maintained even if the places in which they were situated should fall into the hands of the enemy."* By May 1942 arrangements had begun for **Colombo and Trincomalee** (Ceylon). Contemporary Telegraph reporting from the 1997/98 Public Record Office release listed **Colombo, Trincomalee, Malta and Aden**. codenames.info lists the same four. **Gibraltar's is the only one known to have been built.** [ADM 223/464 via Frontline Ulster; Wikipedia; codenames.info; Telegraph via thepoormouth 4 Feb 2007]
- **A "shadow" Tracer team** was being prepared by the DNI from late August 1942. [Wikipedia, citing ADM 223/464]
- **Dennis Woods claimed in 1998** there were two other Tracer teams in Gibraltar and that the units, renamed "Shadow" units, operated during the **Suez Crisis**. A former GCHQ telegraphist reportedly said the same. An MI6-sourced sketch of a different chamber has been described. Together these have fed a long-running belief in a **second, undiscovered chamber** in Gibraltar, reinforced by the fact that the known cave **cannot see northern Gibraltar** and does not fully match Cooper's own description of the chamber he was shown in 1942. **All of this is unverified.** [Gov of Gibraltar HLMP2/036; Wikipedia; Forces News; comment by a discovergibraltar.com researcher, 2007]
- **Distinct from the British Auxiliary Units** (the so-called Churchill's Secret Army, formed 1940 for sabotage and resistance in a German-occupied Britain). Tracer was **not** a resistance or sabotage unit. It was an unarmed Admiralty observation post whose sole job was reporting shipping. There is no evidence it had any offensive role. This distinction is worth making explicitly, because "stay-behind cave" invites the wrong comparison. [Frontline Ulster; British Resistance Archive]

## Status today

- Listed by the **Government of Gibraltar, Ministry for Heritage**, under the Heritage and Antiquities Act, category 2 Monuments, Upper Rock, as **"Stay Behind Cave complex," reference HLMP2/036**.
- The wider **Lord Airey's Shelter** tunnel system remains **Ministry of Defence** property. The Tracer chambers themselves are under the **guardianship of the Gibraltar National Museum**.
- **Access is strictly limited.** The Gibraltar National Museum runs guided tours subject to *"a strict annual quota of 30 visitors,"* groups of no more than five, year-round Monday to Friday weather permitting, usually in the afternoon, by prior arrangement, with a waiting list, first come first served. Tour duration 1 to 1.5 hours.
- **Preservation is minimal.** The surveyor who visited in 2023 reports that **no conservation work has been undertaken**, no archaeological survey has been carried out, and the complex continues to deteriorate as moisture penetrates the fabric. Comparison photographs from 1997/98 against 2023 show significant decay of the timber. Some artefacts, including the leather strap believed to have replaced the bicycle chain, have been removed to the museum for safe storage. One Elsan toilet bowl is broken.
- The Earl and Countess of Wessex toured it in June 2012.
- [Gov of Gibraltar HLMP2/036; Gibraltar National Museum Guided Tours page; Frontline Ulster 2024 visit report; Wikipedia]

- **NOT FOUND: corroboration for the tonsils-and-appendix removal claim and the Fleming "self-heating soup" gadget claim.** Single source, History Hit, uncited.
- **NOT FOUND: any Imperial War Museums holding specific to Operation Tracer or Bruce Cooper.** IWM images cited by researchers (IWM GM63, IWM HU56739) are general Gibraltar and SOE battery-charger images, not Tracer material.
- **UNRESOLVED CONFLICT: 1996 vs 1997** for the rediscovery. See Myth vs record 5.
- **UNRESOLVED CONFLICT: chamber length**, 45 ft (13.7 m) in the 1942 archive versus approximately 18 m as measured today by the Gibraltar authorities. Present both.
- **UNRESOLVED: whether a second Tracer chamber exists in Gibraltar.** Multiple claims, no confirmation.
- **NOT VERIFIED: the claim that the team "lived under cover for two and a half years"** which appears in one 2007 summary. The documented deployment window is 1 August 1942 to 24 August 1943, about one year. Use one year.
- **NOT FOUND: any academic-press book, peer-reviewed article, or credentialed-historian monograph treating Operation Tracer.** This matters and the writer should know it. The entire published literature on Tracer consists of (a) three pages of a post-war official Naval Intelligence Division history, (b) a 2002 local heritage-journal article, (c) a Government of Gibraltar heritage listing, (d) a handful of 2007 to 2011 newspaper pieces built on one man's memory, and (e) a self-published 2024 site survey. **There is no scholarly layer above that.** Everything else online is derivative. This is why the myths propagate so cleanly, and it is why the corrections above matter.
