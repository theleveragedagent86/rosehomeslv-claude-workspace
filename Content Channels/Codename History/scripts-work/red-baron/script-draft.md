## ACT 1: THE WORST DELIVERY BOY IN GERMANY (0:40 - 2:15)

**[VISUAL: Cartoon map: Prussia. A little aristocrat's crest. Baby with a tiny hunting rifle.]**

**NARRATOR:** Manfred von Richthofen was born in 1892 into Prussian nobility, which meant his childhood was basically three activities: riding horses, hunting things, and being told he was better than everyone. He was extremely good at all three.

**[VISUAL: Young Manfred in cavalry uniform on a proud horse, lance in hand. Sabre gleams. Dramatic pose.]**

**NARRATOR:** So when the war started in 1914, he rode off as a cavalry officer. Lance. Sabre. Glory.

**[VISUAL: Same pose, but now facing: trenches, barbed wire, machine guns, mud. The horse looks at the camera with concern.]**

**NARRATOR:** There was just one problem. This war had machine guns, barbed wire, and four hundred miles of trenches. Which meant the cavalry, the most prestigious branch of the German army, was now completely useless.

**[VISUAL: Manfred, dismounted, sulking, carrying a crate of eggs and a wheel of cheese past bored soldiers. A clipboard reads "SUPPLY DUTY."]**

**NARRATOR:** Manfred got reassigned to supply duty. The future deadliest pilot in history spent early 1915 running telephone wire and organizing food. So he wrote a letter to his commanding officer. And this letter is real, and it is fantastic.

**[VISUAL: The letter, wax seal, elegant handwriting, one line highlighted: "I have not gone to war in order to collect cheese and eggs."]**

**NARRATOR:** Quote: "I have not gone to war in order to collect cheese and eggs, but for another purpose." That other purpose being, and I am paraphrasing here, the exact opposite of cheese.

**[VISUAL: An aeroplane sputters overhead. Manfred looks up. Eyes go wide. Sparkle.]**

**NARRATOR:** The army, incredibly, said yes. In May 1915, Richthofen transferred to the flying service. And within a year, he would meet the man who would turn him into a legend. Right before dying in front of him.

---

## ACT 2: THE APPRENTICE (2:15 - 3:50)

**[VISUAL: A train dining car. Manfred, starstruck, leaning over a table toward Oswald Boelcke: composed, Pour le Merite at his neck, coffee in hand.]**

**NARRATOR:** On a train in October 1915, Manfred ran into Oswald Boelcke, Germany's first great ace and the most famous pilot alive. Manfred asked him, essentially, "how do you shoot them down?" Boelcke's answer was basically: get close, aim well, don't miss. Revolutionary stuff.

**[VISUAL: Manfred crashing a training plane nose-first into a field. Instructor facepalms.]**

**NARRATOR:** Inspired, Manfred trained as a pilot, and crashed on his first solo flight. Keep that in mind whenever you feel bad about learning something new.

**[VISUAL: Boelcke, clipboard, pointing at Manfred in a lineup of hopeful pilots. Manfred beams.]**

**NARRATOR:** But Boelcke saw something in him. In 1916 he hand-picked Richthofen for his elite new fighter squadron. Boelcke had written the Dicta Boelcke, the first rulebook of air combat. Attack with the sun behind you. Never run. Fire only at close range. Manfred didn't just learn the rules. He worshipped them.

**[VISUAL: Manfred's first kill: a British two-seater going down. Manfred lands, orders something from a jeweler's catalog.]**

**NARRATOR:** On September 17th, 1916, Richthofen scored his first confirmed kill. And then he did something extremely Manfred. He ordered a silver trophy cup from a jeweler in Berlin to commemorate it. One cup. Per kill. Hold that thought, because this jeweler is going to have a very busy year.

**[VISUAL: Boelcke's funeral. Rows of pilots, heads bowed. TONE: STRAIGHT.]**

**NARRATOR:** Then, that October, Boelcke died. Not shot down. His wing clipped a friendly plane mid-fight, in a war where pilots were not given parachutes. He was twenty-five years old. Germany went into mourning. And his student made a decision: if talent dies by accident, then discipline would have to be perfect.

## ACT 3: THE RED PLANE (3:50 - 5:50)

**[VISUAL: Dogfight: Manfred versus a British ace, circling each other, a literal chess board spinning between them.]**

**NARRATOR:** A month later, Richthofen proved he was the heir. He fought a long spiraling duel with Lanoe Hawker, Britain's first fighter ace with a Victoria Cross, a man the Germans called "the British Boelcke." Richthofen won. That made eleven.

**[VISUAL: January 1917. A hangar. Mechanics stare in horror as Manfred, deadpan, gestures at his Albatros, now painted BRIGHT RED. One mechanic holds a tiny paint sample of sensible camouflage green.]**

**NARRATOR:** And in January 1917, with his kill count climbing and a squadron of his own to command, Manfred von Richthofen did the single most metal thing of the entire war. In an age when every pilot desperately tried to be invisible, he painted his plane bright, blazing, unmissable red.

**[VISUAL: British trench. Two soldiers looking up in terror. One points: "THAT'S HIM."]**

**NARRATOR:** This was not camouflage. This was an announcement. I am here. You can all see me. It will not help you.

**[VISUAL: Squadron of red-nosed, red-splashed Albatros fighters lined up. Each pilot adds red paint, one shrugging: "if he's going to be a target, we all are."]**

**NARRATOR:** His pilots painted their planes red too, partly in style, partly so their boss wasn't the only giant target in the sky. The British started calling him the Red Baron. The name stuck for a hundred years and counting.

**[VISUAL: Calendar page: APRIL 1917. It's dripping red. TONE: STRAIGHT for the second half of this beat.]**

**NARRATOR:** Then came April 1917. The month the Royal Flying Corps still calls Bloody April. Richthofen shot down over twenty aircraft in thirty days. On the other side of those numbers: new British pilots were arriving at the front with barely twenty hours of flying experience, and in that month, many were dead within weeks. The Red Baron was the best pilot in a sky full of teenagers.

**[VISUAL: The Berlin jeweler, buried in paperwork, holding cup number 60. A sign: "OUT OF SILVER."]**

**NARRATOR:** Remember the cups? By 1917 he had sixty of them. Then blockaded Germany ran out of silver. The jeweler offered to keep making them in cheap metal. Manfred, a man of standards, simply stopped collecting. The kills continued. The cups did not.

**[VISUAL: Fast montage: JG 1's colorful planes next to circus tents on a train. A ringmaster's whip crack.]**

**NARRATOR:** By summer he commanded Jagdgeschwader 1, four squadrons packed into tents and trains, moving up and down the front like a traveling show, every plane painted like candy. The Allies called it the Flying Circus. It was the most dangerous circus in history.

**[VISUAL: Freeze. Zoom slowly toward one date on a calendar: JULY 6, 1917.]**

**NARRATOR:** He was twenty-five, famous, untouchable, and precisely on schedule to survive the war. Then came July 6th, 1917. The day the Red Baron's story secretly ended, ten months before his death.

## ACT 4: THE CRACK (5:50 - 7:20)

**[VISUAL: Dogfight with a British two-seater. A tracer line clips the red pilot's head. The world goes white. TONE: STRAIGHT.]**

**NARRATOR:** In a fight with a British two-seater, a bullet grazed Richthofen's skull. For a moment he went blind, in the air, at altitude, in a plane with no parachute. Sight came back just in time for him to crash-land in a field.

**[VISUAL: Hospital. Head wrapped. The famous photo pose. A doctor holds a chart: "DO NOT FLY."]**

**NARRATOR:** The wound never really healed. Splitting headaches. Nausea after flights. People around him said he came back different: quieter, darker, colder. Doctors told him to stop flying. He was back in a cockpit in weeks.

**[VISUAL: German high command, sweating, sliding a desk job across the table. Manfred slides it back.]**

**NARRATOR:** Here's the thing. Germany knew he was worth more alive. He was the most famous man in the country after the Kaiser. They begged him to retire and train pilots. He refused, again and again, saying he could not sit safe while every man in the trenches had no choice. Whatever else he was, he meant that.

**[VISUAL: Manfred at a desk at night, writing. Pages of his own memoir crossed out. TONE: STRAIGHT.]**

**NARRATOR:** He'd written a cocky autobiography in 1917, because the propaganda office made him. By 1918 he disowned it, writing that he was "no longer that insolent fellow," and that after every battle he was, quote, "in wretched spirits." The man in the red plane kept flying anyway. By April 1918, his score stood at eighty confirmed victories. Nobody in the war would beat it.

**[VISUAL: The manual page again: "NEVER chase an enemy low over his own lines." Slow zoom.]**

**NARRATOR:** Which brings us back to that morning over the Somme, and the one question that matters. Why did the most disciplined pilot alive break his own rule?

## ACT 5: SIXTY SECONDS (7:20 - 9:00)

**[VISUAL: Morning, April 21, 1918. Grey-blue sky. Camels and triplanes tangled in a huge dogfight over the Somme. TONE: STRAIGHT from here to the end of the act.]**

**NARRATOR:** That Sunday morning, Richthofen's Circus collided with Sopwith Camels of the RAF's 209 Squadron. In the middle of it: a Canadian rookie named Wilfrid May, on his first combat patrol, under orders to stay out of the fight. His guns jammed. He ran for home.

**[VISUAL: The red triplane latching onto the fleeing Camel. Down, down, to treetop level along the river.]**

**NARRATOR:** The Red Baron picked him. And instead of one clean pass, he followed. Down to the deck, across the river Somme, over Australian infantry lines, into a headwind that slowed him to a crawl. Everything his own manual said never to do.

**[VISUAL: A second Camel diving steeply from above and behind: Roy Brown, May's old school friend from Canada.]**

**NARRATOR:** May's squadron mate and childhood friend, Captain Roy Brown, dove to save him. He fired one burst at the red triplane, then pulled away. Remember that burst. The whole mystery hangs on it.

**[VISUAL: Ground view: Australian machine gunners swinging their guns up. Sergeant at a Vickers gun on the ridge. Riflemen. Everyone firing at the low red plane.]**

**NARRATOR:** Because the chase kept going. Along Morlancourt Ridge, every Australian gun in range opened up on the slowest, lowest, most famous red aircraft in the world.

**[VISUAL: The triplane wobbles. Swings right. Settles into a rough but controlled landing in a field by the road. Silence. TONE: STRAIGHT.]**

**NARRATOR:** One bullet hit him. One. It came up through the right side of his chest and did not come out clean. Somehow, dying, he put the plane down in a beet field, mostly intact. Australian soldiers reached the cockpit within moments. By several accounts he managed one last word: "kaputt." Manfred von Richthofen was dead at twenty-five.

**[VISUAL: Soldiers swarming the red triplane, stripping fabric, instruments, splinters. It vanishes piece by piece.]**

**NARRATOR:** His plane was gone within hours, stripped to the skeleton by souvenir hunters. The most feared machine of the war ended as pocket souvenirs. And then the argument started.

## ACT 6: THE WRONG MAN GOT THE CREDIT (9:00 - 10:20)

**[VISUAL: RAF officers pinning credit on Roy Brown's chest. Brown looks uncomfortable.]**

**NARRATOR:** The RAF officially credited Roy Brown. One problem. Actually, three problems.

**[VISUAL: Diagram: side view of the triplane. Brown's attack line from above-behind-LEFT. The wound path drawn: entering the RIGHT side, angled upward. The two lines glaringly don't match.]**

**NARRATOR:** Problem one: geometry. Brown attacked from behind, above, and the left. The bullet that killed Richthofen entered low on the right side of his chest, traveling upward. Problem two: timing. After Brown's single burst, the Baron kept flying and fighting for around two more minutes. Doctors who have studied the wound agree on the brutal math: with that injury, he had seconds of useful consciousness, not minutes.

**[VISUAL: The ridge. Sergeant Cedric Popkin at his Vickers gun, tracking the plane as it banks toward him, right side exposed.]**

**NARRATOR:** Problem three: there was someone whose position fits. As the triplane banked over the ridge, Sergeant Cedric Popkin, an Australian machine gunner, fired at its exposed right side, at exactly the window the medicine allows. A century of forensic reconstructions, including studies in the late nineties and two thousands, keep landing on the same conclusion. The deadliest fighter pilot of the First World War was almost certainly killed by an infantryman. From the ground. Brown, for his part, never celebrated the credit, and rarely spoke of it.

**[VISUAL: Back to the manual, the circled rule. Then the bandaged head from Act 4, ghosted behind the final chase.]**

**NARRATOR:** So why was he even there to be shot? An unusual east wind that day quietly pushed the whole fight over Allied lines. And modern researchers point at that head wound: damage that can erode judgment and lock a hunter onto his prey. Target fixation, in the one man who taught the world never to fixate. The Red Baron didn't just die that morning. First, for sixty seconds, he stopped being the Red Baron.
