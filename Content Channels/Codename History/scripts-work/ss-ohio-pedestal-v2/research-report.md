# Research Report: SS Ohio and Operation Pedestal, August 1942

Research date: 23 August 2026. Wave 1, independent re-verification of a prior report. Every seed claim was re-checked. Corrections are flagged inline and collected in "Seed corrections".

Anchor primary source used throughout: **Vice-Admiral E. N. Syfret's official Report on Operation "Pedestal", HMS Nelson, 25 August 1942 (TNA ADM 116/4633)**, transcribed at Malta War Diary, plus the **Royal Navy Naval Historical Branch Information Brief** by Dr Malcolm Llewellyn-Jones (hosted by the University of Malta), and the **London Gazette** for citations. Where the despatch and later scholarship disagree, both are given.

---

## One-paragraph summary

In August 1942 Malta was weeks from starvation and had almost no fuel. On the night of 2/3 August a convoy of fourteen fast merchant ships, code-named Operation Pedestal (convoy WS 21S), sailed from the Clyde with the largest escort ever assembled for a convoy: two battleships, four aircraft carriers at various stages, seven cruisers and more than thirty destroyers. The one ship that truly mattered was SS Ohio, an American-built Texaco tanker requisitioned by the US War Shipping Administration, handed to the British Ministry of War Transport on 10 July 1942, flying the Red Ensign with a British crew under Captain Dudley William Mason, carrying roughly 11,500 tons of kerosene and fuel oils. Between 11 and 15 August the convoy was attacked by U-boats, Italian submarines, more than 600 Axis aircraft sorties, E-boats and mines. HMS Eagle was sunk in about eight minutes on 11 August. Nine of the fourteen merchantmen were sunk. Ohio was torpedoed by the Italian submarine Axum, hit by bombs, had a Ju 87 and a Ju 88 crash onto her, had her back broken, her engines killed and her rudder jammed, and was finally dragged the last stretch lashed between two Hunt-class destroyers with a third acting as a rudder. She entered Grand Harbour at 0755 on 15 August 1942, the Feast of Santa Marija, decks awash, to crowds on the bastions and a band playing. Her fuel was pumped out and as the last of it left her, she settled on the harbour bottom. That fuel put Malta's aircraft and submarines back onto Rommel's supply line just as he was preparing the offensive that became Alam Halfa. Over 500 Allied sailors and airmen died to deliver it.

## Verified timeline

All times are as given in Syfret's despatch, which uses D-day numbering: **D1 = 10 August, D2 = 11 August, D3 = 12 August, D4 = 13 August, D5 = 14 August, D6 = 15 August.**

- **15 April 1942** - King George VI awards the George Cross to the Island of Malta, "to bear witness to a heroism and devotion that will long be famous in history." The King drafted and amended the citation himself and dated it 15 April. The medal was flown in on 7 May 1942 aboard a Coastal Command Sunderland carrying the new Governor, General Viscount Gort VC. It was formally presented at Palace Square, Valletta on Sunday 13 September 1942. [NHB Information Brief; The Gazette, "Malta's George Cross"; Bosbury/HMS Ledbury history quoting the citation]
- **June 1942** - Operations Harpoon and Vigorous both largely fail. Vigorous delivers nothing from its 11 ships. Harpoon gets 2 of 6 through, about 15,000 tons. Ohio's sister ship *Kentucky* is lost on Harpoon. [NHB Information Brief; Llewellyn-Jones, *The Royal Navy and the Mediterranean Convoys*]
- **21 June 1942** - Ohio arrives in the Clyde, the first of the American "Red Gap" tankers loaned to Britain to sail independently at high speed from Houston. [NHB Information Brief, citing Payton-Smith, *Oil*, HMSO 1971]
- **10 July 1942** - Ohio is requisitioned by the US Maritime Commission / War Shipping Administration and handed on bareboat charter to the British Ministry of War Transport. Captain Sverre Petersen and the American crew hand her over. US flag down, Red Ensign up. **25 July 1942** management passes to the Eagle Oil and Shipping Company. [NHB Information Brief; Lloyd's Register 1942; Pearson, *The Ohio and Malta*]
- **31 July 1942** - Victorious, Argus, Sirius and destroyers sail from Scapa for Operation Berserk, the carrier work-up west of Gibraltar, 5-10 August. [Syfret despatch, paras 4 and 12]
- **Night 2/3 August 1942** - Convoy WS 21S sails from the Clyde under a bogus WS designation, escorted by Nigeria (flag of Rear-Admiral Burrough), Kenya and destroyers. [Syfret despatch, para 5] CONFIRMED against seed.
- **10 August (D1)** - The force passes Cape Spartel at midnight 9/10, runs into fog at 0245 with visibility down to one cable, clears at 0500. At 0800 each Master opens a sealed envelope marked "Not to be opened until 0800/10th August" containing the First Lord's message: "God Speed". Syfret: "This act of courtesy and encouragement was very highly appreciated." [Syfret despatch, paras 7 and 16, and Diary of Events]
- **11 August (D2)**
  - 0600-2030: HMS Cairo and 24 destroyers refuelled at sea from RFA Brown Ranger and Dingledale. Malta had no oil to spare, so nobody could top up at the destination. [Syfret despatch, paras 17(a) and 46]
  - 1218: HMS Furious moves out to fly off Spitfires for Operation Bellows. First Spitfire off at 1229.
  - **1315: HMS Eagle torpedoed** in position 38°05'N 03°02'E by U-73 (Kapitänleutnant Helmut Rosenbaum). "Hit on the port side by four torpedoes, all within an interval of about 10 seconds." She "heeled rapidly over to port and sank in about eight minutes." Thirteen destroyers were on the screen; none got a contact. **927 survivors, including the Commanding Officer, were picked up by Laforey, Lookout and the tug Jaunty.** [Syfret despatch, Part I paras 11-13]
  - By 1450, 38 Spitfires flown off in five flights; 37 reached Malta, one with a defect landed on Indomitable. Flown off at 584 to 555 miles from Malta. [Syfret despatch, Bellows section and footnote 5]
  - 2056-2130: heavy but unsuccessful dusk air attack by Ju 88s and torpedo bombers. Three enemy aircraft shot down by ships' gunfire.
  - **0050/D3 (just after midnight 11/12)**: HMS Wolverine rams and sinks the Italian submarine **Dagabur**. No survivors. Wolverine's bows are wrecked. [Syfret despatch, para 39 and footnote 8]
- **12 August (D3)**
  - 0907-0920: first raid of about 20 high-level bombers, met by 16 fighters. Fighters claim 8 certain, 3 probable, 2 damaged. No ship damaged.
  - 1200: at least nine aircraft drop parachute mines in the fleet's path. Emergency 90-degree turn to port to avoid them. Syfret notes minelaying ahead of a fleet "has not been used before by the enemy."
  - 1230 (approx): SS Deucalion near-missed, speed reduced, detached with HMS Bramham via the coastal route.
  - 1345: two Italian Reggiane fighter-bombers dive on HMS Victorious. One bomb glances off the armoured flight deck without exploding.
  - **1649-1702: HMS Ithuriel depth-charges, forces to the surface, shells and then rams the Italian submarine Cobalto.** Three officers, including the captain, and 38 ratings taken prisoner. Ithuriel wrecks her own bow and knocks out her asdic.
  - 1830-1855: the big Sardinia/Sicily strike, 100 to 120 aircraft against 22 defending fighters. **HMS Indomitable takes three large bomb hits**, flight deck out of action, two large fires; **HMS Foresight torpedoed aft**. Indomitable is nonetheless able to make 28.5 knots by about 2030.
  - 1855: Syfret turns Force Z west 20 minutes early because of Indomitable's fires. Force X under Rear-Admiral Burrough takes the convoy on through the Skerki Channel.
  - **~1955-2000: the Italian submarine Axum (Tenente di Vascello Renato Ferrini) fires a single salvo of four torpedoes and hits three ships: HMS Nigeria, HMS Cairo and SS Ohio.** Burrough has to shift his flag from Nigeria to HMS Ashanti. Nigeria takes a 13-degree list, is brought under control and returns to Gibraltar. **Cairo's stern is blown off and she is sunk by our own forces.** Ohio and Brisbane Star are "temporarily brought to a stop, but, soon after, gallantly proceeded on their way to the southward." [Syfret despatch Part II paras 2-7; Wikipedia/regiamarina on Axum and Ferrini]
  - 2038-2100: severe dusk dive-bomber and torpedo-bomber attack. **SS Empire Hope** bombed, set on fire, abandoned and sunk by Penn. **SS Clan Ferguson** bombed and blew up. **SS Glenorchy** bombed and sunk, "blowing up with no survivors."
  - 2112: HMS Kenya torpedoed on the forefoot. Proceeds.
  - **2130: SS Deucalion torpedoed by aircraft near the Cani Rocks and sinks.** Bramham picks up survivors.
- **13 August (D4)**
  - 2354/12 the leading ships pass Cape Bon. From about 0035 onward, running fights with Italian and German E-boats and MAS boats off Kelibia.
  - **0120: HMS Manchester hit** (E-boat torpedo or mine). Abandoned by about 0245, seen to sink about 0550. Her captain was later court-martialled and the finding was that abandoning was premature. [Syfret despatch Part II paras 14-17; Roskill, *The War at Sea* Vol II, p.306 fn 2]
  - 0315-0500: **SS Wairangi**, **SS Almeria Lykes** and **SS Santa Elisa** torpedoed by E-boats. Wairangi and Almeria Lykes abandoned; Eskimo deliberately did not finish them off "because he thought there might be an opportunity later for salving them." Santa Elisa bombed at 0530, blew up and sank in five minutes.
  - **~0730-0800: SS Waimarama bombed and blew up.** HMS Ledbury (Lt Cdr Roger P. Hill) went into the burning sea to pick up survivors.
  - 0810: **SS Clan Ferguson** column attack; the contemporary despatch records Clan Ferguson taking a direct hit and blowing up at 0810 on D4, with "her next astern passed through the flames apparently unscathed" and one of the two attacking aircraft not coming out of its dive. (Note: Syfret's own Annex C dates Clan Ferguson to 2055/12. The two accounts inside the same document disagree; see "Conflicts".)
  - **0925: the Stukas "made a set at" Ohio. One Stuka, shot down by the combined fire of HMS Ashanti and Ohio, dived into Ohio's side.** [Syfret despatch, Part II para 24]
  - **1017 and 1050: combined dive-bombing and minelaying attacks. SS Dorset and SS Ohio "were both near missed and stopped." Penn, Ledbury and Bramham stand by.** Ohio comes to a stop at about 1050 and her crew is taken off to HMS Penn. [Syfret despatch Part II para 26; Wikipedia SS Ohio citing Smith, *Pedestal*]
  - 1120: Italian torpedo bombers. **One torpedo becomes entangled in SS Port Chalmers' paravane and is subsequently cleared safely.**
  - 1230: Force X comes within range of Malta's short-range Spitfires and is thereafter unmolested.
  - 1430: the Malta Escort Force under the Senior Officer, Minesweepers, in HMS Speedy joins.
  - 1600: Force X, less Penn, Bramham and Ledbury, retires west.
  - **1825: SS Port Chalmers, SS Melbourne Star and SS Rochester Castle enter Grand Harbour.** [Syfret despatch Part II para 30] CORRECTION to seed, which said 1630.
  - **2014: SS Dorset sunk** after being set on fire by further air attack. Bramham picks up survivors.
  - Overnight: futile attempts to tow Ohio. She is abandoned for the night after a bomb hits at the site of the original torpedo hole and effectively breaks her keel.
- **14 August (D5)**
  - Dawn: "Having spent the night in futile attempts to tow Ohio, Bramham, Penn and Rye are joined by Ledbury. Further attempts to tow the sinking tanker are more successful, albeit very slow." Later joined by HMS Speedy and two Motor Launches from Malta. [Malta war diary, 14 August 1942]
  - 0630: **ML 168 arrives in Malta with 68 convoy survivors aboard.**
  - **0830: "Commander minesweepers reports sweepers in company with Bramham and Penn, with tanker Ohio in tow, very deep in the water and almost unmanageable."**
  - **1430: MV Brisbane Star reaches Malta**, holed in the bows. She had spent the night and part of the day in the Gulf of Hammamet inside Vichy French territorial waters, where "his ship was boarded by the French authorities who, after protest, behaved well and took a seriously wounded man ashore to Susa [Sousse]." [Syfret despatch Part II para 21; Malta war diary] CORRECTION to seed, which gave no time.
  - Daytime: the tug **HMS Robust** is sent out from Marsaxlokk. "She proved, however, to be unsuitable for such a heavy tow and after bumping Penn and damaging her, she was not employed any more."
  - 1840: Ohio and her escorts are in sight of Dingli. "In spite of frequent air attacks, during one of which the tow was parted by a bomb..."
  - 1800: Force X rejoins Force Z at 37°29'N 03°25'E; both withdraw to Gibraltar.
- **15 August (D6), the Feast of Santa Marija**
  - 0600 (approx): with Ohio still on the edge of a British minefield off Zonqor Point, Malta tugs arrive and make fast ahead and astern, destroyers still lashed either side. [Wikipedia SS Ohio citing Attard, *The Battle of Malta*]
  - 0643-0816: eight Spitfires of 185 Squadron from Hal Far patrol over the incoming tanker until she is in Grand Harbour.
  - **0755: "After an epic struggle by her gallant Master and escorts, SS Ohio in tow of Penn and Bramham enters Grand Harbour followed by Ledbury."** [Syfret despatch para 38 (0755 hrs D6); Malta war diary, 15 August 1942] Note: some accounts round this to "about 0800" or "9:30 am"; the primary is 0755.
  - She berths at Parlatorio Wharf in French Creek, is discharged, and settles on the bottom in shallow water. **RFA Boxol takes off about 10,000 tons of fuel oil; emergency salvage pumps take off the kerosene. "The last drops of oil left her and simultaneously her keel settled on the bottom."**
  - 1800: Force X and Force Z reach Gibraltar.
- **16-22 August 1942** - Unloading, code-named **Operation Ceres**: 3,000 army personnel in three shifts day and night, plus civilians, moving about 5,000 tons a day into dispersed field dumps; another 1,500 men servicing and refuelling aircraft. [Governor's Military Situation Report, week ending 15 August 1942]
- **17 August 1942, 2000 hrs** - Penn, Bramham and Ledbury sail for Gibraltar; they arrive 0715 on 21 August. [Malta war diary; Syfret despatch para 38]
- **17 August 1942** - Two days after Ohio arrived, Malta-based Beauforts cripple the Axis supply ship *Rosolino Pilo* (8,326 tons), later finished off by a British submarine by the light of flares from a Wellington. The Malta offensive is back on. [NHB Information Brief, quoting the 1950 Joint Admiralty/Air Ministry historical survey]
- **4 September 1942** - Mason's George Cross is dated. **8 September 1942** - published in the Third Supplement to the London Gazette of Friday 4 September 1942, issue 35695, page 3911.
- **30/31 August to 2 September 1942** - Battle of Alam Halfa. Rommel's two Panzer divisions open with only 20 percent of their required fuel; by the morning of 2 September the offensive is paralysed by petrol shortage. [NHB Information Brief]
- **23 October 1942** - Second Battle of El Alamein opens. **8 November 1942** - Operation Torch.
- **19 September 1946** - Ohio's forward half towed 10 miles offshore and sunk by gunfire from the destroyer HMS Virago. **3 October 1946** - the stern half scuttled in deep water with explosive charges laid by the salvage vessel RFA Salventure. [Caruana, "Ohio Must Get Through", *Warship International* XXIX (1992)]

## Key figures

- **Acting Vice-Admiral Edward Neville Syfret, CB, RN** - Flag Officer Commanding Force F, in HMS Nelson. Overall commander. Made KCB for Pedestal in the same Gazette supplement as Mason's GC, "For bravery and dauntless resolution in fighting an important Convoy through to Malta in the face of relentless attacks by day and night from enemy submarines, aircraft, and surface forces." Wrote the despatch on 25 August 1942. [London Gazette 35695; Syfret despatch]
- **Rear-Admiral Harold M. Burrough, CB, DSO, RN** - Rear-Admiral Commanding 10th Cruiser Squadron, commanding Force X, the close escort that took the convoy through the Narrows. Flag in HMS Nigeria until she was torpedoed at about 1956 on 12 August, then transferred to HMS Ashanti. Syfret calls him "their courageous and resolute leader."
- **Rear-Admiral A. L. St G. Lyster, CB, CVO, DSO, RN** - Rear-Admiral (Aircraft Carriers), Home Fleet, in Victorious. The architect of the Taranto raid in 1940.
- **Captain Dudley William Mason, Master, SS Ohio** - Aged 39. Awarded the George Cross. Full verbatim citation below.
- **James Wyld, Chief Engineer, SS Ohio** - Awarded the DSO. [NHB Information Brief, citing Pearson, *The Ohio and Malta*, p.129]
- **Douglas Gray (Chief Officer), Hector McGilligan (Second Officer), Joseph Stephen (Third Officer), Henry Sless (Junior Engineer), SS Ohio** - each awarded the DSC. Seven other Ohio crew received the DSM. [NHB Information Brief, citing Pearson p.129] NOTE: one source states five DSCs to Ohio's crew; I could confirm only four names. See Gaps.
- **Lieutenant-Commander J. H. Swain, RN - HMS Penn** (Hunt-class destroyer). Offered Mason the first tow with a 10-inch manila rope; later lashed Penn to Ohio's starboard side.
- **Lieutenant-Commander Roger Percival Hill, RN - HMS Ledbury** (Hunt-class destroyer). Aged 32. Went into the burning sea after Waimarama's survivors. Later wrote the memoir *Destroyer Captain* (1975). He had come to Pedestal straight from convoy PQ 17 and never got over the "Scatter" order.
- **Lieutenant E. F. Baines, RN - HMS Bramham** (Hunt-class destroyer). Escorted Deucalion, picked up her survivors and Dorset's, then took Ohio's port side.
- **HMS Rye (J76)** - Halcyon-class minesweeper from Malta, took several of the tows.
- **Kapitänleutnant Helmut Rosenbaum, U-73** - Sank HMS Eagle on 11 August with four torpedoes at very short range after diving under the screen. Awarded the Knight's Cross the following day.
- **Tenente di Vascello Renato Ferrini, Italian submarine Axum** - Fired one salvo of four torpedoes at about 1955 on 12 August and hit three ships: Nigeria, Cairo and Ohio. Arguably the single most effective submarine attack of the Mediterranean war.
- **Lieutenant Alastair Mars, RN, HMS Unbroken (P42)** - On the early morning of 13 August, off the Aeolian Islands / Cape Milazzo, torpedoed and put out of action two Italian cruisers, the heavy cruiser **Bolzano** and the light cruiser **Muzio Attendolo**, from a division that had already been ordered home.
- **Field Marshal Albert Kesselring** - German Commander-in-Chief South. Refused fighter cover for the Italian cruiser divisions, preferring to use his aircraft against the convoy directly. This decision caused Supermarina to recall the Italian surface force.
- **Cadet-Midshipman Francis A. Dales, US Merchant Marine (SS Santa Elisa)** - Manned an anti-aircraft gun on Santa Elisa's bridge, hit an attacking motor torpedo boat, was rescued by HMS Penn about two hours after his ship was abandoned, then went aboard the crippled Ohio with four British crewmen, repaired one of her AA guns and fought her the rest of the way in. Awarded the Merchant Marine Distinguished Service Medal on 22 May 1943, only the third cadet-midshipman ever to receive it.
- **Frederick Treves, Junior Apprentice, MV Waimarama** - Aged 17, on his first voyage. Helped save several shipmates including the only surviving ship's officer, 3rd Wireless Operator John Jackson. Awarded the British Empire Medal and the Lloyd's War Medal for Bravery at Sea. Later trained at RADA and became a well-known British actor, in *The Elephant Man* (1980) among much else. Died 2012.
- **Lieutenant Terence Lewin, RN** - Gunnery officer of HMS Ashanti, the ship Burrough transferred his flag to. Later Admiral of the Fleet Lord Lewin, First Sea Lord and Chief of the Defence Staff during the Falklands. [Family testimony in comments on the Malta War Diary casualty page; Tier 3, corroborate before using on screen]
- **General John Vereker, 6th Viscount Gort, VC** - Governor and Commander-in-Chief, Malta. Carried the island's George Cross in with him on 7 May 1942 and spent the rest of 1942 signalling London about the "Target Date."

## Numbers (high-risk, source each)

### SS Ohio herself
- **Builder:** Sun Shipbuilding & Drydock Co., Chester, Pennsylvania, yard number 190. **Laid down 7 September 1939, launched 20 April 1940, completed June 1940.** Built in seven months and fifteen days. [Lloyd's Register 1941; Shankland and Hunter, *Malta Convoy*]
- **Owner:** The Texas Company (Texaco), registered Wilmington, Delaware. Lead ship of a class of eight, all Texaco. [Lloyd's Register; Sun Shipbuilding list of ships]
- **Tonnage:** **9,625 GRT / 5,405 NRT (1940-42)**, revised on transfer to British registry to **9,514 GRT / 5,436 NRT (1942-45)**. [Lloyd's Register 1941 and 1942, both scanned at plimsoll.southampton.gov.uk] The seed's 9,514 is right for the Pedestal period. Italian sources list her as 9,263 GRT; regard that as an Axis estimate.
- **Dimensions:** 515 ft overall, 495 ft between perpendiculars, beam 68.3 ft, depth 36.2 ft. Capacity 170,000 barrels of oil in 21 tanks, split by two continuous longitudinal bulkheads. Welded, not riveted, which was controversial at the time. Two Westinghouse steam turbines, double reduction gearing, 9,000 shaft horsepower at 90 rpm, 16 knots service, 19 knots on trials. Considered the fastest tanker of her era. [Lloyd's Register; Shankland and Hunter; Moses, *At All Costs*]
- **Crew:** 77, of whom **24 were DEMS gunners** (Royal Navy and Royal Artillery Maritime Regiment). [Wikipedia infobox citing Pearson/Moses; corroborated by NHB Information Brief on awards]
- **Pre-Pedestal strengthening at King George V Dock, Glasgow:** engines remounted on **rubber bearings** to absorb shock; **all steam pipes supported with steel springs and baulks of timber**; a **new diesel compressor plumbed in so that air at 120 psi could pressurise a holed tank and blow the seawater back out**. Armament added: 3-inch AA on the bow, 5-inch low-angle gun aft, a 40 mm Bofors abaft the funnel to deal with stern attacks, two extra Oerlikons and machine guns. [Moses, *At All Costs*, p.103, cited in Wikipedia SS Ohio]
- **Why her specifically:** she was the fastest large tanker available and could keep station in a 15 to 16 knot convoy, and she was already in British waters because she was the first of the American "Red Gap" loan tankers. The direct precedent was her sister *Kentucky*, lost on Harpoon after a near miss broke a steam pipe, which is exactly what the rubber engine mounts and pipe bracing were meant to prevent. [NHB Information Brief; Wikipedia SS Ohio]
- **Cargo:** loaded at **Dunglass on the Clyde, 11,500 tons of kerosene and diesel fuel oils**. IWM captions variously say "10,000 tons of fuel" and "11,000 tons of petrol"; Wikipedia's Operation Pedestal article says 12,000 long tons of oil. RFA Boxol took off about 10,000 tons of fuel oil at Malta, plus kerosene by salvage pump. **Best single figure to quote: about 11,500 tons loaded.** Note the spread honestly.
- **Torpedo hole:** **24 ft by 27 ft** in the port side of the midships pump-room, with a second hole punched out through the starboard side. [Wikipedia SS Ohio, citing Thomas, *Malta Convoys*] The Operation Pedestal article gives 23 ft by 26 ft. Both are in the same range; prefer 24 by 27 and note the variance.

### The operation
- **14 merchant ships** (13 dry cargo plus the tanker Ohio). Named in Syfret's despatch: Empire Hope, Dorset, Wairangi, Rochester Castle, Waimarama, Brisbane Star, Port Chalmers, Almeria Lykes, Santa Elisa, Clan Ferguson, Glenorchy, Melbourne Star, Deucalion, and the tanker Ohio. All were 15-knot ships.
- **5 arrived, 9 were lost.** [Syfret despatch, para 28]
- **Convoy designation: WS 21S.** Syfret's own despatch heads the composition list "Convoy WS21S". The NHB brief's "WS 12S" is a typo. CORRECTION.
- Convoy strength at its peak: 67 vessels by 8 August. [Wikipedia SS Ohio]
- **Five Royal Navy aircraft carriers operated in company simultaneously** on 9 August, which Syfret believed was a first: "it is believed to have been the first occasion when five of HM aircraft carriers have ever operated in company at sea simultaneously." (Victorious, Indomitable, Eagle, Furious, Argus.)
- **Fighter strength:** 72 fighters and 28 Albacores across Victorious, Indomitable and Eagle. Eagle's loss took out 25 percent of the fighter strength at a stroke: 12 Sea Hurricanes went down with her, four were airborne and landed on the other carriers. [NHB Information Brief; Syfret despatch para 17(b)]
- **Operation Bellows:** 38 Spitfires flown off Furious on 11 August at 584 to 555 miles from Malta; **37 arrived**. [Syfret despatch]
- **Fighter claims:** carrier fighters claimed **39 enemy aircraft certain** with "at least the same number" probably incapacitated. [Syfret despatch para 55] Malta's report for 12-14 August: nine enemy aircraft destroyed by ships' fire, eight probable, three possible, with merchant ships' own AA guns accounting for four of the confirmed kills.
- **Emergency turns:** 27 on the passage to the Straits and 48 during D1 to D3, all triggered by A/S screen warnings. [Syfret despatch para 54]
- **Depth charges as routine:** from 1400 to 1900 on 12 August, one destroyer on each side of the screen dropped a depth charge every ten minutes, on principle. [Syfret despatch para 40]
- **Tonnage delivered:** approximately **32,000 tons** of food, ammunition, fuel and stores landed, assessed as enough for **about ten more weeks**. Malta's own contemporary assessment, written a week later, put it at **three months** of food and general supplies. Both figures are in the same band; the Malta command figure is the primary one. [Warfare History Network for the 32,000/ten weeks figure; Malta War Diary, 16-22 August 1942, for the three-month assessment]
- **Unloading:** 3,000 army personnel in three shifts plus civilians, **5,000 tons a day**, dispersed to field dumps. Plus 1,500 men on aerodromes. Governor's Military Situation Report, week ending 15 August 1942.
- **Also delivered:** 31 all-ranks reinforcements and **90 carrier pigeons**. [Governor's Military Situation Report, week ending 15 August 1942]

### Casualties, and the conflicts in them
- **HMS Eagle.** Syfret's despatch is unambiguous that **927 survivors were picked up** and that she **sank in about eight minutes** after four torpedoes in about ten seconds. The death toll is genuinely disputed:
  - **131** - Wikipedia's HMS Eagle article and the RN Naval Historical Branch Information Brief.
  - **~160** - uboat.net gives "2 officers and 158 ratings."
  - **~260** - Wikipedia's SS Ohio article, citing Max Arthur, *Lost Voices of the Royal Navy*, which also says she sank in four minutes.
  - **BEST-SUPPORTED: about 161-163.** I counted the named dead on two independent CWGC-derived rolls. **Don Kindell's Royal Navy casualty list for 11 August 1942 (naval-history.net) contains 161 distinct HMS Eagle entries**, and the Malta War Diary Pedestal casualty list contains **162 HMS Eagle names for 11 August plus one more on 12 August**. That triangulates to roughly 160 to 163 and rules out both 131 and 260.
  - **On screen, say "about 160" and flag that the figure is disputed.** Do not say 131. Do not say 260. Do not say "sank in four minutes"; the commanding admiral's own report says about eight.
- **HMS Nigeria: 52 killed.** [Malta War Diary casualty list, 52 named; corroborated by Axum accounts]
- **HMS Cairo: 24 killed.** [Malta War Diary casualty list, 24 named]
- **HMS Indomitable: 47 named dead on 12 August plus 1 on 15 August.** Wikipedia gives "fifty killed and 59 wounded." An eyewitness on HMS Rodney described her burying "some 50 of them" at sea while escorting her back to Gibraltar.
- **HMS Manchester: 10 named dead on 13 August plus 1 on 14 August.** About 150 of her company were taken off by HMS Pathfinder, another 150 recovered from Carley floats by Eskimo and Somali, and **several hundred more were seen being marched away into captivity on the Tunisian shore**, half a mile from the rescuers. [Syfret despatch Part II paras 16 and 22]
- **HMS Foresight: 4 named dead.** She was torpedoed aft on 12 August, taken in tow by HMS Tartar, and sunk by Tartar at 0955 on 13 August when the tow failed.
- **MV Waimarama:** the worst single loss. Syfret's contemporary annex says **19 survivors out of 120**. Modern research (Crabb; Wikipedia MV Waimarama) says **83 of 107 crew killed**, i.e. 24 survivors. Either way roughly **80 to 100 men died in seconds** when her deck cargo of aviation spirit went up. Use "more than eighty" and flag the range.
- **SS Ohio's own dead: at least one confirmed.** **Gunner Peter Brown, Royal Artillery, 1823820, 7/4 Maritime Regiment RA, killed 13 August 1942.** He is the only Ohio name on both CWGC-derived rolls. See Gaps for the merchant-crew figure.
- **Total Allied dead: "more than 500 Merchant and Royal Navy sailors and airmen."** [Max Hastings, *Operation Pedestal: The Fleet That Battled to Malta 1942*, publisher's own description] Wikipedia's infobox gives a range of 350 to 550+. Use **"more than 500"** and attribute it to Hastings.
- **Axis losses (contemporary assessment in Syfret's report):** 2 submarines sunk (one German, one Italian; in fact both were Italian, Dagabur and Cobalto), 2 E-boats sunk and 4 or more damaged, one Italian cruiser damaged and one possibly sunk. Modern figures: 2 submarines sunk, 2 cruisers seriously damaged (Bolzano and Muzio Attendolo), roughly 48 to 60 aircraft destroyed, about 100 Axis dead. NOTE: the "one German" submarine in Syfret's assessment is wrong; Dagabur and Cobalto were both Italian, and Syfret's own footnotes name them correctly.

## Everything that hit the Ohio, in order

This is the sequence the episode will ride on. Sourcing is layered: **times and the fact of each hit come from Syfret's despatch and the Malta war diary; the interior detail comes from Pearson, Moses, Smith and Thomas as compiled in the SS Ohio literature.**

1. **12 August, ~1955-2000, Skerki Channel.** Torpedo from the Italian submarine **Axum**, one of a salvo of four that also hit Nigeria and Cairo. Hole **24 by 27 ft** in the port side of the midships pump-room, and a second hole punched out the starboard side. A pillar of flame. Burning kerosene bubbling out of fractured tanks, gouts of flame spattering the deck up to 30 yards from the blaze. The deck opened up so you could look down into the ship. **Gyrocompass destroyed, magnetic compass knocked off its bearings, steering gear disabled.** Mason shut the engines down, the crew put the fire out, and she got going again on **emergency steering gear aft**, hand-steered, without a compass, making 13 knots. [Syfret despatch Part II para 2; Wikipedia SS Ohio citing Thomas, *Malta Convoys*]
2. **12/13 August overnight and into the morning.** Repeated near misses from Ju 87s. Plates buckled, forward tank flooded, the **3-inch bow gun twisted in its mountings and put out of action**.
3. **13 August, 0925.** A **Ju 87 Stuka**, shot down by the combined fire of **HMS Ashanti and Ohio herself**, **dived into Ohio's side**. [Syfret despatch Part II para 24, primary]
4. **13 August, same morning.** A **Ju 88**, shot down by Ohio's gunners, crashed onto her **starboard bow forward of the bridge and exploded. Half a wing landed on the upper bridge. The aircraft's bomb failed to detonate.** [Wikipedia SS Ohio citing the Italian account and Max Arthur, *Forgotten Voices of the Second World War*, p.172]
5. **Two sticks of bombs, one either side.** "The vessel was lifted clean out of the water," fell back with a crash. Boiler fires blown out. The engine-room master switches thrown off by the blast; an electrician switched them back on. Engineers relit the furnaces with fire-starter torches and had her doing 16 knots again within 20 minutes.
6. **Another salvo.** Electric fuel pumps broken. Oil in the water pipes, vacuum lost in the condenser, black and white smoke alternating from the funnel. **Ohio came to a stop at 1050 on 13 August.** Crew abandoned to HMS Penn. [Syfret despatch Part II para 26 for the 1017/1050 attacks; Wikipedia SS Ohio for the interior sequence]
7. **13 August, dusk.** Under Penn's tow, a German bomber released its bomb just before Ohio's gunners shot it down. **The bomb hit exactly where the torpedo had hit, effectively breaking her keel.** Abandoned for the night.
8. **14 August, ~1045.** With Rye towing and Ledbury acting as a stern rudder at about 6 knots, a wave of dive-bombers. One **oil bomb** landed close to the bow and showered her with burning liquid. Then a **1,000 lb bomb landed in her wake**. Ohio was flung forward, **Rye's tow parted, her stern plates buckled and a great hole opened**. 16 Spitfires of 229 and 249 Squadrons from Malta were overhead. [Wikipedia SS Ohio citing Jellison, *Besieged*, and McAulay; corroborated by the Malta war diary's Spitfire log]
9. **14 August, later.** Another attack parted the tow again with a bomb. **Rudder immobilised.** Malta war diary: "during one of which the tow was parted by a bomb."
10. **Final state:** back broken, engine room flooded, engines dead, rudder jammed, decks awash amidships, and the whole thing held together mostly by two destroyers.

## The tow, in detail

This is the part the popular telling gets wrong, so get it right.

- **Attempt 1, 13 August afternoon.** **HMS Penn** (Lt Cdr Swain) offered a tow with a **heavy 10-inch manila rope**. Penn strained her engines to the limit. Ohio kept listing to port, the pair made no headway and actually **drifted astern** in an easterly wind. When a serious air attack came in, Penn went to full speed deliberately to part the tow and get clear. [Wikipedia SS Ohio, drawing on Smith, *Pedestal*, and Pearson]
- **Attempt 2, 14 August.** Penn joined by the minesweeper **HMS Rye**. Together they got Ohio up to about **5 knots**, fighting her tendency to swing to port. Air attack broke the tow lines and immobilised her rudder.
- **Attempt 3.** **HMS Rye towing ahead with HMS Ledbury secured astern acting as a stern tug and then as a rudder.** Steering was still impossible, so Swain edged **Penn alongside Ohio's starboard side** as a stabiliser. Rye and **Bramham** got under way with **Ledbury steering from astern**, making about **6 knots**. Then the 1,000 lb bomb landed in the wake and parted Rye's tow.
- **The tug that made it worse.** Malta sent out the tug **HMS Robust**, escorted to Marsaxlokk by Beryl and Swona, and she was reported towing ahead of Ohio. "**She proved, however, to be unsuitable for such a heavy tow and after bumping Penn and damaging her, she was not employed any more.**" [Royal Navy Operations Report, Malta, Friday 14 August 1942]
- **The final configuration.** After the last tow parted, Ledbury was still attached by a heavy wire that the yawing tanker had dragged round until Ledbury ended up alongside Penn **facing the wrong way**. The decision was then taken to simply **lash a destroyer on each side of the tanker and push and pull her in**: **Penn on the starboard side, Bramham on the port side**, with **Ledbury shoving her bow round to make the turns off Delimara and Zonqor Points**. Speed limited to about **5 knots**. Ohio's deck was awash amidships. [Wikipedia SS Ohio citing Bradford, *Siege: Malta 1940-1943*]
- **The last stretch.** Malta's minesweepers (Speedy, Rye, Hebe, Hythe) and two Motor Launches joined. Coastal batteries fired on a U-boat's conning tower and drove off E-boats. At about 0600 on 15 August, with Ohio hovering on the edge of a British minefield near Zonqor Point, **Malta tugs came out, made fast ahead and astern with the destroyers still lashed either side**, and she came up the channel.
- **Contemporary verdict on how bad it was:** "sweepers in company with Bramham and Penn, with tanker Ohio in tow, **very deep in the water and almost unmanageable**." (Commander Minesweepers, 0830, 14 August 1942.)

## Mason's George Cross citation (verbatim, London Gazette)

Source: **Third Supplement to the London Gazette of Friday 4 September 1942, issue 35695, published Tuesday 8 September 1942, page 3911.** Transcribed from the Gazette's own PDF. This is the complete citation.

> **Captain Dudley William Mason, Master, s.s. "Ohio,"**
>
> During the passage to Malta of an important convoy Captain Mason's ship suffered most violent onslaught. She was a focus of attack throughout and was torpedoed early one night. Although gravely damaged, her engines were kept going and the Master made a magnificent passage by hand-steering and without a compass. The ship's gunners helped to bring down one of the attacking aircraft. The vessel was hit again before morning, but though she did not sink, her engine room was wrecked. She was then towed. The unwieldy condition of the vessel and persistent enemy attacks made progress slow, and it was uncertain whether she would remain afloat. All next day progress somehow continued and the ship reached Malta after a further night at sea. The violence of the enemy could not deter the Master from his purpose. Throughout he showed skill and courage of the highest order and it was due to his determination that, in spite of the most persistent enemy opposition, the vessel, with her valuable cargo, eventually reached Malta and was safely berthed.
>
> (The award is dated 4th September, 1942.)

Note the deadpan understatement available for comedy: the official citation calls a torpedo, a keel-breaking bomb, two aircraft crashing onto the ship and a 90-mile tow "**progress somehow continued**." That phrase is quotable and 100 percent verified.

Also on the same page, Syfret's KCB: "**For bravery and dauntless resolution in fighting an important Convoy through to Malta in the face of relentless attacks by day and night from enemy submarines, aircraft, and surface forces.**"

## The Malta side

- **The George Cross to Malta, 15 April 1942.** The King's citation: "**To honour her brave people I award the George Cross to the Island Fortress of Malta to bear witness to a heroism and devotion that will long be famous in history. George R.I.**" The first of only two collective awards of the GC (the other being the Royal Ulster Constabulary in 1999). Presented at Palace Square, Valletta, 13 September 1942.
- **The Target Date.** This is the technical term used in the signals traffic. NHB footnote: "**The 'Target Date' was the date on which surrender of the Island was considered inevitable.**" Gort's post-Pedestal signal of 27 September 1942 asked for "only absolutely essential articles of food and fuel...with small allocation of civil and service stores," with the stated object "**to put off target date until early January**."
  - Contemporary figures for the pre-Pedestal Target Date differ by source: **Gort in July signalled Churchill "estimate food and petrol stocks will be exhausted by August 21"**; another account has Gort advising in early August that Malta would have to surrender by **7 September**; another gives **31 August**. All three are in the same fortnight. **On screen, say "the island had weeks, not months, and the exact surrender date was being argued over in signals" and cite Gort.** Do not present a single hard date as settled.
- **The 15 August coincidence, which is real and documented.** Ten days before the convoy arrived, Malta's manager of food distribution, **Mr Trench**, stated: "**the absolute last issue from Island reserves occurs in five days, on 15 August. After that we are down to the slaughter of horses and goats, once considered adequate for six months...the present census of animals on the Island is estimated to last from five to ten days.**" [Malta War Diary, 10 August 1942, sourcing navalhistory.net, "The Supply of Malta 1940-42"] **Ohio came in on the morning of 15 August.**
- **Rations.** A new ration scale from 20 June 1942 cut average daily civilian calories to under 1,500. A soldier's account: "Our ration went down to 1,000 to 1,400 calories a day. We had one tin of bully beef for eight men, and one slice of bread each. When I went to Malta I weighed 10.9 stone, when I came back it was 8.6 stone. Our uniforms and boots were wearing out. We put bits of cardboard in the boots to protect our feet." (Jimmie Ferguson, Royal Irish Fusiliers, BBC WW2 People's War.)
- **A waitress at Xara Villa, where the Ta Qali pilots ate:** "I was 15. We used to lick the plates we collected after the meal, before we sent them down to wash. When they realised, the pilots even left us a little on the plate." (Maria Parsons née Spiteri, George Cross Island Association.)
- **The Feast of Santa Marija, 15 August.** The Assumption of the Blessed Virgin Mary, one of Malta's principal feast days. Because Ohio arrived on that morning, Pedestal is known in Malta as the **Santa Marija Convoy**. The NHB brief confirms: "This day was traditionally known in the Island as the feast of Santa Maria, and the 'Pedestal' convoy has been called the 'Convoy of Santa Maria'."
- **The crowds and the band.** Crowds packed the Barracca, Fort St Angelo and Senglea. There is a genuine conflict about what the band played:
  - "*Rule Britannia*" - Hogan, *Malta: The Triumphant Years*, and most secondary retellings.
  - "*Hearts of Oak*" - two of Ohio's own gunners, W. R. Cheetham and D. Burke, in their BBC WW2 People's War account: "the military band played 'Hearts of Oak' as we entered, **making us feel very fed up because we did not ask for praise. We had only done what we set out to do.**"
  - **Safest on screen: "a band played" and use the gunners' quote, which is first-hand.**
- **The berth and the sinking.** Berthed at **Parlatorio Wharf, French Creek**, the same creek where HMS Illustrious had survived the Illustrious Blitz. Discharged and settled on the bottom.
- **Memorial:** the Santa Marija Convoy Memorial Anchor at Quarry Wharf, Lascaris Waterfront, Valletta. Ohio's nameplate, ship's wheel and ensign are in Malta's National War Museum in Valletta.

## The payoff (sourced strategic consequence)

The cleanest single sourced line, from the Official Historian: "**The fuel which she carried enabled air strikes to be restarted from Malta just when Rommel was preparing for the offensive intended to drive the Allies finally out of Egypt. The enemy's shipping losses to air attacks at once increased, and the offensive had to be postponed because of shortage of supplies.**" (S. W. Roskill, *The War at Sea*, Vol. II, HMSO 1956, p.306, quoted in the NHB Information Brief.)

Reinforcing evidence, all Tier 1 via the NHB brief:
- **17 August 1942, two days after Ohio berthed:** Malta Beauforts crippled the Axis supply ship *Rosolino Pilo* (8,326 tons), later sunk by a British submarine.
- **The Battle of Alam Halfa opened on the night of 30/31 August 1942 with the two German Panzer divisions holding only 20 percent of their necessary fuel. By the morning of 2 September Rommel's offensive had been paralysed by shortage of petrol.**
- **7 September 1942, the Deputy Chief of German Naval Command in Italy to the Chief of the Naval Staff:** "There is no doubt that, towards the end, continuation of the offensive in North Africa collapsed because of inadequate supply services... **The RAF in the Mediterranean, i.e. in Malta, must be eliminated.**"
- Bullard (Admiralty Operational Research), May 1943: of the Allied air offensive against Axis supply lines, **just under half (45 percent) of the sorties were flown from Malta, and those sank half the enemy shipping**.
- Longer arc: by their own estimate the Axis lost over 16 percent of embarked cargoes crossing 600 miles of Mediterranean, against 3 percent lost by the British over 14,000 miles round the Cape.

## Absurd / comedic beats (all real, all sourced)

Every one of these is documented. Nothing invented.

1. **Mason's reply to the second aircraft crashing on his ship.** The Chief Officer telephoned the bridge to report that a Ju 87 had crashed into the sea and then bounced up onto the stern. Mason "rather curtly" replied: "**Oh that's nothing. We've had a Junkers 88 on the foredeck for nearly half an hour.**" [Max Arthur, *Forgotten Voices of the Second World War*, p.172]
2. **The official citation's greatest understatement.** After a torpedo, a broken keel, a wrecked engine room and two aircraft embedded in the ship, the London Gazette says: "**All next day progress somehow continued.**" (Verbatim, London Gazette 35695 p.3911.)
3. **The rescue tug that crashed into the rescuer.** Malta sent the tug **HMS Robust** out to help tow Ohio. Official Royal Navy operations report, 14 August 1942: "**She proved, however, to be unsuitable for such a heavy tow and after bumping Penn and damaging her, she was not employed any more.**"
4. **Syfret's report card on his own destroyer captain.** HMS Ithuriel depth-charged the Italian submarine Cobalto to the surface, then shelled it, then rammed it, wrecking her own bow and knocking out her own asdic in the middle of an air battle. Syfret, in the official despatch: "**The submarine when it came to the surface after being depth charged was obviously 'all in' and I thought the expensive method chosen by the Commanding Officer, HMS ITHURIEL, to sink it quite unnecessary. Moreover, I was disturbed at the resulting absence of ITHURIEL from the screen when an air attack was impending.**"
5. **The Italian fleet that was recalled and then torpedoed anyway.** Supermarina sent out the 3rd Division (Gorizia, Bolzano, Trieste) and the 7th (Eugenio di Savoia, Montecuccoli, Muzio Attendolo) to finish off the convoy. At 0830 on 12 August, **Kesselring told the Italians the Luftwaffe could not provide fighter cover because it was busy escorting its own bombers.** Supermarina recalled the cruisers to Messina without firing a shot. On the way home, on the early morning of 13 August, **one British submarine, HMS Unbroken (Lt Alastair Mars), torpedoed two of them.** **Muzio Attendolo lost the entire hull forward of her first turret. Bolzano caught fire, had to flood her forward magazines, listed 45 degrees and was beached on the island of Panarea.** Bolzano was never properly repaired.
6. **The torpedo that got caught in the laundry.** During the Italian torpedo-bomber attack at 1120 on 13 August, "**one which became entangled in SS PORT CHALMERS' paravane (subsequently cleared safely).**" A merchant ship went most of the way to Malta with a live torpedo snagged on her minesweeping gear and then simply untangled it. [Syfret despatch Part II para 27]
7. **The convoy in Italian fancy dress.** Force Y, the two ships coming the other way out of Malta (Troilus and Orari), wore **Italian deck markings**. Syfret: "**Though aircraft flew over them on D2, their Italian deck markings seemed to nonplus the enemy's observers**"; on D3 they were "shadowed occasionally by single aircraft who **remained mystified**." They reached Gibraltar untouched. They also smuggled out **44 German and 15 Italian prisoners of war** from the Malta POW cage.
8. **The Italian fighter-bombers nobody shot at.** At 1345 on 12 August two Italian Reggiane fighter-bombers dived on HMS Victorious. One bomb glanced off the armoured flight deck without exploding. Because Victorious was landing aircraft at the time, "**these were taken for friendly fighters and they got away practically unfired at.**" [Syfret despatch Part I para 37]
9. **The ship that sailed through the fireball.** When SS Clan Ferguson blew up, "**Her next astern passed through the flames apparently unscathed**," and HMS Charybdis reported seeing **two aircraft dive on Clan Ferguson and only one come out of the dive**, the other presumably destroyed by the explosion of the ship it had just bombed.
10. **The two abandoned ships nobody bothered to sink, in case they might be handy later.** Wairangi and Almeria Lykes were left afloat and abandoned. HMS Eskimo's captain "**reports that he did not complete the sinking of the ships because he thought there might be an opportunity later for salving them.**" There was not.
11. **The carrier pigeons.** Among the reinforcements landed at Malta from Pedestal: "Reinforcement 31 all ranks details arrived on convoy, **also 90 carrier pigeons**." Nine merchant ships and over 500 men were lost delivering, among other things, 90 pigeons.
12. **The unloading operation was code-named Ceres**, after the Roman goddess of grain, which is either lovely or hilarious depending on delivery. 3,000 men in three shifts.
13. **The ship that could not be allowed to sink but had to be sunk.** Ohio was deliberately berthed in shallow water so that when she inevitably went down she would go down sitting on the bottom with her cargo still reachable. As the last of the oil was pumped out, "**the last drops of oil left her and simultaneously her keel settled on the bottom.**" She never sailed again.
14. **The afterlife.** Ohio then broke clean in two at her berth, and the two halves were used as **storage hulks and then as barracks for Yugoslav troops**. The famous tanker spent her retirement as two separate buildings. In September and October 1946 the halves were disposed of separately: the bow towed 10 miles out and shot to pieces by HMS Virago, the stern blown up by RFA Salventure.
15. **The American crew who were furious about it.** Texaco's marine department cabled its London agent that "**on no account was Ohio to leave her discharging port.**" Captain Sverre Petersen and his American crew were, in the words of the account, "exasperated by the seemingly outrageous order" but had to pack their kit and hand over. There was "no formal ceremony and little goodwill." Two months later she was the most famous ship in the Mediterranean.
16. **The American who got sunk and then went back for more.** Cadet-Midshipman Francis Dales, whose own ship Santa Elisa had been torpedoed and blown up under him, was fished out by HMS Penn, and then went aboard the wrecked Ohio with four British crewmen to fix and man an anti-aircraft gun for the rest of the trip.
17. **The band, and the men who hated it.** Ohio's own gunners on the reception at Grand Harbour: "the military band played 'Hearts of Oak' as we entered, **making us feel very fed up because we did not ask for praise. We had only done what we set out to do.**"
18. **Syfret's closing line in the official despatch**, after losing a carrier, two cruisers, a destroyer and nine merchant ships: "**In conclusion I think I am speaking for all in saying that we are disappointed at not doing better but we should like to try again.**"

## Human-cost beats (PLAY THESE COMPLETELY STRAIGHT)

Flagged clearly. No jokes anywhere near these.

- **HMS Eagle.** Four torpedoes in about ten seconds. She heeled over to port and was gone in about eight minutes. **About 160 men died, the great majority of them in the machinery spaces**, stokers and engine room artificers who had no chance of getting up and out. Read the roll and it is page after page of "Stoker 1c," "Stoker 2c," "Act/Leading Stoker." Nine of them were ship's musicians, Royal Marines bandsmen who by tradition manned the transmitting station deep in the ship during action. 927 men were pulled out of the water.
- **MV Waimarama, 13 August.** Her deck cargo was drums and cases of aviation spirit. She was hit and detonated. **More than eighty of her crew of about 107 to 120 died in seconds.** A 17-year-old apprentice on his first voyage, Frederick Treves, swam back into burning oil to pull men out.
- **The men from Melbourne Star who jumped into the fire.** Melbourne Star was next astern of Waimarama. When Waimarama exploded, men aboard Melbourne Star **thought their own ship had been hit and jumped overboard.** HMS Ledbury picked up 42 survivors from that stretch of burning water, and **23 of them were Melbourne Star's men who had abandoned a ship that was perfectly fine.** Five of Melbourne Star's people were killed on 12-13 August. [Syfret despatch, Annex C, contemporary]
- **SS Glenorchy, 12/13 August.** Bombed and sunk. Syfret's report and the contemporary annex both record: "**blowing up with no survivors**" and "No known survivors." Her Master, **G. Leslie, DSC**, is on the casualty roll along with seven of his crew. (A small number are now known to have survived; the contemporary record says none, which is itself the point about what those nights were like.)
- **SS Clan Ferguson.** "Bombed and blew up. **No known survivors**" in the contemporary annex.
- **HMS Manchester.** Scuttled off the Tunisian coast. About 300 of her company were picked up by British ships. The rest walked ashore into Vichy French internment, and their own shipmates watched it happen: Eskimo and Somali, half a mile off the coast, "**were able to see several hundreds of, apparently, HMS Manchester's ship's company being marched away.**" Her captain was court-martialled and the finding was that he had abandoned ship prematurely.
- **HMS Cairo, HMS Nigeria, HMS Indomitable, HMS Foresight.** 24, 52, roughly 48 and 4 named dead respectively. HMS Rodney's crew watched Indomitable bury about fifty of hers at sea on the way home: "**From the stern of the ship we could see bundles toppling into the sea as 'Indom' buried her dead. There were some 50 of them, a sight that remains vivid in my memory to this day.**" (L. Myers, HMS Rodney, BBC WW2 People's War.)
- **Total: more than 500 Merchant Navy and Royal Navy sailors and airmen killed** to move one convoy 1,100 miles. Nine of fourteen merchant ships lost, one fleet carrier, two cruisers and a destroyer.
- **A civilian counterpoint from the same fortnight.** On 1 August 1942 a 13-year-old boy, Joe Galea, was killed in Birkirkara when he jumped over a rubble wall while playing hide and seek and landed on an undetected German butterfly bomb left over from a raid on Ta Qali. Malta's war was not only at sea.
- **SS Ohio's own dead: Gunner Peter Brown, Royal Artillery, 7/4 Maritime Regiment, killed 13 August 1942.** Name him.

## Myth vs record (corrections worth putting on screen)

1. **"Ohio steamed into Malta under her own power."** **FALSE.** Her engines stopped for good at about 1050 on 13 August. She entered Grand Harbour at 0755 on 15 August **in tow, lashed between HMS Penn and HMS Bramham, with Malta tugs made fast ahead and astern and HMS Ledbury shoving her bow round the turns**. Syfret's despatch, para 38: "HM Ships PENN, LEDBURY and BRAMHAM arrived at Malta with SS OHIO at 0755 hrs D6." [Syfret despatch; Malta war diary 15 August 1942]
2. **"A German U-boat broke her back."** **FALSE on both counts.** The torpedo that started it came from the **Italian** submarine **Axum** (Tenente di Vascello Renato Ferrini) at about 1955 on 12 August, in a salvo of four that also hit Nigeria and Cairo. Her **back was broken the following evening by a German bomb** that landed in almost exactly the same spot as the torpedo. Two different weapons, two different air forces and navies, two different days. [Syfret despatch Part II para 2; Wikipedia SS Ohio citing Smith, *Pedestal*]
3. **"Pedestal was a clean win."** **FALSE.** **Nine of fourteen merchant ships were sunk.** So were an aircraft carrier, two cruisers and a destroyer. Syfret himself wrote: "The losses suffered by Force F were **regrettably heavy** and the number of merchant ships which reached Malta **disappointingly small**." The strategic result was decisive; the tactical result was a mauling.
4. **"HMS Eagle sank in four minutes with 131 lost."** **BOTH FIGURES ARE SHAKY.** Syfret's own despatch says she "sank in **about eight minutes**." The named CWGC-derived dead total **about 160 to 163**, not 131 and not 260. Say "about eight minutes" and "about 160 men."
5. **"Ohio was an American ship with an American crew."** **HALF FALSE.** She was American-built and Texaco-owned, but she was **requisitioned by the US War Shipping Administration, bareboat-chartered to the British Ministry of War Transport on 10 July 1942, and operated by the Eagle Oil and Shipping Company under the Red Ensign with a British crew and British and Commonwealth DEMS gunners**. Her American master, Captain Sverre Petersen, and his crew were put ashore against their will. (One American, Cadet-Midshipman Francis Dales, did end up aboard her, but only because his own ship had been sunk.)
6. **"Ohio was saved and sailed on."** **FALSE.** She was **effectively scuttled in shallow water to unload her**, broke in two, was used as storage and then as barracks for Yugoslav troops, and was disposed of in two separate operations in September and October 1946. She never sailed again after 15 August 1942.
7. **"The Italian fleet came out and was driven off in a battle."** **FALSE.** The Italian cruiser divisions were **recalled by Supermarina without ever engaging**, because **Kesselring refused to provide fighter cover**. The only thing that hit them was HMS Unbroken, on their way home, and she put two of them out of the war for months.
8. **"The convoy was WS 12S."** **FALSE.** The convoy was **WS 21S**, per Syfret's own composition list in the despatch. (The RN Naval Historical Branch brief contains this typo; the despatch is the primary.)
9. **"Malta's George Cross was awarded for Operation Pedestal."** **FALSE.** The George Cross was awarded to Malta on **15 April 1942**, four months before Pedestal, for the island's endurance under the spring blitz.
10. **"Ohio arrived on 14 August."** **FALSE** in every primary. 0755 on **15 August 1942**, the Feast of Santa Marija. (Note that the Malta War Diary's transcription of Part II of Syfret's despatch places para 31 under a "Friday 14 August (D5)" heading; this is a transcription/heading error, contradicted by para 38 of the same document, by the Malta war diary's hour-by-hour log for 15 August, and by IWM. **Brisbane Star** is the ship that arrived on 14 August, at 1430.)

## Writer's note

The comedy in this story is structural, not invented. The Royal Navy assembled the largest convoy escort of the war, including five aircraft carriers operating together for the first time, to protect a fleet of merchant ships, then watched two thirds of the merchant ships get sunk, then spent two days dragging the surviving tanker to Malta at walking pace with a tug that crashed into a destroyer and a destroyer being used as a rudder, arriving on the exact day Malta's food reserves ran out, to be met by a band playing a tune the crew resented. Meanwhile the entire Italian battle fleet was recalled without firing a shot because the Luftwaffe would not lend it fighters, and got torpedoed on the way home by one submarine.

None of that needs embellishment. The men who died do.
