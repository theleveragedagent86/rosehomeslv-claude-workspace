# Research Report: The Great Panjandrum

## One-paragraph summary

The Great Panjandrum was a real British secret weapon developed by the Admiralty's Directorate of Miscellaneous Weapons Development (DMWD) in 1943 and 1944. It was a giant rocket-propelled wheel: a steel drum meant to be packed with explosive, slung between two large wooden wheels about 10 feet (3 m) in diameter and about 1 foot (0.3 m) wide, driven by dozens of cordite rockets on the rims. The idea was to launch it from a landing craft so it would race up a beach at high speed and detonate against the concrete sea wall of Hitler's Atlantic Wall ahead of D-Day. In repeated trials on the beach at Westward Ho! in Devon (first trial 7 September 1943), it went wildly out of control: rockets detached and flew off, it veered and spiraled, and in a final demonstration in January 1944 before assembled officers and an official photographer it careened off course, shed live rockets, and scattered the spectators. It was named by novelist and aeronautical engineer Nevil Shute Norway, who worked at DMWD, after "the grand Panjandrum" in an 18th-century nonsense passage by Samuel Foote. It was never used in battle and was abandoned. No deaths or injuries are documented.

## Verified timeline
- 1943 - DMWD tasked with a device to breach the concrete defenses of the Atlantic Wall, launchable from a landing craft. [Wikipedia "Panjandrum"; The Past]
- 7 September 1943 - first trial at Westward Ho!, Devon. Nevil Shute Foundation DMWD trial log: "18 rockets - 20 second burning. Travelled along the beach covering 668 feet." [Nevil Shute Foundation DMWD Narrative; Wikipedia]
- Autumn 1943 into January 1944 - repeated trials; rockets detached, device veered and spiraled and overturned. Three-wheel version "Swerved and tilted over - damaged"; another trial "travelled in a tight spiral and overturned." [Nevil Shute Foundation DMWD Narrative]
- January 1944 - final demonstration before Navy officials, scientists, and an official photographer; Panjandrum broke away, shed live rockets, and scattered the observers. [Wikipedia; The Past; Amusing Planet]
- Project abandoned; never used in action. [Nevil Shute Foundation DMWD Narrative: "The problems of controlling the Panjandrum were never solved and it was not used in action."; Wikipedia]

## Key figures
- Nevil Shute Norway (17 January 1899 - 12 January 1960), English aeronautical engineer and novelist (pen name Nevil Shute). Royal Naval Volunteer Reserve; department head in the DMWD; named the weapon. [Nevil Shute Foundation; Wikipedia "Nevil Shute"; The Engineer]
- Directorate / Department of Miscellaneous Weapons Development (DMWD), Admiralty body for experimental weapons, nicknamed the "Wheezers and Dodgers." [Wikipedia "Department of Miscellaneous Weapons Development"; Nevil Shute Foundation]
- Klemantaski - the official photographer at the January 1944 trial who kept filming through a telescopic lens until the Panjandrum came at him. [Wikipedia; The Past]
- Samuel Foote (1720-1777), 18th-century actor and dramatist who coined "Panjandrum" in a nonsense passage (c. 1754-1755) to test actor Charles Macklin's memory. [World Wide Words; Wikipedia "The Great Panjandrum Himself"]

## Numbers (high-risk, source each)
- Wheel diameter: 10 ft (3.0 m). [Wikipedia; The Past; War History Online]
- Wheel width: 1 ft (0.30 m). [Wikipedia]
- Atlantic Wall defenses to breach: about 10 ft (3.0 m) high, 7 ft (2.1 m) thick concrete. [Wikipedia]
- Explosive load: Shute calculated over 1 long ton (about 1,000 kg) needed; the intended full warhead is given as 4,000 lb (1,800 kg). Sources give a RANGE here: "over 1 long ton" (the requirement) vs a "4,000-pound (1,800 kg) load" (the design figure). Give best-sourced figures and note the range. [Wikipedia; The Past]
- Predicted speed at full load: around 60 mph (about 100 km/h). [Wikipedia; The Past]
- Rockets: first trial used 18 cordite rockets; later trials carried over seventy cordite rockets; individual rockets about 20 lb (9.1 kg). [Wikipedia; Nevil Shute Foundation DMWD log ("18 rockets")]
- First trial distance: 668 feet along the beach. [Nevil Shute Foundation DMWD Narrative]

## Absurd / comedic beats (all real)
- The whole weapon: a ton of explosive on a giant flaming Catherine-wheel meant to roll up a beach at 60 mph. [Wikipedia; The Past]
- Secrecy broke down because the test beach at Westward Ho! was a popular holiday spot, so trials had civilian audiences from the start despite safety warnings. [Wikipedia]
- Rockets detaching and flying off the rims; the device veering, spiraling, and overturning. [Wikipedia; Nevil Shute Foundation]
- Steering cables added, then the Panjandrum "proved to be too powerful," snapping the cables and whipping them back across the beach. [Wikipedia]
- The January 1944 finale: it turned off course toward the photographer Klemantaski, shedding live rockets in all directions, while admirals and generals dived for cover behind the pebble ridge into barbed-wire entanglements. NOTE: attribute as witness/eyewitness account (from later retellings), not certainty. [Wikipedia; The Past]
- The dog: an army officer's Airedale named Ammonal (after the high explosive) chased one of the loose rockets. NOTE: attribute as witness/anecdote, often retold, not certainty. [Wikipedia; The Past]
