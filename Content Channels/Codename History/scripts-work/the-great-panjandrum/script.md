# EPISODE SCRIPT: THE GIANT FLAMING WHEEL BRITAIN BUILT TO WIN THE WAR

**Target runtime:** 11-13 minutes (~1,750 narration words at ~150 wpm)
**Style:** OverSimplified format. Comedic animated history. Flat colors, simple characters, exaggerated reactions, fake dialogue.
**Story type:** Single obscure real weapon (the Great Panjandrum, DMWD, 1943-1944). Pure comedy episode. No documented human cost.
**Title (standard tier):** `The Giant Flaming Wheel Britain Built to Win the War | Codename History`

> ACCURACY NOTE FOR PRODUCTION: Every device, date, and dimension here is real and sourced. The wildest trial anecdotes (the dog chasing a rocket, the photographer being chased, admirals diving into barbed wire) come from witness accounts and later retellings and are often embellished, so the narration attributes them as what witnesses said, not as certainties. Where sources disagree on the explosive load, both figures are given. Keep the jokes on the absurdity. Do not turn the "witnesses said" beats into flat fact.
>
> VOICE NOTE: This is the channel's pure-comedy episode. The comedy lives in the NARRATION. Deadpan delivery. The narrator describes a runaway bomb-wheel like it's a coworker's doomed side project. Fake dialogue for the DMWD and the brass. Second-person "you" pulls the viewer in. There is no human-cost beat here, nobody is documented killed or hurt, so the tone stays light throughout. Do not manufacture a tragedy.

## COLD OPEN (0:00 - 0:40)

**[VISUAL: A calm English holiday beach. Deck chairs, an ice cream cart, seagulls. Peaceful.]**

**NARRATOR:** This is a quiet beach in Devon, England, in 1943. Families. Deck chairs. A nice day out.

**[VISUAL: On the horizon, a giant wooden wheel taller than a man rolls into frame, spitting fire from rockets all around its rim.]**

**NARRATOR:** And that is a ten foot wheel packed with explosives, on fire, doing sixty miles an hour, coming straight at them. On purpose. Sort of.

**[VISUAL: A holidaymaker slowly lowers an ice cream cone, staring.]**

**NARRATOR:** This was a real weapon. The British government built it, funded it, and tested it, to help win the Second World War. It was named after a joke. And nothing about it worked the way anyone hoped.

**[TITLE CARD: "THE GIANT FLAMING WHEEL BRITAIN BUILT TO WIN THE WAR"]**

## ACT 1: THE DEPARTMENT OF BAD IDEAS (0:40 - 3:00)

**[VISUAL: A cramped Admiralty office labeled DMWD, cluttered with blueprints of ridiculous contraptions.]**

**NARRATOR:** To understand this, you need to meet the department that made it. The Directorate of Miscellaneous Weapons Development. The DMWD.

**[VISUAL: A brass plaque on the door reading "Miscellaneous Weapons." Below it, someone has scrawled "Wheezers and Dodgers."]**

**NARRATOR:** Even the Royal Navy did not know what to call these people, so it just went with Miscellaneous. Everyone else called them the Wheezers and Dodgers. This was the department for ideas too weird for any other department. If a plan sounded insane but might just work, it landed on their desk. And in fairness, some of their gadgets did work. This is not one of those stories.

**[VISUAL: A man at a drafting table, glasses, calm and precise. Nameplate: NEVIL SHUTE NORWAY.]**

**NARRATOR:** And one of the people working there was this man. Nevil Shute Norway. Aeronautical engineer. Also, in his spare time, a best selling novelist writing under the name Nevil Shute.

**[VISUAL: Shute holding a slide rule in one hand and a novel with his pen name on the cover in the other.]**

**NARRATOR:** So the man about to help design a flaming explosive wheel was, genuinely, a professional author. He kept the two careers separate so nobody at work would think the novelist was not a serious engineer. Which is a little funny, given what he was about to put on that beach.

**[VISUAL: A general slaps a photo on the desk. It shows a thick concrete wall along a coastline.]**

**NARRATOR:** Here was the problem they were handed. Germany had built the Atlantic Wall, a line of coastal defenses guarding occupied Europe. To land an invasion someday, the Allies would have to punch through concrete like this.

**[VISUAL: A ruler measuring the wall: ten feet tall, seven feet thick.]**

**NARRATOR:** Roughly ten feet high and seven feet thick. That is a lot of concrete. And the brief made it worse.

**[VISUAL: A memo. It reads: "Must be launched from a landing craft."]**

**NARRATOR:** Whatever cracked that wall had to be launched from a landing craft bobbing in the surf. So you could not exactly drive a truck up to it. Under enemy fire, on an open beach, you needed something that could get itself out of the water, across the sand, and into the wall, all on its own, carrying a very large bomb. Most people, handed that problem, would think of a vehicle. The DMWD thought of a wheel. Hold that thought.

---

## ACT 2: WHAT IF THE BOMB HAD WHEELS (3:00 - 5:15)

**[VISUAL: A lightbulb over the DMWD office. Inside the bulb, a crude sketch of a wheel with a bomb in the middle.]**

**NARRATOR:** And this is the idea they landed on. Do not carry the bomb. Make the bomb drive itself. Give it wheels.

**[VISUAL: Blueprint animation assembles the machine. Two big wooden wheels appear, then a drum slides in between them.]**

**NARRATOR:** Two large wooden wheels, ten feet across and about a foot wide, joined in the middle by a steel drum. And that drum was the bomb.

**[VISUAL: The drum fills with explosive. A dial next to it.]**

**NARRATOR:** How much explosive? Shute calculated you needed over a ton to crack that wall. The intended warhead was around four thousand pounds. Sources vary between the two, so just picture it as an enormous amount, more than most bombs of the war, sitting in the middle of a wheel, on a public beach.

**[VISUAL: Rockets snap onto the rim of each wheel, one after another, ringing the whole wheel.]**

**NARRATOR:** Now, how do you make a one ton wheel move? You bolt rockets all around the rim. Cordite rockets, dozens of them. As the wheel turns, the rockets on the rim fire and drag the whole thing forward, faster and faster, like the world's angriest Catherine wheel. At full load, they expected it to hit sixty miles an hour. A ton of explosive, doing sixty, steering itself. What could possibly go wrong.

**[VISUAL: The finished machine, rockets blazing, drum in the center. A tiny label points to it: "THE GREAT PANJANDRUM."]**

**NARRATOR:** They called it the Great Panjandrum. Which brings us to the name, because the name is fantastic.

**[VISUAL: Cut to the 1700s. A theater. A powdered-wig actor, Samuel Foote, scribbling gibberish.]**

**NARRATOR:** Back in the seventeen hundreds, an actor named Samuel Foote wrote a paragraph of complete nonsense, on purpose, to test whether a rival actor could really memorize anything after hearing it once.

**[VISUAL: The nonsense text scrolls by. The phrase "the grand Panjandrum" glows in the middle of it.]**

**NARRATOR:** The gibberish included a made up important sounding word. The grand Panjandrum. It meant nothing. It was a joke word for a pompous big shot.

**[VISUAL: Shute, back in 1943, pointing at his flaming wheel and grinning.]**

**NARRATOR:** So the novelist looked at his giant self important bomb wheel and named it after a two hundred year old joke about nonsense. Which, honestly, was the most accurate thing anyone did on this whole project.

---

## ACT 3: WESTWARD HO! (5:15 - 7:30)

**[VISUAL: A road sign: "WESTWARD HO!" with the exclamation mark included.]**

**NARRATOR:** They needed a beach to test it. They picked Westward Ho! in Devon. Yes, with the exclamation mark. That is the real name of the town.

**[VISUAL: The Panjandrum being unloaded off a truck at night, under tarps, men shushing each other.]**

**NARRATOR:** The whole thing was top secret. They moved it at night. They kept it covered. Very hush hush.

**[VISUAL: Daytime. The tarp comes off to reveal the giant wheel, and a crowd of holidaymakers with deck chairs is already watching.]**

**NARRATOR:** There was one flaw in the security plan. The beach they chose was a popular holiday spot. So the top secret weapon got tested in front of whoever happened to be at the seaside that day, despite the Navy politely warning them it might not be safe.

**[VISUAL: September 7, 1943. The Panjandrum on the sand, rockets loaded, a countdown.]**

**NARRATOR:** First trial. September seventh, nineteen forty three. Eighteen rockets, all lit at once.

**[VISUAL: It launches, spitting fire, and actually rolls straight down the beach for a good distance.]**

**NARRATOR:** And at first, it worked. The rockets caught, the wheel bit into the sand, and it tore down the beach for six hundred and sixty eight feet. That is more than two football fields, of flaming wheel, on target. For one glorious moment, the giant bomb was doing exactly what it was told, and somewhere a very proud engineer thought the hard part was over.

**[VISUAL: Then a single rocket pops off the rim and cartwheels away across the sand.]**

**NARRATOR:** And then a rocket fell off. Then another. And with rockets missing from one side, the wheel started pulling to that side.

**[VISUAL: The Panjandrum curving off course, leaning, then flopping over onto the sand.]**

**NARRATOR:** It veered. It leaned. It fell over. A one ton flaming wheel does not fall over quietly. But nobody was hurt, and the holidaymakers got a free show, so the team went back to the drawing board to fix the one small issue of the bomb steering itself into the sea.

---

## ACT 4: STEERING IS HARD (7:30 - 9:30)

**[VISUAL: Engineers add a third wheel in the middle of the Panjandrum for stability.]**

**NARRATOR:** Attempt two. Add a third wheel. More stability, right?

**[VISUAL: The three wheel version launches, immediately swerves, tilts, and crashes.]**

**NARRATOR:** The trial log for the three wheel version is one sentence long. "Swerved and tilted over. Damaged." That is the entire report. They took the third wheel back off.

**[VISUAL: Engineers now attach long steel cables to the wheels, held by winches, like reins on a horse.]**

**NARRATOR:** Attempt three. Steering cables. Run cables to each wheel, put men on winches, and steer it like a giant flaming puppet. Reasonable.

**[VISUAL: The Panjandrum launches, the cables go taut, and then snap, whipping back violently across the beach.]**

**NARRATOR:** The Panjandrum was too strong. It hit the end of the cables, and instead of turning, it snapped them clean and whipped them back across the beach like giant steel whips. So now the list of things flying around uncontrolled on this beach included loose rockets, loose steel cables, and, in the middle of it all, the giant bomb. The steering solution had made the beach more dangerous, not less.

**[VISUAL: A trial log entry: "Travelled in a tight spiral and overturned."]**

**NARRATOR:** Other trials went about as well. One log entry just reads, "travelled in a tight spiral and overturned." Every single time, the machine found a fresh new way to lose its mind. But they kept refining it. And they scheduled one last big demonstration to show it off.

**[VISUAL: An invitation card: "Panjandrum Demonstration, January 1944. Admirals and Generals welcome."]**

**NARRATOR:** January nineteen forty four. In front of the top brass. What could go wrong.

---

## ACT 5: THE GRAND FINALE (9:30 - 11:45)

**[VISUAL: A crowd of admirals and generals in dress uniforms lined up on the pebble ridge. Very serious. A photographer sets up a camera on a tripod.]**

**NARRATOR:** Picture the scene. Rows of admirals and generals in their finest. Scientists. And an official photographer named Klemantaski, there to capture Britain's newest wonder weapon for the record.

**[VISUAL: Klemantaski bent over his camera, eye pressed to a long telescopic lens, framing the shot.]**

**NARRATOR:** Klemantaski set up with a long telescopic lens, put his eye to the viewfinder, and got ready for the money shot. Which meant he was watching everything through a lens that made faraway things look close. Remember that. It matters in about thirty seconds.

**[VISUAL: The Panjandrum launches, rockets blazing, rolling fast down the beach.]**

**NARRATOR:** They lit it. Rockets roared to life all around the rim, and it surged forward down the sand. And for one perfect second, in front of all those medals and all that gold braid, the Great Panjandrum looked absolutely magnificent. This was the moment it was supposed to prove everyone right.

**[VISUAL: The wheel begins veering off course, tilting, flinging rockets off in every direction.]**

**NARRATOR:** Then, according to the witnesses, it did what it always did. It turned. It started shedding rockets in every direction. And it began heading, of all places, straight for the photographer.

**[VISUAL: Klemantaski still filming, not realizing through his zoom lens how close and how fast it now is.]**

**NARRATOR:** Because Klemantaski was watching through that zoom lens, the story goes that he could not tell how close it really was, and kept filming. Then he heard the roar, looked up over the camera, and saw a flaming one ton wheel almost on top of him.

**[VISUAL: Klemantaski bolting away from his tripod. Behind him, the row of admirals scattering.]**

**NARRATOR:** As he ran for it, witnesses say he glanced back and saw the assembled admirals and generals diving for cover behind the pebble ridge, some of them straight into the barbed wire.

**[VISUAL: An Airedale dog charging happily after a loose rocket bouncing across the sand.]**

**NARRATOR:** And in the retellings there is one more detail, and it is a good one. An officer's dog, an Airedale reportedly named Ammonal, after the high explosive, watched a live rocket bounce past across the sand, decided that looked like a wonderful time, and gave chase. Named after an explosive. Chasing an explosive. On a beach full of explosives.

**[VISUAL: The Panjandrum finally crashing down and disintegrating on the sand. A caption: "Witness accounts, often embellished."]**

**NARRATOR:** Now, fair warning. Those last few images come from eyewitnesses and later retellings, and they have grown in the telling over the years. What is solid is this. The machine broke away, threw its rockets, and disintegrated on the sand. And the wonder weapon demonstration ended with the Royal Navy's finest in the barbed wire.

## OUTRO (11:45 - 12:15)

**[VISUAL: The wrecked Panjandrum on an empty beach. Engineers walking away, hands in pockets.]**

**NARRATOR:** They never solved the steering. Trial after trial, the Great Panjandrum found a new way to embarrass everyone standing near it, and eventually the Navy quietly shelved it. It was never used in battle. And here is the best part. Across every single test, nobody is recorded as being killed or hurt by any of it. Its entire body count was the dignity of several admirals and one very confused dog.

**[VISUAL: The real invasion happening elsewhere with ordinary landing craft and engineers, no giant wheels.]**

**NARRATOR:** When the invasion finally came, it was done the boring, careful way. The flaming wheel stayed a footnote. But it was a real weapon, seriously built, seriously funded, by real people trying to win a war with a bomb on wheels.

**[VISUAL: End card. Subscribe button. Two thumbnails for next episodes.]**

**NARRATOR:** For the true, strange, forgotten weapons of the war that history quietly filed under Miscellaneous, subscribe. Next time: the plan to build an aircraft carrier out of ice.

**[END]**

## PRODUCTION NOTES

**Voice choices:**
- Pure comedy throughout. There is no documented human cost, so the tone never has to drop. The one restrained beat is the outro line confirming nobody was killed or hurt, which is played as a light payoff, not a somber turn.
- Every wild anecdote (photographer chased, admirals in the barbed wire, the dog Ammonal) is explicitly framed as "witnesses say / the story goes / in the retellings," with an on-screen caption "Witness accounts, often embellished," per the accuracy caveat.
- Fake dialogue was kept minimal because the machine itself is the joke; the DMWD "Wheezers and Dodgers" nickname and the deadpan trial-log quotes carry the comedy.
- Second-person "you" used in the design act ("how do you make a one ton wheel move").

**Accuracy corrections / notes:**
- Explosive load given as a range on purpose: Shute's "over one ton" requirement vs the "around four thousand pounds" design warhead. Not flattened to a single number.
- Naming traced accurately to Samuel Foote's nonsense passage and "the grand Panjandrum," not treated as a random invented word.
- "Never used in battle" and "problems never solved" taken verbatim in spirit from the Nevil Shute Foundation DMWD narrative.

## FACT-CHECK LOG

- **DMWD / "Wheezers and Dodgers":** Directorate (originally Department) of Miscellaneous Weapons Development, Admiralty experimental-weapons body, nicknamed Wheezers and Dodgers. Confirmed. [Wikipedia DMWD; Nevil Shute Foundation]
- **Nevil Shute Norway:** aeronautical engineer and novelist (pen name Nevil Shute), RNVR, DMWD department head; named the Panjandrum. Confirmed. [Nevil Shute Foundation; Wikipedia "Nevil Shute"; The Engineer]
- **Purpose / Atlantic Wall:** device to breach roughly 10 ft high, 7 ft thick concrete, launchable from a landing craft. Confirmed. [Wikipedia "Panjandrum"; The Past]
- **Dimensions:** two wooden wheels 10 ft (3 m) diameter, about 1 ft (0.3 m) wide, steel drum between. Confirmed. [Wikipedia; The Past; War History Online]
- **Explosive load:** Shute calculated over 1 long ton needed; intended warhead about 4,000 lb (1,800 kg). RANGE given deliberately. Confirmed both figures. [Wikipedia; The Past]
- **Rockets:** cordite rockets on the rims; first trial 18 rockets; later trials over seventy; about 20 lb each. Confirmed. [Wikipedia; Nevil Shute Foundation trial log]
- **Predicted speed:** around 60 mph (100 km/h) at full load. Confirmed. [Wikipedia; The Past]
- **First trial:** 7 September 1943, Westward Ho!, Devon; ran 668 feet. Confirmed. [Nevil Shute Foundation DMWD Narrative; Wikipedia]
- **Holiday-beach secrecy failure:** test beach was a popular holiday spot; trials had civilian audiences despite safety warnings. Confirmed. [Wikipedia]
- **Trial failures:** rockets detaching, veering, three-wheel version "swerved and tilted over - damaged," another "travelled in a tight spiral and overturned," steering cables snapped and whipped back. Confirmed. [Wikipedia; Nevil Shute Foundation trial log]
- **January 1944 demonstration:** before officials, scientists, and photographer Klemantaski; device broke away, shed rockets, headed toward Klemantaski; observers dived for cover. Attributed as WITNESS accounts, often embellished. Confirmed as sourced anecdote, flagged as such. [Wikipedia; The Past; Amusing Planet]
- **Dog "Ammonal":** Airedale said to have chased a loose rocket. Attributed as retold anecdote, not certainty. [Wikipedia; The Past]
- **Never used in action:** "The problems of controlling the Panjandrum were never solved and it was not used in action." Confirmed. [Nevil Shute Foundation DMWD Narrative; Wikipedia]
- **No casualties:** no death or injury documented in any consulted source. Stated as "nobody recorded killed or hurt," not as absolute proof. Confirmed as NOT FOUND for any casualty.
- **Samuel Foote / "grand Panjandrum":** 18th-century nonsense passage written to test actor Charles Macklin's memory; "panjandrum" a coined joke word. Confirmed. [World Wide Words; Wikipedia "The Great Panjandrum Himself"]
