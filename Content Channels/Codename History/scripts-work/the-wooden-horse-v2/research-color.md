# Research Report (Color and Absurdity): The Wooden Horse

**Story:** The Wooden Horse escape from Stalag Luft III, East Compound, Sagan (now Zagan, Poland). Digging began 8 July 1943. Breakout on the evening of Friday 29 October 1943. Escapers: Flight Lieutenant Eric Williams (RAF), Lieutenant Richard Michael Clinton Codner (Royal Artillery), Flight Lieutenant Oliver Lawrence Spurling Philpot (RAF).

**Lane:** documented absurdity, texture and comedy fuel. A separate agent verifies the factual spine. Everything below is sourced or explicitly marked NOT FOUND.

**Sourcing note up front:** the two richest veins are (1) Imperial War Museums catalogue records and the IWM oral history reel summaries for Philpot (catalogue 9938, recorded 21 September 1987), and (2) the Daily Telegraph obituary of Philpot published 6 May 1993, which is unusually detailed and reads as if drawn from his MI9 debrief and from "Stolen Journey". Where a beat rests on only one of these, I say so.

## Camp life, slang and the ferrets

### Confirmed slang, with definitions

The Stalag Luft III POW glossary (stalagluft3.com, a dedicated camp-history site) gives these verbatim:

- **Goon:** "German Guard or Officer"
- **Goon Box:** "Sentry tower manned by Guards"
- **Goon Baiting:** "Needling and pranking the Guards"
- **Ferret:** "Group of German Guards tasked with detecting tunnels"
- **Penguin:** "POW engaged in the disposal of tunnel waste"
- **Kriegie:** "A POW, derived from German word kriegsgefangener"
- **Cooler:** "Solitary confinement block in the vorlager"
- **Stooge:** "POW involved in surveillance of the Guards"

All eight terms are genuinely Stalag Luft III usage. Corroboration for goon, goon box, goon baiting, ferret and penguin also appears in the 392nd Bomb Group camp history (b24.net) and in Wikipedia's Stalag Luft III article.

**The "goon" joke is real and is the single best slang beat.** The men told the guards that "goon" was an acronym for "German Officer Or Non-Com," and the guards, not knowing the English insult, accepted the nickname willingly. Sources: b24.net Stalag Luft III page; Wikipedia Stalag Luft III. Two sources, but they may share lineage. Treat as strongly attested, widely repeated, and note it is a POW-told story about themselves.

**"Goon-baiting" is confirmed as a named, deliberate camp activity**, not a later coinage. This matters because the Wooden Horse crew's stunts (below) are literal documented goon-baiting.

**Direct usage in a 1949 first-hand account.** A prisoner present at the camp wrote in Air Mail magazine in 1949, quoted by the RAF Association: "When the 'goons' [guards] realised that the whole proceeding had been carried on right under the nose of the guard in the 'goon-box', day after day, week after week, their sense of humour, never very noticeable, evaporated completely." That is a contemporary POW using both "goons" and "goon-box" in print, which settles the slang question.

### The ferrets

- Ferrets were a separate specialist anti-escape unit, described by seat61 (drawing on the Zagan museum) as "a separate group attached to the military intelligence service. Their job was to prevent escapes, ferreting out tunnels and hides of escape material."
- Method, per b24.net and Wikipedia: they could enter the compound at any time and search any hut without warning; they carried long metal probes to push into the ground hunting for the bright yellow subsoil; they hid under the raised huts to listen to prisoners talking. Blue overalls are the standard description in the popular literature but I did not find a Tier 1 confirmation of the uniform colour (see Gaps).
- Barracks at Stalag Luft III were deliberately "raised approximately 60 cm (24 in) off the ground to make it easier for guards to detect tunnelling" (Wikipedia, Stalag Luft III).
- The soil trick: "the surface soil was dark grey, so it could easily be detected if anyone dumped the brighter, yellow sand found beneath it" (Wikipedia, Stalag Luft III). Confirmed independently by the RAF Association and by the National Archives.

### "Rubberneck" (Karl Griese)

Real person. Unteroffizier Karl Griese (spelling also appears as Greise), nicknamed "Rubberneck" for a conspicuously long neck, was one of the two principal ferrets at Stalag Luft III and, per stalagluft3.com, "had developed a reputation as the most wiley and formidable of that specialist team." IWM holds a March 1944 photograph captioned as Corporal Karl Greise, nicknamed 'Rubberneck', demonstrating a ventilation device for the 'Harry' tunnel (Alamy reproduction of the IWM image, IWM Great Escape collection).

**Caution for the writer.** Every source I found places Rubberneck's documented activity in the North Compound and around the Great Escape of March 1944, including the well known fact that he was away on leave the night of the Great Escape. **I did NOT find a source placing Rubberneck in the East Compound during the Wooden Horse dig (July to October 1943).** Do not have him personally circling the vaulting horse. If the script wants a named ferret, it has to be flagged as Great Escape era, or use generic "ferrets".

### Seismograph microphones (handle with care)

- The microphones are real and well attested. Wikipedia's Stalag Luft III article lists among the anti-escape measures "Seismograph microphones around the perimeter of the camp, which were expected to detect any sounds of digging." The National Archives exhibition text likewise notes the Germans "employed seismographs to detect vibration caused by digging." stalagluft3.com: "A system of seismographic microphones also ringed the compound, specifically designed to detect tunneling activity."
- **The claim that the thumping of the vaulters masked the digging from those microphones is where I would slow down.** It is repeated everywhere, but every version I could trace runs back to the same Wikipedia-lineage sentence ("The gymnastics not only disguised the real purpose of the vaulting horse, but the activity kept the sound of the digging from being detected by the microphones"), reproduced on Military Wiki, everything-everywhere, spyscape and others. Two sites copying one paragraph are not two sources.
- What the participant-adjacent sources actually stress is different and simpler: the horse existed because the tunnel had to START close to the wire, and there was nothing near the wire to hide a trapdoor under. The Telegraph obituary describes "the ruse of placing a hollow wooden vaulting horse some 100ft from the camp wire." The RAF Association account says the point was to get an entrance near the perimeter and to hide the yellow spoil. Wikipedia's Eric Williams article does mention seismographs among the obstacles.
- **Recommendation:** the script can say the Germans buried listening gear and that a hundred men landing on the same square yard of sand every afternoon was not a helpful acoustic environment for it. Do not state as fact that masking the microphones was the men's stated reason for vaulting, because I could not source that to a participant.

## The horse itself

### What it was built from

Multiple independent sources, converging:

- **Red Cross crates / plywood from Red Cross parcels.** RAF Association: "a wooden vaulting horse made from Red Cross crates." National Archives: "a hollow vaulting horse made from Red Cross boxes." seat61: "a wooden vaulting horse out of plywood from red cross parcels."
- **The fullest version, from Wikipedia's Philpot article citing "Stolen Journey":** "A vaulting horse was constructed from stolen timber, plywood with cigarette package wrappings used for the top." So the top surface, the bit men actually landed on, was faced with cigarette packet wrappers. That is a beautiful detail and it is single-sourced to Philpot's own book via Wikipedia. Flag as such.
- The tunnel shaft itself was then lined with "plywood panels from Red Cross parcel boxes" (same source).

### Dimensions and weight

**Conflicted and unresolved. Do not put a number on screen.**

- One widely reproduced set (2ndww blog, echoed in search summaries): four feet six inches high, base five feet by three feet.
- A different set (throughouthistory.com): roughly three feet high, two feet wide at the base, with a shaft two feet six inches square and five feet deep, and a trapdoor eighteen inches below the surface.
- Neither is Tier 1. They disagree. Marked NOT VERIFIED.

**Weight: NOT FOUND.** No source I found gives a weight for the horse loaded with a man, tools and sandbags.

**Carrying party:** the RAF Association states the horse "would be carried out of the recreation hut by four fellow prisoners, while one or two escapers clung on inside." Note the verb: *clung on*. The men inside were not sitting comfortably, they were braced against the frame while it swung. Corroborated in substance by IWM and by seat61 (one or two diggers concealed inside).

**Carrying poles:** throughouthistory.com describes "long wooden shafts that could be inserted through holes on its sides," used both to carry it and, they say, to admit air. Single source, Tier 3, but mechanically consistent with a four-man carry. Flag.

**A documented near-drop or stumble: NOT FOUND.** What IS documented is the opposite, a deliberate drop (see next).

### Did the Germans inspect the horse?

**Yes, and this is confirmed at Tier 1 in outline.** The IWM catalogue for Philpot's oral history lists, in Reel 5, the topics: "construction of wooden horse; German suspicion of wooden horse; methods of disposal of sand from tunnel." Reel 6 opens with "security scares." So German suspicion of the horse is an explicitly catalogued theme of the participant's own testimony.

The detail of that inspection comes from lower-tier retellings of Williams' book: guards examined the horse closely in its first days looking for hidden compartments, and for several weeks the horse was used for nothing but genuine vaulting so the guards would get bored of it (throughouthistory.com; also surfaced in search summaries of Williams' text). Treat the *fact* of inspection as solid, the *specifics* as memoir-derived.

### The deliberate pratfalls (best-sourced comedy in the whole file)

From the RAF Association account, which is drawn from an Air Mail article of 1949 written by men who were there:

> "Sometimes the horse would be carried out empty and 'accidentally' knocked over. On one occasion, the prisoners even left the vaulting horse, which covered the tunnel entrance, and ran a circuit around the compound. These incidents helped to allay any suspicion among the watching guards."

Two separate stunts, both documented: they knocked their own escape tunnel over on purpose to prove it was just a box, and they once walked away from it entirely and jogged a lap so the guards could see nobody was hiding in it. This is textbook goon-baiting as an engineering control.

### The Trojan Horse joke: the men made it themselves

**Confirmed, and not a later framing.**

- IWM object record for Philpot's escape compass (EPH 6366): the three "made their escape after 114 days of digging on 29 October 1943 concealed by using a hollow wooden vaulting horse **inspired by the story of the 'Trojan Horse'**."
- Telegraph obituary, 6 May 1993: "Flt-Lt Eric Williams, **whose idea was inspired by the Trojan Horse**, and Lt R M C Codner, Royal Artillery, wedged themselves inside the horse."
- Wikipedia's Michael Codner article: "Drawing inspiration from the Trojan Horse legend."
- Kirkus's 1952 review of "Stolen Journey" calls it "the 'Trojan Horse' tunnel scheme," i.e. the framing was in the contemporary reception too.
- Williams titled his own book "The Wooden Horse."

Three thousand years after Troy, British officers reused the gag, and the Germans, in a country that taught Homer in its schools, watched them carry a hollow wooden horse toward the wall every afternoon for four months.

### How the entrance was hidden each day

- "After each session, care was taken to secure the top of the shaft and to replace the surface sand on top." (Wikipedia, Philpot, citing "Stolen Journey")
- More specific, from mid-tier retellings: a wooden board or plywood sheet was laid over the shaft mouth, the hollow above it filled with sandbags and dry sand, and the grey surface soil brushed back over the top. The trapdoor sat about eighteen inches below the surface (throughouthistory.com). Single-sourced on the measurements.
- The RAF Association confirms the practice generally: they were "carefully replacing the topsoil each day."
- **Registration detail worth using:** "Two visible ground indentations created by repeated vaulting marked the exact tunnel location for consistent access" (throughouthistory.com, single source). The dents made by four months of men landing became the aiming marks for putting the horse back on the trapdoor. Nice if the writer wants it, but flag as single-sourced.

## The digging

### Air

- **They did not run a proper ventilation line.** The account repeated by Look and Learn and others is blunt: "There was an ever-present danger of suffocation in the tunnel, because the tunnellers dared not bore airholes."
- Wikipedia's Stalag Luft III article says they dug "over 30 m (100 ft) of tunnel, using bowls as shovels **and metal rods to poke through the surface**," which is corroborated in search-level sources describing "rods of metal to poke through the surface of the ground to create air holes." So: they punched occasional holes up through the roof with a rod and hoped.
- These two statements are in tension (dared not bore airholes vs poked rods through). The reconciliation in the memoir-derived accounts is that the rod was used sparingly and mainly to check depth and direction. **Flag as a conflict.** Do not build a joke on either version alone.
- Light was candles, in already oxygen-poor air (throughouthistory.com, single source).

### Hauling the digger and the sand

Best single passage, from Wikipedia's Philpot article citing "Stolen Journey":

> "Digging was a cramped job and sand was dragged back to the bottom of the shaft by the digger and then placed into bags for lifting and disposal. Only 12 bags per session were lifted. After a while, the team swapped to a four-cycle dig-and-lift scheme, where two men went out on the first trip and dug 36 bags of sand. For the next three trips, one man went out and recovered 12 bags each time. As the tunnel grew longer, a bowl and string were used for pulling the sand back."

The reason they changed the system is the funniest and most human logistics note in the story: "it meant three shorter sessions and one longer one for the volunteers using the horse for vaulting, **an important change as the number of volunteers was small and they were becoming tired**." The tunnel schedule was redesigned around the fact that the gym class was knackered.

Sand handling on the surface: bags were sewn from cut-off trouser legs; the sand went back inside the horse with the digger and was unloaded in a hut. Eric Williams' Wikipedia article adds a specific: "Sand was carried back inside the horse and **dried in the attic of the camp canteen** before being distributed in the compound."

Mac Colquhoun, a Royal Canadian Air Force POW who was one of the men handling the spoil, told War History Online they hid sand "in vegetable gardens, roof spaces, and even poured some down a theater stage hole," and that other prisoners walked behind the penguins "kicking up the soil around" them to mask the trail. First-hand, named participant, in a mid-tier outlet.

### Collapses and the one great beat

**The cave-in with the faked twisted ankle.** The account, consistent across Look and Learn, throughouthistory.com and search-level reproductions of Williams' text:

Codner was in the tunnel when part of the roof gave way and buried him. The collapse broke through to the surface, opening a visible hole in the middle of the exercise area, in daylight, in front of the guards. A vaulter coming over the horse saw the hole in mid-air, **deliberately mistimed his landing, fell over the hole and lay on top of it**, pretending he had twisted his ankle, while underneath him Codner dug himself out and shored the roof with planks pulled from the shaft lining.

This is the single best action beat in the story. **Sourcing caveat:** I could not tie it to a Tier 1 record. It is memoir-derived (Williams) and reaches me through Tier 2/3 retellings. It is not a film invention as far as I can tell, but the writer should treat it as "as Williams told it."

**Other near-misses, Tier 1 confirmed in outline:** the IWM oral history lists "security scares" and "testing direction of tunnel" (Reel 6). Wikipedia's Philpot article, citing "Stolen Journey," states: "The tunnel had several narrow escapes from detection or accidental destruction **due to digging by either the guards or other POWs**." Other prisoners nearly destroyed the tunnel by digging in the compound without knowing it was there.

**The tunnel was going the wrong way.** Also from "Stolen Journey" via Wikipedia: "Experimentation with a poker showed that the tunnel was about 30 inches (76 cm) below ground **but also running approximately 30 degrees off the intended course**." Four months of work, thirty degrees off. They found out by poking a stick up through the ground.

**Depth:** roughly 30 inches, i.e. two and a half feet of sand between the diggers and the boots of the men vaulting. This is a Tier 2 number from the memoir.

### Vaulting on a hollow box with a man underneath

- Confirmed structurally: the first stretch of tunnel had to be reinforced because of the pounding. throughouthistory.com: "The initial seven feet of tunnel were reinforced with wooden bedboards on all sides due to constant landing impact from vaulters above." Single source, but it is the obvious engineering consequence and matches the shaft-lining detail in "Stolen Journey."
- The vaulters' condition is documented and is a somber-comic note: the RAF Association says "the prisoners had to continue exercising over the vaulting horse, despite their poor physical condition caused by a near-starvation diet. And the further the tunnel grew, the longer the vaulters had to go on jumping." Sessions ran two to three hours (Telegraph obituary: "as PoWs vaulted for two or three hours at a stretch").
- **Anyone actually hurt vaulting: NOT FOUND.** The only injury in the record is the fake one.

### Getting the routine past the guards

- They ran the horse out for weeks of genuine, pointless exercise before a single shovel went in, purely to bore the guards into ignoring it (throughouthistory.com; consistent with search-level summaries of Williams).
- The horse always went to the same spot, every day, near the perimeter (RAF Association; seat61: "the exercise area outside the canteen").
- The Germans did get suspicious and the horse was inspected (IWM oral history Reel 5, "German suspicion of wooden horse"), and the prisoners answered with the knock-it-over and walk-away-from-it stunts (RAF Association / Air Mail 1949).
- The whole thing happened, per the 1949 POW account, "right under the nose of the guard in the 'goon-box', day after day, week after week."

## The escape and the run

### The timetable of 29 October 1943

From the Telegraph obituary, 6 May 1993:

- Digging began **8 July 1943**.
- **1pm, 29 October:** Codner was sealed into the tunnel and stayed underground all day.
- Codner's absence from evening roll call was covered and "the count was falsified" (Telegraph; Wikipedia/"Stolen Journey" says his absence "was covered").
- Williams and Philpot were then carried out in the horse, with a fourth man, **McKay**, who sealed the three of them in and went back with the horse (Wikipedia, citing "Stolen Journey").
- **5pm:** all three sealed in. They broke out **just after 6pm**.
- They surfaced "some 12ins short of the target spot, **to find themselves right in the sentry's path. Fortunately the night patrol was late**." (Telegraph)

Twelve inches short of the mark, in the sentry's walking line, saved by German unpunctuality.

### The break-out, in Philpot's own voice

The National Archives "Great Escapes" exhibition plays a recording of Philpot describing the moment. Transcribed on the exhibition page:

> "We're watching the watch carefully and when we reckoned it was, it was dark then Williams said 'Ah, we're breaking now.' He shouted back to me, 'Follow me.' I got up to the front of the tunnel and, and there I could see the night's sky above, and I looked up and I saw the German looking in. He's looking in to the camp, fortunately. Well there's no time to study him or anything, so I hoicked my bundles up one after another and followed them up, and then streaked across the little road, hauled myself out, streaked across the little road and in to those heavenly pine trees, which gave cover."

He climbed out of the ground and made eye level with a German sentry who was, at that exact second, staring the other way, into the camp he had just left.

They were dressed in "black clothes and face masks" for the break (Telegraph).

### Philpot's cover identity: the margarine man

**The irony is real and it is documented from several directions.**

- Prewar: Philpot joined Unilever as a management trainee in 1934 and in 1936 "was appointed assistant commercial secretary in Unilever's home margarine executive. This proved to be useful in his wartime escape as the post required him to travel extensively in Germany and to learn to speak German." (Wikipedia, Philpot)
- The Telegraph puts it flatly: "His cover story was that he was a margarine executive (his own peacetime occupation) on exchange from Norway to Berlin."
- The character: "a Norwegian margarine salesman called Jon Jörgensen." He picked Norwegian because Norwegians were common enough in Germany to be unremarkable "but with a reduced likelihood of meeting someone who spoke Norwegian, **as Philpot did not speak any Norwegian**." A Norwegian POW helped flesh out the character, "even to the extent of making him a Quisling." (Wikipedia, Philpot, citing "Stolen Journey")
- The full cover story, from a specialist escape-lines blog citing Philpot's own IS9 file at the National Archives: "Herr Jon Jorgensen a Quisling Norwegian" on exchange from **Denofa** (the real Norwegian oil and fat company at Fredrikstad) to the **Margarine Verkaufs Union, Berlin**, "doing a tour of branches and factories throughout Greater Germany."
- **He built the character BEFORE he had an escape.** "Philpot hit upon the idea of inventing an escape character and papers etc. before involvement with any escape, rather than the opposite way round." (Wikipedia, Philpot) He made himself a fake identity speculatively, then went looking for a hole in the ground to use it with.
- **He was the escape committee's hut coordinator.** Williams and Codner came to Philpot in June 1943 "to 'register' their escape scheme with the escape committee, Philpot being the escape co-ordinator for the hut in which the three of them lived." He approved their plan, then talked his way onto it.

### The document list (from his IS9 file)

Per the escape-lines blog citing IS9 files at the National Archives, Philpot's papers were:

- Vorlaufige Ausweis (temporary identity card, an original)
- Two polizeiliche Erlaubnisse (police travel permissions, one an original)
- Bescheinigung (certificate)
- Arbeitskarte (work card)
- Bestatigung (confirmation of workbook issue)
- A typed letter from the Margarine Verkaufs Union introducing him
- **A typed letter from Nasjonal Samling inviting him to attend a Quisling speech**
- A Nasjonal Samling membership card
- A Swedish sailor's pass, which Philpot himself described as "very bogus"

He was carrying, among his forged escape documents, an invitation to a fascist rally.

### The props

From the Telegraph obituary:

> "He carried a small suitcase containing shaving gear and wore a black Homburg, an RAF officer's greatcoat and gloves, new shoes, a pair of Fleet Air Arm officer's trousers and a black civilian jacket. **He chewed on a pipe, as an excuse for slurring his speech, and for good measure he sported a Hitler moustache.**"

The pipe was not decoration. It was a functional device to make his accent unreadable. And he grew a Hitler moustache.

His jacket survives: IWM EPH 6364, "Double-breasted black jacket with pockets inside and out, four large plastic buttons down each side (eight in total), leather hook for hanging inside neck; as worn by Oliver Philpot during his escape." His necktie survives too: IWM EPH 6375, 111cm by 8.5cm.

### The compass, and Philpot's own words about it

IWM EPH 6366, physical description: "compass (7.5 x 2.5cm), handmade; bowl from a gramophone disc, needle from a gramophone needle (which fits into the press stud of an RAF officer's uniform), cardboard compass card, magnetisation from two halves of a razor blade affixed to the underside of the card, phosphorous from broken watches, and blackened with soot."

Made for him in two days by **Jerry Dawkins**, an RAF bomber pilot from Coventry. Philpot's own account, quoted in full on the IWM record from "Stolen Journey" pp. 284 to 285, is in the quotes section below. The razor blades were magnetised off the camp's own electric light circuit.

### Williams and Codner's cover

- They travelled together **posing as French workmen** (Wikipedia, Philpot, citing "Stolen Journey"; Wikipedia, Eric Williams: "posed as French labourers"). Codner spoke French.
- Route: Sagan to Frankfurt an der Oder, then to Stettin via Küstrin. They made contact with conscripted French forced labourers and with Danish seamen.
- Williams "credited their escape from Germany to the Frenchmen who'd been conscripted as forced labor and a 19-year-old Danish seaman who smuggled them aboard his ship" (per search-level summary of Williams' account; single-sourced, flag).
- **Conflict on the ship's nationality.** IWM (EPH 6366 record) says they "finally embarked for Sweden via Denmark on 6 November" and arrived Stockholm six days later. Wikipedia's Stalag Luft III and Eric Williams articles say a Danish ship and Danish Resistance contacts. Wikipedia's Michael Codner article says a Swedish ship. **Most credible reading: a Danish ship, via Denmark.** Note the disagreement.

### Philpot's run, and the near-misses

Route: Sagan station to Frankfurt an der Oder, then Küstrin, then Danzig, then a Swedish ship to Sweden.

**The cancelled train.** His night train from Frankfurt an der Oder to Küstrin was cancelled. He had to spend the night outdoors in woods and catch the slow train in the morning (Wikipedia, citing "Stolen Journey"; corroborated by the IWM reel summary for Reel 6/7, "cancellation of his Kustrin train" and "night spent in open at Frankfurt an der Oder"). The first thing that nearly beat the greatest escape of the war was German rail scheduling.

**The best near-catch, and it is a joke.** Telegraph obituary:

> "The next morning he caught the slow train to Kustrin, where he joined the Konigsberg Express, went to asleep sitting on his suitcase in a third-class gangway, fell off and exclaimed 'Damn]' But his fellow passengers simply laughed."

(The "]" is an OCR artefact for an exclamation mark in the archived text.) Wikipedia's version, from "Stolen Journey": "After almost giving himself away by falling off his case and swearing in English, his false papers were good enough to survive checking by a police officer." So: he fell asleep on his suitcase, fell off it, swore in English in a carriage full of Germans, and the carriage laughed at him instead of arresting him.

**The document check.** Telegraph: "Challenged by a plainclothes policeman on the train, Philpot fobbed him off with an identity card which displayed the mug shot of a fellow officer." The IWM reel summary for Reel 7 lists "inspection of his documents on train; **revelation of the inaccuracy of his documents**." His ID card had somebody else's face on it and it worked.

**Lavatories.** IWM Reel 7 lists "problems with lavatory in Frankfurt an der Oder Railway Station" and "use of lavatory to prepare for his journey." Kirkus's 1952 review sums his run up as "Living on Ovaitine tablets, hiding out in lavatories." A meaningful proportion of this escape happened in German railway toilets.

**Danzig.** Less than 24 hours out of the camp (Telegraph says 23 hours from breaking ground). He then "treated himself to a glass of beer in the station refreshment room, took a tram to recce the docks and returned to an hotel near the station, where he was obliged to share a room." He checked into a hotel in occupied Danzig and had to share it with a stranger. The next morning he took a **harbour pleasure cruise** to scout for a neutral ship (IWM Reel 7: "pleasure trip around Danzig Harbour").

**Boarding the Aralizz.** Telegraph: he "climbed up a mooring cable and wriggled on to the deck. After lying low for a while he crawled to a door leading to a galley, **drank the hot chocolate he found simmering there** and stowed away in a coal bunker." IWM Reel 8 lists "attempt to hide in coal; hiding in oily tank."

**The captain.** The crew spotted him. The captain ordered him off because he was endangering the ship. Philpot refused. The chief engineer and a seaman hid him without the captain's knowledge until the ship sailed on Tuesday. Once at sea, Philpot revealed himself again and the captain "took a different attitude now the ship was no longer in port," inviting him to be a guest for the rest of the voyage (Wikipedia, citing "Stolen Journey"; Telegraph). Danger to the crew in harbour, dinner guest in international waters.

**Arrival.** Docked Södertälje around midnight on 3 November. **He spent his first night of freedom in a Swedish police cell** (Telegraph). Then walked into the British Legation in Stockholm on Thursday 4 November 1943, less than five days after the breakout. He changed disguise again on the ship, to a Swedish merchant seaman (IWM Reel 7).

He was reunited with Codner and Williams a week later. All three flew home. Philpot got back to the UK on a BOAC flight just before Christmas 1943.

### A "German helped by accident" beat

**The best documented one is passive, not active:** the sentry who was looking the wrong way, and the night patrol that was late. Neither is a German knowingly helping.

I found **no** documented instance of a German actively and unwittingly assisting the Wooden Horse escapers. (There is a well documented one at a different camp: German POWs escaping Camp 198 at Bridgend in Wales in March 1945 stole a doctor's car, it would not start, and "four unsuspecting prison guards helped them get it" going, per the National Archives. That is a different story and must not be transplanted.)

### What the Germans found

RAF Association, from the 1949 Air Mail account:

> "Later that night, a guard walking outside the wire stumbled when his foot disappeared into a hole in the ground. The alarm was raised, but the three escapees were long gone."

A guard discovered the greatest escape of the war by standing in it.

> "The Germans roused the remaining prisoners, but numerous roll calls and identity checks had to be abandoned when prisoners continually disrupted proceedings to buy the escapees more time. To restore order, a few hundred German soldiers guarding a nearby aerodrome were drafted in, and the checks restarted with the compound now surrounded by heavily armed guards. This led to the day being known thereafter as **'Tommy-gun Saturday'**."

The prisoners deliberately sabotaged their own roll call, repeatedly, until the Germans had to import several hundred extra troops from an aerodrome.

### What happened to the horse

**NOT FOUND.** No source I could find states the fate of the original vaulting horse. It does not appear in any museum catalogue I searched. IWM holds dioramas of the horse (objects 30019628 and 30019652) and much of the original escape kit, but not the horse itself. The only full-size horse that exists today is the modern replica **Robert J. Laplander built for research** while writing "The True Story of the Wooden Horse" (Pen and Sword, 2014). **Do not claim the horse survives, and do not claim it was burned. Both are unsourced.**

## Aftermath and legacy

### The books

- **Eric Williams** wrote a short first version on the voyage home aboard RMS *Queen Mary* at the end of the war, titled **"Goon in the Block"**. Four years later he rewrote it, much longer and in the third person, as **"The Wooden Horse" (1949)**. Sources: Wikipedia, Eric Williams (writer); corroborated in search-level summaries. He later wrote "The Tunnel" (1951) about the earlier failed Szubin escape.
- Williams changed the names because he was writing while bound by the **Official Secrets Act**, and the escape techniques were still live intelligence. historyofwar.org's review notes he later removed "a fictional incident that he later regretted have added" in a revised edition.
- **Oliver Philpot**, "Stolen Journey", London: Hodder and Stoughton, **1950**, illustrated by **Ronald Searle** (who had himself been a POW of the Japanese on the Burma Railway). Kirkus reviewed the US edition on 15 June **1952**. IWM's catalogue label says **1953**. **Conflict noted:** the UK first edition is 1950; the later dates are the US edition and a probable IWM cataloguing slip.
- IWM holds Philpot's private papers including "first ms draft for his autobiography 'Stolen Journey' (64pp, incomplete, March 1944)". He started writing the book **five months after getting home**, while the war still had over a year to run.

### The 1950 film and the fictionalisation (correct this on screen)

The film "The Wooden Horse" (dir. Jack Lee, released 16 October 1950) uses invented names for all three real men:

| Real man | Film character | Played by |
|---|---|---|
| Eric Williams | **Peter Howard** | Leo Genn |
| Michael Codner | **John Clinton** | Anthony Steel |
| Oliver Philpot | **Philip Rowe** | David Tomlinson |

Sources: Wikipedia, The Wooden Horse (film); Kristen Alexander (author of Australian POW histories), "Not quite a film review: The Wooden Horse".

**A detail worth catching:** Codner's full name was **Richard Michael Clinton Codner** (IWM catalogue, Philpot oral history associated persons). The film's fictional name "John **Clinton**" quietly reuses his real third given name. Williams wrote the screenplay himself.

Williams wrote both the book and the screenplay. The film was the third most popular British film of 1950 in UK box office terms (£266,545). It was shot largely in Germany, with the camp rebuilt near Lüneburg Heath.

### Peter Butterworth: the best legacy beat in the story

**Confirmed at Tier 1 by the National Archives.** Peter Butterworth, later famous as a "Carry On" comic actor, was a Fleet Air Arm pilot shot down on 21 June 1940 and held at Stalag Luft III. He was **one of the men who vaulted over the horse** to cover the diggers.

National Archives, "Great Escapes" exhibition text:

> "Peter Butterworth, the comic actor best known for the 'Carry On' films, auditioned for a part in The Wooden Horse. **He didn't get it, which is ironic because in 1943 he was actually one of the camp inmates who helped cover for the escapers.**"

Wikipedia's film article gives the producers' stated reason: he "didn't look convincingly heroic or athletic enough." Kristen Alexander's write-up: "one real vaulter, Peter Butterworth, could not get a part in the film because he looked neither athletic nor heroic enough."

He was rejected for being insufficiently convincing as himself.

**Caution on the "too fat" version.** Some low-tier sites (broread, feedc, IMDb trivia) claim the real reason was that he had gained weight since the war and looked too portly to pass for a starving POW, and explicitly call the "not heroic enough" line an urban myth. **The "not heroic or athletic enough" version is the one carried by Wikipedia's film article and by a credentialed POW historian; the "too fat" version is Tier 3 only.** Use the sourced version.

**Bonus, also from the National Archives:** in the camp Butterworth "directed and appeared in many shows that helped to lift morale **while also being loud enough to cover the noise of tunnels being dug**." His Wikipedia article, per his own account, describes performing a song with **Talbot Rothwell** (who would later write most of the "Carry On" scripts) followed by comic patter that "provoked enough boos and hisses to have the desired effect of drowning out the sounds of an escape tunnel being dug by other prisoners."

Two future "Carry On" men, in a German prison camp, deliberately bombing on stage so hard the audience booed loudly enough to hide a tunnel.

### How the camp reacted

- The best-sourced reaction is the roll call sabotage and Tommy-gun Saturday (above).
- The 1949 Air Mail POW account describes the prior pattern the escape broke: "the Commandant and a horde of his junior officers would inspect the site and, with great *bonhomie*, congratulate the would-be escapees on their ingenious efforts, commiserating with them that weeks of hard work had been discovered and left unrewarded. We came to take this very much for granted and were amazed at the difference it made when the Huns found we had, for once, pulled a fast one on them."
- **A documented celebration inside the camp: NOT FOUND.** No source I found describes cheering, a party, or a marked celebration.
- IWM Reel 9 notes "lack of publicity about his escape during war," i.e. the story was suppressed at the time for operational reasons.

### The three were the only home runs from the East Compound

"Williams, Codner and Philpot were the only three men to escape from the east compound at Stalag Luft III and regain their freedom (the so-called 'home run')." (Wikipedia, Philpot; corroborated by IWM). For context, IWM states that of 170,000 British and Commonwealth POWs in Germany, "fewer than 1,200 of them managed to escape successfully and make a 'home run'."

### Decorations

All three received the **Military Cross**, unusual for RAF officers, awarded for the escape. Philpot: London Gazette supplement 36516, page 2242, published 12 May 1944 (Wikipedia gives the award date as 16 May 1944; minor discrepancy between gazetting and award dating). Codner: Military Cross gazetted 27 April 1944 (Wikipedia, Michael Codner). Philpot also held the DFC (1941).

## VERIFIED ABSURD BEATS (the comedy list)

Ranked strongest first. Strength = quality of sourcing plus comic payload.

**1. The pratfall defence: they knocked their own tunnel over on purpose, and once jogged a lap and left it unattended.**
"Sometimes the horse would be carried out empty and 'accidentally' knocked over. On one occasion, the prisoners even left the vaulting horse, which covered the tunnel entrance, and ran a circuit around the compound." The single funniest operational security measure of the war: prove the escape tunnel is not an escape tunnel by dropping it.
Sources: RAF Association / Air Mail 1949 (POW eyewitness). Corroborated in substance by search-level summaries of Williams' book.

**2. Peter Butterworth auditioned to play himself and was rejected for not looking heroic enough.**
He actually vaulted over the horse in 1943. In 1949 the producers turned him down.
Sources: The National Archives, "Great Escapes" exhibition (Tier 1); Wikipedia, The Wooden Horse (1950 film); Kristen Alexander. Three independent.

**3. Philpot escaped disguised as a margarine salesman because he really was a margarine executive, and he built the fake identity before he had an escape to use it on.**
He also could not speak a word of Norwegian, and chose Norwegian specifically to minimise the odds of meeting one.
Sources: Telegraph obituary 6 May 1993; Wikipedia (Philpot) citing "Stolen Journey"; IS9 file detail via escape-lines research blog.

**4. He fell asleep on his suitcase on a German train, fell off it, swore in English, and the carriage laughed.**
Then a plainclothes policeman checked his papers and he passed using an ID card carrying another officer's photograph.
Sources: Telegraph obituary; Wikipedia citing "Stolen Journey"; IWM oral history Reel 7 catalogue ("revelation of the inaccuracy of his documents").

**5. He carried the pipe as a device to disguise his accent, and grew a Hitler moustache "for good measure".**
"He chewed on a pipe, as an excuse for slurring his speech, and for good measure he sported a Hitler moustache."
Source: Telegraph obituary. Single-sourced but from a major-paper obituary drawing on his own account. Flag as such.

**6. They came out of the ground twelve inches short, directly in the sentry's path, and were saved because the night patrol was running late.**
Philpot then looked up into the face of a German who happened to be facing the wrong way.
Sources: Telegraph obituary (the 12 inches and the late patrol); The National Archives, Philpot's own recorded voice (the guard looking in).

**7. The tunnel was thirty degrees off course, and they found out by poking a stick through the ground.**
"Experimentation with a poker showed that the tunnel was about 30 inches below ground but also running approximately 30 degrees off the intended course."
Source: Wikipedia (Philpot) citing "Stolen Journey". Single-sourced to the memoir. Flag.

**8. Codner was buried alive by a cave-in that opened a hole in the middle of the exercise yard, and a vaulter deliberately fell over it and lay on it pretending to have twisted his ankle.**
The highest-drama beat in the story. Memoir-derived (Williams), reaching me via Tier 2/3. Attribute as "the way Williams told it."
Sources: Look and Learn; throughouthistory.com; multiple retellings of Williams' text. NOT Tier 1.

**Runners-up worth having in the pocket:**

- **The guards adopted the insult.** Prisoners told them "goon" meant "German Officer Or Non-Com" and the guards accepted the nickname happily. (b24.net; Wikipedia, Stalag Luft III)
- **A German guard discovered the escape by putting his foot in it.** "A guard walking outside the wire stumbled when his foot disappeared into a hole in the ground." (RAF Association / Air Mail 1949)
- **Tommy-gun Saturday.** The prisoners disrupted roll call so persistently that the Germans had to bus in several hundred extra troops from a nearby aerodrome to count them. (RAF Association / Air Mail 1949)
- **Philpot's forged papers included an invitation to a Quisling speech and a Nasjonal Samling membership card, plus a Swedish sailor's pass he himself called "very bogus".** (IS9 file via escape-lines blog, citing The National Archives)
- **He took a harbour pleasure cruise around occupied Danzig to scout for an escape ship, and had a beer in the station buffet first.** (Telegraph obituary; IWM Reel 7)
- **He boarded the Swedish ship, found hot chocolate simmering in the galley, drank it, and then hid in the coal bunker.** (Telegraph obituary; IWM Reel 8 "attempt to hide in coal; hiding in oily tank")
- **The captain threw him off in port and invited him to dinner at sea.** (Wikipedia citing "Stolen Journey"; Telegraph)
- **First night of freedom: a Swedish police cell.** (Telegraph)
- **The dig schedule was redesigned because the gym class was getting tired.** "An important change as the number of volunteers was small and they were becoming tired." (Wikipedia citing "Stolen Journey")
- **The horse's top surface was faced with cigarette packet wrappings.** (Wikipedia citing "Stolen Journey", single-sourced)
- **The compass was a gramophone record, a gramophone needle, a uniform press stud, two halves of a razor blade magnetised off the camp lighting circuit, and phosphorus scraped out of broken watches.** (IWM EPH 6366)
- **Codner was in the Royal Artillery and was in an air force camp by mistake.** IWM describes him as "an officer in the Royal Artillery who was mistaken for an Air Force prisoner and sent to Stalag Luft III." He escaped from the wrong camp. (IWM EPH 6366; Wikipedia, Michael Codner, notes he was routed through Dulag Luft, the aircrew interrogation centre.)
- **Two future "Carry On" men deliberately bombed on stage to make the audience boo loudly enough to cover tunnel noise.** (The National Archives; Wikipedia, Peter Butterworth)
- **IWM's Philpot collection includes "the flying log book (31pp, November 1941 to November 1942) belonging to his dog, Sammy, who had accompanied him on non-operational flights."** His dog had a logbook. (IWM Private Papers of Flight Lieutenant O L S Philpot)
- **IWM Reel 9 lists "civil servant's failed attempt to secure expenses for his stay in Sweden."** Someone in Whitehall tried to sort out the expenses paperwork for the escape and failed. NOTE: the catalogue phrasing is ambiguous about who was claiming from whom. Verify before using.
- **Philpot's listed recreations in later life:** "political activity including canvassing, sculling Boat Race course and return (No 452 in Head of the River Race for Scullers, 1986), talking, idling, listening to sermons, reading Financial Times and obituaries in Lancet." He rowed the Boat Race course into his seventies. (Telegraph obituary)

## Direct quotes (exact wording + source)

**Oliver Philpot, on the moment of breaking out** (recorded, played at The National Archives "Great Escapes" exhibition):
> "We're watching the watch carefully and when we reckoned it was, it was dark then Williams said 'Ah, we're breaking now.' He shouted back to me, 'Follow me.' I got up to the front of the tunnel and, and there I could see the night's sky above, and I looked up and I saw the German looking in. He's looking in to the camp, fortunately. Well there's no time to study him or anything, so I hoicked my bundles up one after another and followed them up, and then streaked across the little road, hauled myself out, streaked across the little road and in to those heavenly pine trees, which gave cover."
Source: The National Archives, Great Escapes exhibition.

**Oliver Philpot, on his home-made compass** ("Stolen Journey", pp. 284 to 285, quoted in full on the IWM object record EPH 6366):
> "I needed a compass. I did not want one of the tiny ones which were difficult to see, although I proposed to take my original stud compass which I had kept ever since taking off from Leuchars. Jerry, the pilot from Coventry, used some of the craft and cunning for which his city is famous and produced the best kriegie compass which I had ever seen. The bowl was a moulded gramophone record and it had a glass top measuring three inches across, and in the bowl stood an inverted gramophone needle. Two pieces of a razor blade had been magnetised by the camp electric light circuit, and had been pasted on to the under-side of a circular cardboard compass card. The card had, in the middle, a press-stud from an officer's uniform into which the gramophone needle fitted neatly, enabling the card to swing freely under the glass. The card markings were large and clear, with phosphorus added from old and broken watch-faces."
> "The great advantage which this instrument possessed was its quick readability. It settled reasonably quickly, and was big enough to give a clear guide. I thought it would be useful generally, especially if I had to take to the woods near Sagan."
Source: IWM object record EPH 6366, quoting Philpot, "Stolen Journey".

**An unnamed Stalag Luft III prisoner, writing in Air Mail magazine, 1949**, on how the Germans usually behaved and how the Wooden Horse changed it:
> "[During previous, failed, escape attempts] the Commandant and a horde of his junior officers would inspect the site and, with great bonhomie, congratulate the would-be escapees on their ingenious efforts, commiserating with them that weeks of hard work had been discovered and left unrewarded."
> "We came to take this very much for granted and were amazed at the difference it made when the Huns found we had, for once, pulled a fast one on them over the wooden-horse episode."
> "When the 'goons' [guards] realised that the whole proceeding had been carried on right under the nose of the guard in the 'goon-box', day after day, week after week, their sense of humour, never very noticeable, evaporated completely."
Source: RAF Association, quoting Air Mail, 1949.

**Oliver Philpot, on his Swedish sailor's pass:** "very bogus".
Source: Philpot's IS9 file, The National Archives, quoted via The Escape Line research blog.

**Oliver Philpot on his own train misadventure**, as rendered in the Telegraph obituary:
> "went to asleep sitting on his suitcase in a third-class gangway, fell off and exclaimed 'Damn!' But his fellow passengers simply laughed."
Source: Daily Telegraph obituary, 6 May 1993. (The obituary is paraphrasing; "Damn!" is presented as his word.)

**Oliver Philpot, in a letter to his wife Nathalie from Oflag XXI-B, 13 December 1942**, on being fished out of the North Sea by the German navy:
> "It was nice getting below in the dry and warm, and the hot soup was good."
Source: VE/VJ Day 80 letters project (UK government), letter contributed by Michael L A Andrews.

**Oliver Philpot's listed recreations, later in life:**
> "political activity including canvassing, sculling Boat Race course and return (No 452 in Head of the River Race for Scullers, 1986), talking, idling, listening to sermons, reading Financial Times and obituaries in Lancet"
Source: Daily Telegraph obituary, 6 May 1993.

**A direct quote from Eric Williams: NOT FOUND** in any freely accessible source. His book is not full-text searchable online (Internet Archive copies are lend-only and search-inside APIs returned nothing). If the script needs Williams in his own voice, someone has to open the book.

**A direct quote from Michael Codner: NOT FOUND.** He never wrote a memoir and was killed in 1952.

## Human-cost beats (play straight, no jokes)

**1. Michael Codner was murdered in 1952.**
After the war Codner returned to combat in Italy, finished his Oxford degree, and joined the Colonial Service as an Assistant District Officer in Malaya during the Malayan Emergency. On 25 March 1952, while repairing a sabotaged water pipeline near Tanjung Malim, Codner and eleven others were ambushed and killed by communist guerrillas. He was thirty-one. (Wikipedia, Michael Codner; IWM's Philpot papers record includes "two scrapbooks (46pp) of newspaper cuttings relating to Codner's murder by Communist guerrillas during the Emergency in Malaya, 1952", meaning Philpot kept a scrapbook of his friend's death.)

**2. The Great Escape and the murder of fifty, from the same camp, five months later.**
On the night of 24 to 25 March 1944, 76 men got out through tunnel "Harry" from the North Compound. 73 were recaptured. **50 were murdered** on Hitler's personal orders. Only three made home runs: Per Bergsland and Jens Müller (Norwegian) and Bram van der Stok (Dutch).

Nationalities of the fifty murdered: twenty British, six Canadian, six Polish, five Australian, three South African, two New Zealanders, two Norwegians, and one each from Argentina, Belgium, Czechoslovakia, France, Greece and Lithuania.

Method: they were driven away in small groups under the pretence of being returned to camp, stopped in the countryside, invited to get out and relieve themselves, and shot at close range from behind.

Postwar: a British war crimes trial opened at the Curio Haus in Hamburg on 1 July 1947 against 18 defendants; verdicts came on 3 September 1947; thirteen were hanged at Hamelin Prison in February 1948 by Albert Pierrepoint. Of 72 identified perpetrators, 21 were eventually executed, 17 imprisoned, 11 killed themselves.

Sources: Wikipedia, Stalag Luft III and Stalag Luft III murders; IWM; The National Archives.

**Flag for the writer:** the Wooden Horse is a comedy. The Great Escape, in the same camp, in the same war, five months later, is not. If the episode touches it at all, it should be a hard tonal stop, and the numbers should be handed to the fact-check agent.

**3. The physical state of the vaulters.**
"The prisoners had to continue exercising over the vaulting horse, despite their poor physical condition caused by a near-starvation diet. And the further the tunnel grew, the longer the vaulters had to go on jumping." (RAF Association). This can be played as heroic, but not as slapstick.

**4. Philpot's crew.** On one Norwegian sortie he flew a badly shot-up Beaufort back to Leuchars "with his crew dead or dying" and belly-landed it. (Telegraph obituary). Straight.

**5. The scale of successful escape.** Of 170,000 British and Commonwealth POWs held in Germany, "fewer than 1,200 of them managed to escape successfully and make a 'home run'." (IWM). The Wooden Horse's three were three of that 1,200.

## Myth vs record (corrections)

**1. The names in the story most people know are fake.**
"Peter Howard", "John Clinton" and "Philip Rowe" are Eric Williams' inventions, used in his 1949 book and carried into the 1950 film. The real men were Eric Williams, Richard Michael Clinton Codner and Oliver Philpot. Williams changed them because he was writing under the Official Secrets Act. This is the single most useful correction the episode can make.
Sources: Wikipedia, The Wooden Horse (film); historyofwar.org review; Kristen Alexander.

**2. "The Wooden Horse" the book is a semi-fictionalised third-person narrative, not a plain memoir.**
Williams first wrote a short factual version, "Goon in the Block", on the ship home in 1945, then rewrote it as a much longer third-person book in 1949. He later cut "a fictional incident that he later regretted have added." Philpot's "Stolen Journey" (1950) is the straighter first-person account.
Sources: Wikipedia, Eric Williams (writer); historyofwar.org.

**3. Not all three men dug it, and not from the start.** Williams and Codner conceived and began it in July 1943. Philpot was brought in later, and only after they had first approached him in his role as the hut's escape coordinator to register the plan.
Source: Wikipedia, Philpot, citing "Stolen Journey".

**4. Who had the idea is genuinely contested.** The Telegraph credits Williams ("whose idea was inspired by the Trojan Horse"). Wikipedia's Michael Codner article credits Codner. Wikipedia's Philpot article says "The Wooden Horse was the idea of Lieutenant Michael Codner RA and Flight Lieutenant Eric Williams." **Safest wording: Williams and Codner together.**

**5. The date. It was 29 October 1943, not 19 October.** seat61 and everything-everywhere both print 19 October. IWM, The National Archives, Wikipedia, the Telegraph obituary and the RAF Association all say 29 October. **29 October is correct.** The 19 October error appears to have propagated from one source.

**6. The escape was NOT a mass breakout.** Three men, one at a time, out of a tunnel roughly 100 feet long. Popular telling sometimes blurs it into the Great Escape. Also note: the Wooden Horse came from the **East** Compound; the Great Escape came from the **North** Compound, which did not exist yet in 1943.

**7. Do not import Great Escape statistics.** Figures like "1,400 Red Cross powdered milk tins converted into shovels" and "4,000 bed boards dismantled" belong to the Great Escape tunnels, and I saw them attached to the Wooden Horse in at least one search result. The Wooden Horse crew dug with bowls and tins and lined the shaft with Red Cross box plywood; there is no comparable inventory for it.

**8. The seismograph-masking explanation is a modern gloss, not a sourced participant claim.** See the section above. The microphones are real; the "vaulting was to mask them" line traces to a single Wikipedia-lineage sentence.

**9. Philpot's death date.** He died **29 April 1993**, aged 80. The Telegraph obituary was **published 6 May 1993**, which is why several sources give 6 May as the death date.

**10. "Stolen Journey" publication year.** UK first edition Hodder and Stoughton, **1950**. Kirkus reviewed a US edition in June 1952. IWM's catalogue label says 1953. Use **1950**.

**11. Rubberneck.** Do not put Karl Griese at the vaulting horse. All sourced accounts of him concern the North Compound and the Great Escape period.

## Gaps (NOT FOUND) - be explicit

Every item here is something the popular telling asserts, or something the brief asked for, that I could **not** verify. Treat all of these as forbidden unless someone opens the primary books.

1. **The weight of the horse**, loaded or empty. No figure anywhere.
2. **Reliable dimensions of the horse.** Two incompatible sets exist, neither Tier 1. Do not put a number on screen.
3. **Any documented near-drop, stumble or fumble by the carrying party** (the "accidentally" knocked-over incidents were deliberate).
4. **Anyone actually injured while vaulting.** The only ankle injury in the record is faked.
5. **The fate of the original wooden horse.** No source. Not in any museum catalogue I searched. The only extant full-size horse is Robert Laplander's modern research replica.
6. **A documented in-camp celebration** after the escape. The sourced reaction is roll-call sabotage, not cheering.
7. **A German who unwittingly helped the escapers.** The nearest sourced thing is a sentry facing the wrong way and a late night patrol, which is luck, not help. Do not transplant the Bridgend car-pushing story.
8. **Any direct quotation from Eric Williams.** Not available in accessible sources.
9. **Any direct quotation from Michael Codner.** He left no memoir.
10. **The ferrets' blue overalls.** Universally described in popular accounts, but I found no Tier 1 confirmation of the uniform colour.
11. **Rubberneck's presence during the Wooden Horse dig.** No source places him in the East Compound in 1943.
12. **A sourced statement that the men vaulted specifically to defeat the seismograph microphones**, from any participant. Only the Wikipedia-lineage assertion.
13. **A briefcase.** The brief asked about one. The sourced item is a **small suitcase containing shaving gear** (Telegraph). "Business papers" in the sense of a portfolio of margarine paperwork: what is documented is a typed introduction letter from the Margarine Verkaufs Union, not a briefcase of samples.
14. **Whether the pipe was lit or ever smoked.** Only its function as a speech-slurring prop is documented.
15. **The number of men who vaulted in total**, or a named list of the vaulters beyond Peter Butterworth.
16. **Exact ventilation method.** Sources conflict: "dared not bore airholes" versus "metal rods to poke through the surface to create air holes."
17. **What the Germans did with or to the horse after the discovery.** Nothing.
18. **Whether the Germans ever formally recorded the horse in their own reports.** Not located. German-side documentation was not accessible to me.
19. **Whether the guards ever probed the horse with the ferrets' metal rods specifically.** Inspection is confirmed in outline by IWM's oral history catalogue; the method is not.
20. **The identity of the vaulter who fell on the cave-in hole.** Unnamed in every version I found.
21. **Whether "Tommy-gun Saturday" was applied to 30 October 1943 specifically.** 29 October 1943 was a Friday, so "Saturday" fits the following day, but no source states the date explicitly. Verify before dating it on screen.
22. **The full IWM oral history audio.** I could only reach the catalogue reel summaries, not the recordings themselves. IWM catalogue 9938, nine reels, 270 minutes, recorded 21 September 1987 by Conrad Wood. **Reels 5 to 8 are the whole escape in Philpot's own voice and would be the highest-value follow-up research in this project.** They are listenable free under IWM's non-commercial licence.
