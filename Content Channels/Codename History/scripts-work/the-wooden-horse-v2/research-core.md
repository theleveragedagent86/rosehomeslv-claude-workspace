# Research Report (Core): The Wooden Horse

Wave 1, lane: core factual verification and Tier 1 source upgrade.
Compiled 23 August 2026. Replaces the Wikipedia-heavy v1 report.

## One-paragraph summary

Stalag Luft III was a Luftwaffe run camp for captured Allied airmen at Sagan in Lower Silesia, now Zagan in Poland, opened in March 1942 and deliberately sited and built to defeat tunnelling. Huts sat well back from the wire, barracks were raised off the ground, the subsoil was bright yellow sand under grey surface soil so spoil showed instantly, and seismograph microphones were buried around the perimeter. Three prisoners beat all of it with a gymnastics vaulting horse. Lieutenant Richard Michael Clinton Codner of the Royal Artillery had the idea, Flight Lieutenant Eric Ernest Williams RAF developed it with him, and in June 1943 they took it to Flight Lieutenant Oliver Lawrence Spurling Philpot RAFVR, the hut escape co-ordinator, to register it with the escape committee. Philpot later joined as the third escaper. Digging started on 8 July 1943 and the tunnel, 95 to 100 feet long and shallow, was finished 114 days later. On the evening of Friday 29 October 1943 the three broke out just after 6 p.m. Williams and Codner went as French workmen through Frankfurt an der Oder and Kustrin to Stettin, were hidden by a Danish sailor, sailed via Copenhagen and were put ashore in Sweden on 11 November, reaching Stockholm on 12 November. Philpot travelled alone as a Norwegian margarine salesman called Jon Jorgensen, reached Danzig inside 24 hours, stowed away on the Swedish ship Aralizz and was at the British Legation in Stockholm on Thursday 4 November. All three made home runs. They were the only men ever to make a home run from the East Compound. Five months later the North Compound produced the Great Escape, and that one ended with 50 men murdered.

## Key figures

**Lieutenant Richard Michael Clinton Codner, MC. Royal Artillery, 457 Light Battery. Service number 200507.**
Born 29 September 1920. Educated Bedford School and Exeter College, Oxford. Commissioned 2 August 1941, served in North Africa, captured at Medjez el Bab, Tunisia, on 14 December 1942. Sent first to Rome, then by German error to Dulag Luft at Frankfurt, the collection and interrogation centre for captured aircrew, and from there into the air force POW system. He was the man who thought of the horse. IWM describes him as "an officer in the Royal Artillery who was mistaken for an Air Force prisoner and sent to Stalag Luft III." **He was a Lieutenant at the time of the escape, not a Captain.** He reached the rank of Captain later. Killed on 25 March 1952 at Tanjung Malim during the Malayan Emergency, aged 31.
Sources: TNA WO 373/94/547; TNA WO 416/71/64; TNA WO 208/3317/1618; London Gazette 36486 p. 1926; IWM EPH 6366 label.

**Flight Lieutenant Eric Ernest Williams, MC. Royal Air Force. Service number 117660.**
Born 13 July 1911, died 24 December 1983. Navigator, not pilot. Lost on operations 17 December 1942 in Stirling BK620 of 75 (NZ) Squadron, which came down at Aalsmeer / Fort Kudelstaart in the Netherlands. Held at Oflag XXI-B Schubin, escaped and was recaptured with Codner, then sent to Stalag Luft III. Co-designer of the horse and one of the two original diggers. Wrote *Goon in the Block* (1945) and then *The Wooden Horse* (Collins, 1949). IWM catalogues his papers as "Private Papers of Flight Lieutenant E Williams MC" (Documents.4077).
Sources: TNA WO 373/94/549; TNA AIR 81/22372; TNA WO 208/3317/1619 and WO 208/5273/1622; IWM Documents.4077; CWGC.

**Flight Lieutenant Oliver Lawrence Spurling Philpot, MC, DFC. Royal Air Force Volunteer Reserve, 42 Squadron, No. 18 Group, Coastal Command.**
Born Vancouver, British Columbia, 6 March 1913, died 29 April 1993. British and Canadian citizenship. Radley, then PPE at Worcester College, Oxford, where he joined the Oxford University Air Squadron. Joined Unilever in 1934 and by 1936 was assistant commercial secretary in the home margarine executive, a job that took him around Germany and taught him German. Pilot, shot down 11 December 1941 off Norway in Beaufort AW243. DFC gazetted 1 July 1941 (London Gazette 35206 p. 3770). Promoted flight lieutenant August 1942. Escape co-ordinator for the hut. Initially only helped dispose of sand, then was asked to join as a digger and third escaper. MC gazetted 16 May 1944. Postwar chief executive of Findus, then managing director of Remploy, and chairman of the RAF Escaping Society. Wrote *Stolen Journey* (Hodder and Stoughton, 1950), illustrated by Ronald Searle.
Sources: London Gazette 36516 supplement p. 2242; TNA AIR 81/10866; TNA WO 416/289/369; TNA WO 208/3317/1617; IWM EPH 6366 label; IWM oral history 9938; IWM Documents.13260.

**Others named in the record**
- McKay: the fourth man carried out in the horse on the last day to seal the three escapers into the tunnel. First name NOT FOUND.
- Jerry Dawkins: RAF Manchester bomber pilot from Coventry who made Philpot's escape compass in two days (IWM EPH 6366).
- Squadron Leader Roger Bushell: organiser of the Great Escape, not the Wooden Horse. Arrested at Saarbrucken station 26 March 1944, murdered 29 March 1944, aged 33.

## Numbers (high-risk, source each)

**Tunnel length: 95 to 100 feet.** The MC recommendation for Codner in TNA WO 373/94/547 states "The tunnel was 95 to 100 feet long, and the exit was in the open about 15 feet outside the wire." CWGC independently gives "a 30m tunnel." 30 m is 98.4 feet, which sits inside the 95 to 100 foot band. Use "about 100 feet, roughly 30 metres." Do not state a single precise figure.

**Exit distance beyond the wire: about 15 feet.** TNA WO 373/94/547. Now Tier 1, upgraded from the single Wikipedia sourcing in v1.

**Tunnel depth: about 30 inches, roughly 76 cm, below the surface.** Determined in the camp by probing with a poker, which also showed the tunnel was running roughly 30 degrees off the intended line. Source: Philpot, *Stolen Journey*, p. 262, cited via Wikipedia. Tier 2 for now, single-sourced to the memoir. Flag as "per Philpot's own account."

**Digging duration: 114 days, 8 July to 29 October 1943 inclusive.** See "Conflicts resolved" below. This is 3 months and 3 weeks.

**The horse: 4 feet 6 inches high, base 5 feet by 3 feet.** Sides covered with 2 foot square plywood sheets from Red Cross packing cases stolen from the German store. Top of solid wood boards, padded with bedding and covered with white linen taken from the bales the cigarettes arrived in. Legs from four pieces of timber about 3 inches square and 5 feet long. Four slots, 4 inches by 3 inches, cut in the plywood sides took carrying poles of rafter 6 feet long and 3 inches by 2 inches. Source: Eric Williams, *The Wooden Horse*, chapter 3. Participant account, so Tier 1 quality for the physical object but single-sourced.

**Carried by four men, sedan chair fashion.** Williams, *The Wooden Horse*, ch. 3, corroborated independently by the Royal Air Forces Association, which says the horse was "carried out of the recreation hut by four fellow prisoners" with "one or two escapers" inside. Two independent sources agree.

**Red Cross crate plywood: CONFIRMED.** Williams describes plywood sheets from Red Cross packing cases for the sides and, per Philpot, plywood panels from Red Cross parcel boxes lining the entry shaft. Corroborated by RAFA and by the Stalag Luft III general literature. Note the nuance: the frame was stolen timber, the skin was Red Cross crate plywood. "Made entirely from Red Cross crates" overstates it.

**Sand lifting rates.** Initially 12 bags per session. Later a four-cycle system: two men out on the first trip digging 36 bags, then one man on each of the next three trips recovering 12 bags each. That gave 36 bags per cycle against 48 under the old system but meant three shorter vaulting sessions and one long one, which mattered because the pool of volunteer vaulters was small and tiring. Source: Philpot, *Stolen Journey*, p. 255. Single-sourced, memoir.

**Total sand removed: NOT FOUND** as a verified weight or volume.

**Camp scale.** IWM: at its peak Stalag Luft III held almost 11,000 Commonwealth and American men across several compounds on a 60 acre site, roughly 2,500 RAF officers, 7,500 USAAF and about 900 from other Allied air forces. Located about 100 miles, 160 km, south east of Berlin.

**Barracks raised off the ground.** Commonly given as about 60 cm, 24 inches. CWGC confirms the fact ("each hut was raised off the ground, making tunnelling attempts easily visible") without the figure. The 60 cm figure is Tier 2 only. Use "raised off the ground" and only give the number as approximate.

**Home run context.** IWM: "Of the 170,000 British and Commonwealth prisoners of war in Germany in the Second World War, fewer than 1,200 of them managed to escape successfully and make a 'home run'." Tier 1, and a very strong stat for the script.

**Williams, Codner and Philpot were the only men to make a home run from the East Compound.** IWM states it. The MC recommendation adds the contemporary framing: no one had got home direct from Sagan, or even made a break from the East Compound, for over a year before them.

### Great Escape contrast numbers, night of 24 to 25 March 1944, North Compound

- **Date: night of 24 to 25 March 1944.** The National Archives quotes the escape committee's own report: "The mass escape through the tunnel 'HARRY' took place from the night of the 24th to the early morning of the 25th March, 1944." CWGC calls it "the moonless night of 24 March 1944." TNA's MI9 escape reports for Bergsland and Muller both record "Escaped: Stalag Luft III, 24/25 March 1944." Use "the night of 24 to 25 March 1944." Do not say simply "25 March."
- **76 got out.** CWGC: "Seventy-six men escaped, but the seventy-seventh man was spotted and the alarm was raised." The National Archives: "76 POWs made it through tunnel 'Harry'." Two Tier 1 sources agree.
  - Variant worth knowing: IWM's public history page frames it as "80 men climbed out of the 102 metre-long tunnel. Four were caught at the tunnel's mouth when the escape was spotted, 76 got away." Same end number, different framing of the moment of discovery. Do not present 80 and 76 as contradictory. 76 is the number that got clear.
- **Tunnel Harry.** CWGC: 2 feet wide, nearly 30 feet deep, extending more than 330 feet out of the camp. IWM: 102 metres long. 102 m is 335 feet. These agree.
- **73 recaptured.** Not stated as a raw number by either Tier 1 source, but both state 76 out and three home runs, so 73 follows arithmetically. CWGC: "All but three of the escapees were recaptured over the following weeks." Present it as "all but three, so 73, were recaptured."
- **3 home runs, and their names are now Tier 1 confirmed:**
  - Sergeant **Per Bergsland**, 332 Squadron, Norwegian Air Force serving with the RAF, service number 373. Captured in the sea east of Dieppe, 19 August 1942. "Escaped: Stalag Luft III, 24/25 March 1944. Arrived in UK: 7 April 1944." (TNA WO 208/3319/1866.) He was carried on camp records under the false identity "Peter Rockland" (TNA WO 416/311/342), a precaution Norwegians used against reprisals.
  - Second Lieutenant **Jens Einar Muller**, 331 Squadron, Norwegian Air Force serving with the RAF, service number 1007. Captured at Vlissingen, 19 June 1942. "Escaped: Stalag Luft III, 24/25 March 1944. Arrived in UK: 7 April 1944." (TNA WO 208/3319/1867.) The two Norwegians travelled together by train to Stettin and then by ship to Sweden.
  - Flight Lieutenant **Bram van der Stok**, RAF, Dutch, born 30 October 1915. MI9 report reference M.I.9/S/P.G./2032, described as "Escape from Stalag Luft III, Sagan, Germany via Holland, Belgium and France to Spain." (TNA WO 208/5273/5378 and WO 208/3664/53/1.) His run took roughly four months overland.
- **50 murdered on Hitler's orders.** The National Archives: "Soon after the escape, 50 escapees, including Bushell, were murdered by the Gestapo. This was a clear violation of the Geneva Convention." CWGC: Hitler "ordered that they should all be shot, along with the camp commandant of Stalag Luft III, the camp architect, and all the guards on duty at the time of the escape," and "ultimately fifty Great Escapers were executed to deter others from future escape attempts." Two Tier 1 sources.
- **48 of the 50 are buried at Poznan Old Garrison Cemetery, Poland.** Their bodies were cremated and the ashes interred after the war. CWGC, Tier 1.

## Conflicts resolved (or precisely characterized)

**1. Digging duration. RESOLVED: 114 days, not "three months" and not "four and a half months."**
- The Royal Air Forces Association blog says tunnelling lasted "four-and-a-half months." That is wrong. Four and a half months back from 29 October puts the start in mid June, before the scheme was even registered with the escape committee.
- Wikipedia says "three months." That rounds down.
- The Tier 1 record settles it. Codner's Military Cross recommendation, TNA WO 373/94/547, states "On 8 Jul 43 the three men began digging a tunnel" and "On 29 Oct 43 the tunnel was complete and the three men escaped." 8 July to 29 October 1943 inclusive is exactly 114 days.
- IWM's label for Philpot's escape compass, EPH 6366, independently states the three "made their escape after 114 days of digging on 29 October 1943."
- Philpot's own account, via his Daily Telegraph obituary, gives the same: the escape date "was 114 days after the first digging commenced."
- **Script line: 114 days. Three months and three weeks.** Do not say "three months" and do not say "four and a half months."

**2. Escape date and year. RESOLVED: Friday 29 October 1943.**
29 October 1943 was in fact a Friday, verified by calendar. The date is confirmed independently by the Codner MC recommendation (TNA WO 373/94/547), IWM (twice, on the compass label and on its public history page), CWGC, and all three men's MI9 escape report catalogue entries at TNA (WO 208/3317/1617, 1618, 1619), which all read "the 'Wooden Horse' escape from Stalag Luft III on 29 October 1943." The "October 29, 1942" seen in the Coconut Times reprint and its derivatives is a typo. **1943.**

**3. Codner's rank. CORRECTED. He was a Lieutenant, not a Captain.**
The v1 report called him "Captain Michael Codner." The London Gazette of 27 April 1944 lists him as "Lieutenant Richard Michael Clinton Codner (200507), Royal Regiment of Artillery." TNA WO 373/94/547 and WO 208/3317/1618 both record Lieutenant. His German POW record card records Lieutenant. CWGC's own write up says "Lieutenant Michael Codner and Flight Lieutenants Eric Williams and Oliver Philpot." He became Captain later. **In October 1943 he was a Lieutenant.**

**4. Whose idea was the horse. Codner's, developed with Williams. Approved June 1943.**
Wikipedia's Philpot article states it plainly: "The Wooden Horse was the idea of Lieutenant Michael Codner RA and Flight Lieutenant Eric Williams." Williams' own book stages the moment of inspiration with the Codner character, John Clinton, running across the camp with the phrase "the god in the box, the Trojan horse" in his head, then going to find the Williams character. The MC recommendation records the sequence in official language: "Lt. CODNER with F/Lt. Williams started such a scheme with F/Lt. Philpot as third participant." The June 1943 approach to Philpot to register the scheme holds up and is sourced to Philpot's own memoir. **Confirmed.**

**5. Philpot's role. He was not an original digger.**
He was the hut's escape co-ordinator, the man they had to register the scheme with. He first helped dispose of sand, and only later was asked by Codner and Williams to join in the digging and become the third escaper (*Stolen Journey* p. 240). The MC recommendation compresses this into "the three men began digging" on 8 July, which is the official simplification. Handle carefully: **Philpot approved it before he joined it.**

**6. Eric Williams' aircraft and squadron. Two live discrepancies, neither load bearing.**
- TNA AIR 81/22372 records "Pilot Officer Eric E Williams: prisoners of war; Stirling BK620, 75 (NZ) Squadron, aircraft failed to return from an operational flight, Aalsmeer, Fort Kudelstaart, Netherlands, 17 December 1942." TNA WO 373/94/549, the award recommendation, gives his unit as "7 Squadron Bomber Command Royal Air Force." Both squadrons flew Stirlings. The loss record is the more specific document. Prefer 75 (NZ) Squadron for the aircraft loss and simply say "a Stirling bomber of RAF Bomber Command" if you want to avoid the issue.
- RAFA calls Williams a "pilot." He was a navigator, per the jacket copy of his own book and per Wikipedia. Do not call him a pilot.
- The 1979 Penguin jacket copy of *The Wooden Horse* says Williams was shot down "On November 28th, 1942." The TNA loss record says 17 December 1942. **Prefer the TNA date.**

**7. Codner's capture date. 14 or 18 December 1942.**
The British MC recommendation says "captured at Medjez-El-Bab on 14 Dec. 42." The German POW record card (TNA WO 416/71/64) records "Date of Capture: 18 December 1942, Theatre of Capture: Tunis." The German date is most likely the registration date. Prefer 14 December 1942, note the German card if precision matters.

**8. Codner's birthplace. Malaya, not Sweden.**
The German POW card reads "Place of Birth: Selanger, Sweden." That is a German clerical garble of Selangor, Malaya. Wikipedia and his biography give Malaya. Do not repeat "Sweden."

**9. Philpot's service number. 77131 or 88131.**
The OCR of the London Gazette MC entry reads "(88131)." TNA's German POW record card and Wikipedia both give 77131. Almost certainly a 7 read as an 8 in a 1944 print scan. Not load bearing. If you must print one, print 77131.

**10. Great Escape framing: 76 versus 80.**
See the Great Escape section above. 76 got clear. IWM's "80 climbed out, four caught at the mouth" is a different way of counting the same night. Use 76.

**11. Silesia. Lower, not Upper.**
IWM's American Archive page says Sagan was in "Upper Silesia." Sagan / Zagan is in **Lower** Silesia. Every other source, including IWM's own object records ("Zagan, Lower Silesia, Poland"), gets this right.

## Human-cost beats (play straight)

Flag every item in this section as no jokes.

1. **The Great Escape murders.** From the same camp, five months later. 76 men got clear of tunnel Harry on the night of 24 to 25 March 1944. All but three were recaptured over the following weeks. Hitler ordered that the recaptured men be shot, and 50 of them were murdered by the Gestapo, in ones and twos, at the roadside, and their bodies cremated. This was a war crime under the 1929 Geneva Convention. Roger Bushell, who organised the escape, was arrested at Saarbrucken station on 26 March 1944 and murdered on 29 March 1944 at the age of 33. Forty-eight of the fifty lie today in Poznan Old Garrison Cemetery in Poland. Sources: The National Archives, CWGC.

2. **Hitler's order was wider than the 50.** CWGC records that Hitler ordered the shooting not only of the escapers but also of the camp commandant of Stalag Luft III, the camp architect, and every guard on duty on the night of the escape. Senior Nazi officials argued him down to the 50. The number 50 was a compromise.

3. **Escape was not a game.** At Stalag Luft III a single strand of trip wire ran about fifteen feet inside the main fence, twelve inches off the ground, and anyone who stepped over it was shot at by the guards. Guard towers held two men each with searchlights and telephones, and guards with sub machine guns patrolled between them. Source: Williams, *The Wooden Horse*, ch. 2, participant description.

4. **Codner's death.** Michael Codner survived the war, went back to the Royal Artillery in Italy, finished his Oxford degree, married in 1948, joined the Colonial Service and went to Malaya as an Assistant District Officer. On 25 March 1952, during the Malayan Emergency, he and eleven other men were ambushed while repairing a sabotaged water pipeline at Tanjung Malim and killed. He was 31. Note also, and handle with care, that the British response under General Gerald Templer was collective punishment of the nearby civilian population, including 22 hour curfews and enforced food rationing. Do not use Codner's death as a punchline.

5. **The odds.** Fewer than 1,200 of 170,000 British and Commonwealth prisoners in Germany made a home run. That is well under one in a hundred. IWM, Tier 1.

## Myth vs record (corrections)

1. **The Wooden Horse is not the Great Escape and did not end in the 50 murders.** Different compounds, different tunnels, five months apart. The Wooden Horse was the East Compound, October 1943, three men, about 100 feet of tunnel, three home runs and no deaths. The Great Escape was the North Compound, March 1944, 76 men, a 102 metre tunnel, three home runs and 50 murders. Sources: IWM, CWGC, The National Archives, TNA MI9 report catalogue.

2. **Codner was a Lieutenant, not a Captain.** See conflicts, item 3.

3. **Codner was not an airman.** He was a Royal Artillery officer who ended up in a Luftwaffe air force camp because the Germans mis-sorted him. The MC recommendation records that after capture in Tunisia he was sent to Rome and then "by mistake" to Dulag Luft, the interrogation centre for captured aircrew, and thereafter stayed in the air force POW system. IWM says the same. The man who cracked the Luftwaffe's escape proof airmen's camp was not supposed to be in it.

4. **It was not "four and a half months" of digging.** It was 114 days. The Royal Air Forces Association's own article has this wrong. See conflicts, item 1.

5. **The date was 1943, not 1942.** One widely reprinted article gives October 29, 1942. Wrong by a year.

6. **The horse was not made entirely of Red Cross crates.** The frame was timber obtained inside the camp. The skin was plywood from Red Cross packing cases. Both parts of the story are true, the shorthand is not.

7. **Eric Williams was a navigator, not a pilot,** and *The Wooden Horse* is a novelisation, not a documentary. Williams says so himself in his author's note: he wrote under wartime censorship and changed the names. Peter Howard is Williams, John Clinton is Codner, Philip Rowe is Philpot. Anchor every hard fact to the MI9 records, the award recommendations and the museum records, not to the book's character names or its dramatised episodes. Williams himself revised the 1979 edition specifically to remove a fictional episode in which a German dockside guard is killed. That episode never happened.

8. **The 1950 film is a further layer of fiction on top of the novelisation.** Treat film details as inadmissible.

9. **The vaulting horse trick was not unique to Stalag Luft III.** Peter Tunstall's memoir *The Last Escaper* (Duckworth, 2014) claims he and Dominic Bruce used a wooden horse to camouflage a tunnel opening at Spangenberg two years earlier, in 1941. Single-sourced claim by a rival participant. Do not present the Wooden Horse as the first ever use of the trick without this caveat. It was the first to succeed.

## Absurd or ironic beats that are documented

Every item here is sourced above. All are real.

1. The Germans built a camp specifically to make tunnelling impossible, then let the prisoners build a large wooden box and set it down in the same spot near the wire every single day for four months.
2. The prisoners' cover story was exercise, and the exercise was the weapon. The thumping of the vaulters overhead blanketed the very seismograph microphones the Germans had buried to catch diggers. The counter-measure was defeated by noise, not silence.
3. The horse went out with a man, his tools and, by the end, up to fifteen bags of sand inside it, carried by four men who had to walk casually while staggering under the weight.
4. It is an explicit, self-aware Trojan Horse. Roughly three thousand years after the original, hiding men inside a wooden horse still worked.
5. Codner, the man who thought of it, was an artilleryman who was only in the air force camp because the Germans filed him wrong.
6. Philpot, who approved the scheme in his capacity as hut escape co-ordinator, then joined the scheme he had approved.
7. Philpot's cover identity was a Norwegian margarine salesman, because he had genuinely been a margarine executive at Unilever before the war and that job had taught him German. He spoke no Norwegian at all. His Norwegian character was, on the advice of a Norwegian prisoner, fleshed out as a Quisling collaborator, on the theory that no German would want to talk to him.
8. Philpot nearly blew it by falling off his own suitcase and swearing in English, and his papers still passed a police check.
9. He then boarded the Swedish ship Aralizz, was found by the crew, was asked to leave by the captain, and refused. The chief engineer and a seaman hid him from their own captain until the ship sailed.
10. One of the vaulters missed his footing, crashed into the horse and knocked it onto its side in full view of the guards. It happened to be empty that time. They righted it and carried on. Williams, *The Wooden Horse*.
11. The tunnel ran barely 30 inches under the surface and about 30 degrees off its intended course, and they found this out by poking a stick up through the ground.
12. The reason the escape had to happen in October at all: the escape committee's stolen local railway timetables only ran to the end of the month. A deadline set by a train schedule.
13. The break out at 6 p.m. was covered by fellow prisoners staging a deliberate racket in the huts nearest the wire, blowing trumpets, singing and banging the walls.
14. Philpot was home in under five days by train, boat and a bed in a Danzig hotel. Williams and Codner spent a fortnight on it, including a day and a night hidden in a ship's chain locker.

## Gaps (NOT FOUND)

- Total weight or volume of sand removed from the tunnel. NOT FOUND in any reliable source.
- The exact number of prisoners in the vaulting squad on a given day. Sources say "a squad," and the pool was small enough to tire, but no headcount. NOT FOUND.
- First name and unit of "McKay," the fourth man carried out on the last day to seal the escapers in. NOT FOUND.
- Direct confirmation from a Tier 1 source that Eric Williams' award was specifically the Military Cross. TNA WO 373/94/549 confirms the recommendation and the 27 April 1944 gazette date but its catalogue entry does not carry an "Award" field, unlike Codner's. It is corroborated by IWM's own catalogue title, "Private Papers of Flight Lieutenant E Williams MC" (Documents.4077), by his consistent use of the postnominal, and by his membership of the same omnibus list as Codner's MC. Treat as confirmed, but this is the one decoration claim without a clean Tier 1 award line.
- The RAFA article's story of a guard's foot going into the tunnel hole the next day, and the phrase "Tommy-gun Saturday" for the ensuing lockdown, is single-sourced to RAFA. The chronology works, 29 October 1943 was a Friday and the discovery would have fallen on Saturday 30 October. NOT INDEPENDENTLY CONFIRMED. Use only if you attribute it to RAFA or drop it.
- The specific 60 cm figure for the height of the raised barracks. The fact is Tier 1, the number is Tier 2 only.
- Full text of the three MI9 escape reports (TNA WO 208/3317/1617, 1618, 1619). Catalogue level only online. These are the primary accounts, written within weeks of the escape, and would settle every remaining detail. They require a visit to Kew or a paid copy order.
- Philpot's own MC recommendation document reference. WO 373/94/548 returns nothing and his award came through the Air Ministry, so it is likely in an AIR series. NOT FOUND.
