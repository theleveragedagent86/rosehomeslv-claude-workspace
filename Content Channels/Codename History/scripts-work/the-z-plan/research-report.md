# Research Report: The Z Plan (Japan's Combined Fleet Secret Operation Order captured by Filipino guerrillas, March-April 1944)

## One-paragraph summary

In late March 1944 Admiral Mineichi Koga, commander-in-chief of Japan's Combined Fleet and Yamamoto's successor, was moving his headquarters from Palau to Davao in the Philippines. Two flying boats flew the staff. Koga's plane vanished in a storm on March 31, 1944, killing him. The second plane, carrying his chief of staff Vice Admiral Shigeru Fukudome and a copy of the "Z Plan" (the Combined Fleet Secret Operation Order for the defense of Japan's inner Pacific), ran out of fuel and ditched in the Bohol Strait off Cebu around 2:30 a.m. on April 1, 1944. Fukudome survived and was captured by Filipino guerrillas. The red leather portfolio holding the Z Plan documents floated free and was found on the beach by Filipino civilians, who turned it over to guerrillas under Lt. Col. James M. Cushing, an American mining engineer leading the Cebu resistance. To stop Japanese reprisals that were killing Cebuano civilians, Cushing negotiated the return of Fukudome and the surviving airmen on April 10, 1944, but kept the documents. The papers, rolled inside two empty mortar shells, were smuggled to Negros, picked up by the submarine USS Crevalle on the night of May 11, 1944, and carried to Australia, where the Allied Translator and Interpreter Section (ATIS) translated them. The intelligence was distributed to Nimitz and reached Fifth Fleet commander Admiral Raymond Spruance before the Battle of the Philippine Sea (June 19-20, 1944). The National Archives calls it one of the greatest single intelligence feats of the war in the Southwest Pacific, while stressing its exact effect on the battle is impossible to measure.

## Verified timeline

- March 8, 1944 - The Z Plan is issued as Combined Fleet Secret Operation Order (identified by ATIS as "Secret Combined Fleet Order No. 73"). [National Archives, "The Z Plan Story"; JAVA (Nisei translators account)]
- March 31, 1944 - Admiral Mineichi Koga's Kawanishi H8K ("Emily") flying boat is lost in a tropical storm en route Palau to Davao. No survivors; Koga killed. [National Archives; Wikipedia "Mineichi Koga"; Pacific Wrecks]
- April 1, 1944 (~2:30 a.m.) - Fukudome's flying boat, low on fuel and fighting headwinds, ditches in the Bohol Strait near Cebu. Fukudome survives and is captured by Filipino guerrillas. [National Archives; Wikipedia "Mineichi Koga"; National Museum of the Pacific War]
- April 3, 1944 - Filipino civilians (named in NARA as Pedro Gantuangoko and a companion) find the red leather portfolio of Z Plan documents floating offshore / on the beach; they dry and bury the box. [National Archives]
- April 9-10, 1944 - The documents reach Lt. Col. James M. Cushing's guerrillas. [National Archives]
- ~April 10, 1944 - Cushing returns Fukudome and the surviving airmen to Japanese lines near Cebu City to stop reprisals against civilians. Keeps the documents. [National Archives; Wikipedia "James M. Cushing"]
- April 13, 1944 - Cushing signals SWPA (MacArthur's headquarters) about the captured documents; papers rolled and hidden inside two empty mortar shells. [National Archives]
- May 11, 1944 (night) - Submarine USS Crevalle (Lt. Cdr. Francis D. Walker Jr.) picks up the documents and ~40-41 evacuees off Negros. [National Archives; National Museum of the Pacific War]
- ~May 19-20, 1944 - Crevalle delivers documents to Australia (Darwin, then Brisbane). [National Archives; National Museum of the Pacific War]
- May 21, 1944 - ATIS (under Col. Sidney F. Mashbir) begins translating the documents. [National Archives]
- May 23, 1944 - ATIS issues the translation as "Limited Distribution Translation No. 4"; copies to Marshall and MacArthur. [National Archives]
- By June 8, 1944 - Retranslated intelligence distributed via JICPOA (Capt. Edwin T. Layton) to Nimitz and Fifth Fleet; reaches Spruance before the Marianas operation. [National Archives]
- June 19-20, 1944 - Battle of the Philippine Sea ("Great Marianas Turkey Shoot"). US victory. [Wikipedia "Battle of the Philippine Sea"; Britannica (per search); Warfare History Network]

## Key figures

- Admiral Mineichi Koga, Commander-in-Chief, Combined Fleet (successor to Yamamoto, appointed 21 May 1943). Killed March 31, 1944. Promoted Marshal Admiral posthumously; death not announced until May 1944; replaced by Adm. Soemu Toyoda. [Wikipedia "Mineichi Koga"; Pacific Wrecks]
- Vice Admiral Shigeru Fukudome, Chief of Staff, Combined Fleet. Carried the Z Plan copy; survived the ditching; captured; returned April 10. (Some secondary sources call him Rear Admiral; NARA uses Vice Admiral.) [National Archives; Wikipedia]
- Lt. Col. James M. Cushing, US Army, American mining engineer, commander of the Cebu Area Command guerrillas. Received the documents; negotiated Fukudome's return to protect civilians; kept and evacuated the documents. [National Archives; Wikipedia "James M. Cushing"]
- Lt. Cdr. Francis D. Walker Jr., commander, USS Crevalle. Ran the submarine evacuation. [National Archives; National Museum of the Pacific War]
- Col. Sidney F. Mashbir, head of ATIS, Australia. [National Archives]
- Capt. Edwin T. Layton, intelligence officer at JICPOA/Pearl Harbor who got the intelligence to Nimitz's fleet. [National Archives]
- Admiral Raymond A. Spruance, US Fifth Fleet commander at the Battle of the Philippine Sea; his cautious positioning is linked to the captured plans. [Wikipedia; Britannica]
- Lt. Col. Seiiti Ohnishi, Japanese commander at Cebu City whose troops burned villages and killed civilians during the search. [National Archives; Wikipedia]

## Numbers (high-risk, source each)

- Koga's plane: no survivors. [National Archives; Wikipedia "Mineichi Koga"]
- USS Crevalle evacuees: ~40 (NARA) / 41 (National Museum of the Pacific War); planned for 25. [National Archives; National Museum of the Pacific War]
- Z Plan issued as order dated March 8, 1944. [National Archives]
- Battle of the Philippine Sea, June 19-20, 1944: Japanese lost 3 carriers (Taiho, Shokaku, Hiyo) and roughly 550-645 aircraft; US lost about 123 aircraft (many on June 20 ran out of fuel returning at night). "Over 200" Japanese planes downed on the morning of June 19 alone. [Wikipedia "Battle of the Philippine Sea"; Warfare History Network]
- Cushing's force strength during the standoff: about 25 men facing a much larger Japanese force (search-cited as ~500). [search summary of Wikipedia/Z Plan]

## Absurd / comedic beats (all real)

- Japan lost its top naval strategy document not to a codebreaker or a spy, but to fishermen who found a red leather briefcase bobbing in the surf. [National Archives]
- Two consecutive commanders-in-chief of the Combined Fleet were killed in aircraft in about eleven months: Yamamoto (April 1943, shot down) and Koga (March 1944, lost in a storm). [Wikipedia "Mineichi Koga"]
- The single most secret plan in the Japanese Navy was protected on its journey to the Allies by being stuffed inside two empty mortar shells. [National Archives]
- Japan hid Koga's death for weeks and only announced it in May 1944, by which point his master plan had already been read in Australia. [Wikipedia "Mineichi Koga"; National Archives]
- By the time the plan reached the fleet, the Japanese had rolled it forward into "Operation A-Go," which was "essentially unchanged" from the captured Z Plan, so the Allies were reading a plan the enemy barely bothered to update. [Wikipedia "Battle of the Philippine Sea"]

## Human-cost beats (play straight)

- Japanese forces on Cebu, searching for the crash survivors and the documents, burned villages and killed hundreds of Filipino civilians. [National Archives; Wikipedia "James M. Cushing"]
- Cushing's decision to return Vice Admiral Fukudome and the surviving airmen (while keeping the documents) was made specifically to stop those reprisals and protect Cebuano civilians. This was a grave, wrenching decision, not a clever trick. The Japanese kept the promise to stop the killing. Play completely straight. [National Archives; Wikipedia "James M. Cushing"]
- The Battle of the Philippine Sea and the wider Pacific war it fed into cost enormous numbers of lives on both sides; the intelligence coup is not a "win" to celebrate glibly over the dead. [general record]

## Myth vs record (corrections)

- Popular telling: "The Z Plan won the Battle of the Philippine Sea." / Actual: The National Archives account is careful. It calls the exploitation of the Z Plan one of the greatest single intelligence feats in the Southwest Pacific, but says its actual effect on the battle is "impossible to answer." Spruance's caution had multiple causes; the plans reinforced, they did not single-handedly decide. Do not overstate. [National Archives]
- Popular telling: "Fukudome was carrying the briefcase and it was taken off him." / Actual (NARA): the portfolio floated free from the sinking plane and was found separately by civilians on/near the beach; Fukudome himself was captured alive. Some secondary sources compress this into "taken from Fukudome." Prefer the NARA version. [National Archives]
- Detail check "hidden in mortar shells": DOCUMENTED, not embellished. NARA states the papers were placed inside two empty mortar shells to protect them from the elements. [National Archives]
- The captured plan is the "Z Plan" / Combined Fleet Secret Operation Order; by June the Japanese were executing the follow-on "Operation A-Go," described as essentially unchanged from the Z Plan. Both names are correct in their place. [National Archives; Wikipedia "Battle of the Philippine Sea"]
- Fukudome rank: authoritative NARA says Vice Admiral; some sources say Rear Admiral. Use Vice Admiral. [National Archives]
