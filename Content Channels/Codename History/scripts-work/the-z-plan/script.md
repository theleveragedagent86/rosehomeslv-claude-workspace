# EPISODE SCRIPT: THE PLAN JAPAN LOST IN THE OCEAN

**Target runtime:** 21-23 minutes (~3,300 narration words at ~150 wpm)
**Style:** OverSimplified format. Comedic animated history. Flat colors, simple characters, exaggerated reactions, fake country dialogue. Comedy on the absurdity, human cost played completely straight.
**Story type:** Deep-dive on a real, documented intelligence coup. The Z Plan, March to June 1944. (Not an Allied codenamed operation, so standard title format.)
**Title (working):** `The Plan Japan Lost in the Ocean | Codename History`

> ACCURACY NOTE FOR PRODUCTION: Every beat here is sourced (see fact-check log and SOURCES). The one claim to NOT overstate is impact. The National Archives is explicit that the Z Plan's actual effect on the Battle of the Philippine Sea is impossible to measure. The script says the intelligence reached the fleet and reinforced US caution. It does not say the plan "won the battle." Do not re-simplify that back into a myth because the myth is punchier.
>
> VOICE NOTE: Comedy lives in the NARRATION. Deadpan. The narrator explains a strategic disaster like it is the world's dumbest lost-and-found story. Countries and admirals get fake dialogue. Real, documented bad luck and bad decisions get roasted. Never invent a flaw for a joke. The Cebu reprisals and Cushing's decision to return Fukudome are played completely straight, no jokes, quiet and factual. Those beats are marked TONE: STRAIGHT.

## COLD OPEN (0:00 - 0:45)

**[VISUAL: A red leather briefcase bobbing gently in ocean waves at dawn, off a tropical coastline. Calm. Almost peaceful.]**

**NARRATOR:** In April 1944, two Filipino fishermen found a briefcase floating in the sea.

**[VISUAL: The fishermen paddle over, fish it out of the water, turn it over in their hands. A single letter is stamped on the cover: Z.]**

**NARRATOR:** Inside was the entire defensive war plan of the Imperial Japanese Navy. Its whole strategy for the next phase of the Pacific war. Just, in the ocean. In a bag.

**[VISUAL: Cut to a Japanese admiral in a distant city, sipping tea, blissfully unaware.]**

**NARRATOR:** Nobody in Tokyo knew it was gone. And by the time anyone figured out what had happened, the Americans in Australia had already read the whole thing.

**[VISUAL: The briefcase, dripping, sitting on a beach. Zoom on the Z.]**

**NARRATOR:** This is the story of the single most secret document in the Japanese Navy, and how it ended up soaking wet in the hands of the enemy. Every part of it is real.

**[TITLE CARD: "THE PLAN JAPAN LOST IN THE OCEAN"]**

## ACT 1: A VERY BAD YEAR TO BE IN CHARGE (0:45 - 3:30)

**[VISUAL: A calendar flipping to 1944. A map of the Pacific with a shrinking red Japanese zone and a growing wave of American arrows.]**

**NARRATOR:** Setup. By early 1944, the war in the Pacific was not going well for Japan. At all.

**[VISUAL: Little American aircraft carriers hopping island to island, planting flags, getting closer to Japan with each hop.]**

**NARRATOR:** The Americans had figured out a strategy called island hopping. Skip the strongholds, grab the islands that matter, keep moving toward Japan. And it was working. The map kept turning less red every month. Japan needed to stop the bleeding, and it needed to do it soon, because every island the Americans took was one step closer to the home islands themselves.

**[VISUAL: A grand Japanese naval headquarters. A portrait on the wall of Admiral Yamamoto, draped in black.]**

**NARRATOR:** Now, running the entire Japanese fleet was a job with a bit of a curse on it. The famous Admiral Yamamoto, the man who planned Pearl Harbor, had been the commander in chief. Then, in April 1943, American fighters ambushed and shot down his plane. He was dead.

**[VISUAL: A new admiral, Mineichi Koga, stepping up to the desk, straightening his uniform, looking determined.]**

**NARRATOR:** His replacement was this man. Admiral Mineichi Koga. Commander in chief of the Combined Fleet. The most powerful naval officer Japan had left. And Koga knew the war was slipping away, so he did what commanders do. He wrote a plan.

**[VISUAL: Koga at a huge table covered in maps, drawing arrows, stamping documents. A folder is labeled with a big Z.]**

**NARRATOR:** He called it the Z Plan. On paper it was the Combined Fleet Secret Operation Order. The idea was simple to say and very hard to do. When the Americans came for the inner ring of islands, Japan would throw everything it had left, the carriers, the planes, the battleships, into one enormous decisive battle and smash the US fleet in a single blow.

**[VISUAL: A cartoon diagram of the plan. Japanese carriers luring the American fleet one way while battleships sneak around the side.]**

**NARRATOR:** There was even a clever bit. Lure the American carriers off with a decoy, then have your battleships swing around the side and hit the landing forces where they were weak. On paper it looked sharp. In reality Japan had fewer good pilots, fewer good planes, and less fuel than the plan assumed. But it was Japan's last big idea. And it was so secret that only a handful of officers were even allowed to read it.

**[VISUAL: Koga locking the Z Plan folder in a case, looking satisfied.]**

**NARRATOR:** So Koga had his master plan. Now he just had to move his headquarters to somewhere safer to run it from. The plan was to fly the whole staff from Palau, in the middle of the Pacific, over to Davao, in the Philippines.

**[VISUAL: Two enormous flying boats sitting on the water at Palau, engines idling, staff climbing aboard.]**

**NARRATOR:** Two giant flying boats. One for Koga. One for his chief of staff. And the most secret document in the Japanese Navy, tucked in a briefcase, going along for the ride.

**[VISUAL: Storm clouds gathering on the horizon, dark and enormous, right in their flight path.]**

**NARRATOR:** The weather that night was not good. It was about to get a lot worse.

## ACT 2: THE NIGHT TWO PLANES WENT DOWN (3:30 - 6:30)

**[VISUAL: The two flying boats lifting off into a darkening sky, March 31, 1944.]**

**NARRATOR:** March 31st, 1944. The two flying boats took off from Palau, heading for the Philippines, and flew straight into a tropical storm.

**[VISUAL: The planes buffeted by wind and rain, lightning flashing, the two of them losing sight of each other in the clouds.]**

**NARRATOR:** In the dark and the weather, the two planes lost contact with each other. And then, one of them lost a lot more than that.

**[VISUAL: TONE SHIFT, brief. Koga's flying boat, alone in the storm, its lights blinking out. It does not come back into frame.]**

**NARRATOR:** Admiral Koga's plane flew into the storm and was never seen again. There were no survivors. The commander in chief of the entire Japanese fleet was gone.

**[VISUAL: An empty admiral's chair back at headquarters. A calendar showing weeks passing.]**

**NARRATOR:** And here is the first strange part. Japan was so worried about morale, and about admitting a second fleet commander had died in a plane in under a year, that they did not announce his death. For weeks. The most powerful sailor in Japan had vanished, and the Japanese public had no idea.

**[VISUAL: The SECOND flying boat, still airborne, engines sputtering. A fuel gauge dropping toward empty.]**

**NARRATOR:** But this story is not really about Koga's plane. It is about the other one. The one carrying his chief of staff, Vice Admiral Shigeru Fukudome. And the briefcase.

**[VISUAL: Inside the second plane, Fukudome gripping a red leather briefcase on his lap, the plane shaking.]**

**NARRATOR:** Fukudome's plane survived the worst of the storm. But it burned through its fuel fighting the headwinds, and it could not reach the Philippines. So in the small hours of April 1st, out of fuel and out of options, the pilot ditched it into the sea. Off the coast of an island called Cebu.

**[VISUAL: The flying boat slamming into the black water, breaking apart, men spilling into the waves.]**

**NARRATOR:** The plane hit the water hard and started to sink. Fukudome survived. He and a few others clung to wreckage in the dark and tried to swim toward the shadow of the coastline.

**[VISUAL: The red briefcase, knocked loose, floating up and away from the sinking plane on the current. Drifting toward the beach on its own.]**

**NARRATOR:** And the briefcase, the one with the Z Plan inside, was knocked loose in the crash. It did not sink with the plane. It floated. And it started drifting, all by itself, toward a beach full of people the Japanese really did not want reading it.

**[VISUAL: The dark outline of Cebu. Small fires and huts along the coast.]**

**NARRATOR:** Because Cebu was in the occupied Philippines. And the Philippines was not quietly accepting occupation.

## ACT 3: THE ISLAND THAT WAS NOT QUIET (6:30 - 9:15)

**[VISUAL: A map of the Philippine islands with little hidden guerrilla camps marked in the hills and jungles.]**

**NARRATOR:** Quick context. When Japan took the Philippines in 1942, it did not exactly settle down into a peaceful occupation. All across the islands, Filipinos took to the hills and formed guerrilla groups to fight back.

**[VISUAL: A ragtag but organized guerrilla band, Filipino fighters with a mix of rifles, radios, and homemade gear, moving through jungle.]**

**NARRATOR:** These were serious operations. They gathered intelligence, they radioed information to the Americans, and they controlled big chunks of the countryside that the Japanese could not. Whole networks of radios, coast watchers, and couriers ran underneath the occupation the entire time. So when a Japanese plane crashed just offshore, it did not crash into empty water. It crashed into a coastline that was already watching.

**[VISUAL: An American in a worn uniform standing among the Filipino guerrillas, blueprints and a mining pick beside him.]**

**NARRATOR:** And on Cebu, the man in charge of the guerrillas was not a career soldier. He was an American mining engineer who had been living in the Philippines when the war broke out and simply refused to surrender. His name was Lieutenant Colonel James Cushing.

**[VISUAL: Cushing at a jungle table, maps and a radio in front of him, running his little army.]**

**NARRATOR:** Cushing led the Cebu guerrillas. Now, remember, at this exact moment Cushing had no idea any of the plane stuff had happened. He was just running his resistance operation. Then word started coming up from the coast.

**[VISUAL: Filipino fishermen wading ashore at dawn, dragging the waterlogged red briefcase and other bits of wreckage.]**

**NARRATOR:** Down on the beach, Filipino civilians had found the wreckage washing in. And they found a red leather briefcase. Waterlogged, but intact. So they did the sensible thing. They fished it out, they carefully dried the papers inside, and they buried the whole box to keep it safe.

**[VISUAL: The buried box under a marker, then hands digging it back up and passing it up a chain toward the hills.]**

**NARRATOR:** And over the next few days, the box made its way up the chain, from the fishermen, to local guerrillas, and up toward Cushing.

**[VISUAL: The same fishermen and guerrillas also dragging in soaked, exhausted Japanese survivors from the crash, including one senior officer on a makeshift stretcher.]**

**NARRATOR:** But the documents were not the only thing that came ashore. The survivors of the crash came ashore too. Including one very important, very soggy senior officer.

**[VISUAL: Fukudome, wet and injured, sitting under guard, guerrillas realizing slowly who they have caught.]**

**NARRATOR:** The Cebu guerrillas had captured a Japanese vice admiral. The chief of staff of the entire Combined Fleet. Which is roughly the biggest fish a guerrilla band could possibly land.

**[VISUAL: Cushing looking at the captured admiral, then at the buried box of documents, the pieces clicking together.]**

**NARRATOR:** So now Cushing had two problems in his lap. A captured enemy admiral. And a mysterious box of Japanese documents that, if they mattered, needed to get to the Americans somehow. He did not yet know how important either one was. He was about to find out that the Japanese wanted them both back very, very badly.

## ACT 4: THE PRICE OF KEEPING THEM (9:15 - 12:00)

**[VISUAL: A Japanese garrison at Cebu City getting the news. An officer slamming a table.]**

**NARRATOR:** When the Japanese realized what had happened, that a flying boat had gone down, that a vice admiral was missing, and that it had all landed in guerrilla-held territory, they reacted the way you would expect. They came looking. Hard.

**[VISUAL: Japanese troops fanning out across the countryside, searching, the map filling with search lines.]**

**NARRATOR:** They launched an intensive search across the island for the survivors and for anything the crash had left behind.

**[VISUAL: TONE SHIFT to somber, muted colors. Villages, from a distance, with smoke rising. No jokes, no comedic characters in frame. TONE: STRAIGHT for this whole beat.]**

**NARRATOR:** And the search turned into a reprisal. Japanese forces on Cebu burned villages and killed hundreds of Filipino civilians while hunting for the crew and the documents. Ordinary people, who had nothing to do with any of it, paid for it with their homes and their lives.

**[VISUAL: Cushing in his camp, grim, holding a radio message, the weight of the decision on his face. Still muted. Still straight.]**

**NARRATOR:** Cushing was watching this happen. He had a captured admiral and a box of documents. And he knew that as long as he held the admiral, the killing of civilians would continue. His guerrilla force was small. He could not fight off the Japanese army. So he made a decision.

**[VISUAL: A small procession under a white flag, Fukudome on a stretcher, being escorted toward Japanese lines. Solemn, quiet.]**

**NARRATOR:** On the morning of April 10th, 1944, Cushing arranged to hand Vice Admiral Fukudome and the surviving airmen back to the Japanese. In exchange for one thing. That the Japanese would stop killing civilians on Cebu.

**[VISUAL: The handover at the edge of Japanese-held territory. Fukudome carried across. The white flag lowered.]**

**NARRATOR:** This was not a clever trick. It was a grave choice made to save lives, and it cost Cushing a prisoner he had every reason to want to keep. The Japanese took the admiral back. And they kept their side of it. The reprisals against the villages stopped.

**[VISUAL: RETURN to normal color and tone. Cushing, back in his camp, opening the box of documents. A small, dangerous smile.]**

**NARRATOR:** But here is the thing Cushing did not hand back. The documents. He gave them the admiral. He kept the box.

**[VISUAL: The Z Plan papers spread on the table, dense Japanese naval writing, the letter Z on the cover.]**

**NARRATOR:** And the Japanese, as far as anyone can tell, assumed the papers had been lost in the crash or destroyed by the sea. They had their admiral back. They stopped looking. They never told Tokyo that the Z Plan itself might be sitting in an American guerrilla camp in the mountains of Cebu.

**[VISUAL: Cushing tapping out a radio message, the signal beaming out toward Australia.]**

**NARRATOR:** So on April 13th, Cushing radioed MacArthur's headquarters in Australia. He had something. Now he just had to get a box of soggy paper off a Japanese-occupied island, across open ocean, without losing it. And Cushing had a very Cushing idea about how to protect it.

## ACT 5: HOW TO SMUGGLE A WAR PLAN (12:00 - 15:00)

**[VISUAL: Cushing holding two empty metal mortar shells, then rolling the documents tightly to fit inside them.]**

**NARRATOR:** To keep the papers dry and hidden on the journey, the guerrillas rolled them up tightly and stuffed them inside two empty mortar shells.

**[VISUAL: The sealed mortar shells, documents safely inside, being packed for travel. A little comedic beat, the most secret plan in the Japanese Navy inside artillery casings.]**

**NARRATOR:** Which means the entire naval strategy of Imperial Japan spent part of this journey packed inside the world's most secret ammunition. Nobody was going to open those looking for battle plans.

**[VISUAL: A map. The documents moving from Cebu across the water to the neighboring island of Negros.]**

**NARRATOR:** The plan was to move the documents to the neighboring island of Negros, and have an American submarine slip in at night, surface, grab them, and disappear. Simple. Except every single step ran through Japanese-occupied territory and open, patrolled sea.

**[VISUAL: A US submarine, the USS Crevalle, cruising underwater toward the Philippines.]**

**NARRATOR:** The submarine sent for the job was the USS Crevalle, commanded by Lieutenant Commander Francis Walker. Its mission was to surface off Negros, pick up the documents and a group of evacuees, and get out.

**[VISUAL: Night. The Crevalle surfacing quietly off a dark coastline. Small boats rowing out to meet it.]**

**NARRATOR:** On the night of May 11th, 1944, the Crevalle surfaced off Negros. And a crowd showed up.

**[VISUAL: More and more people climbing aboard the sub, the crew counting, the number climbing past the plan.]**

**NARRATOR:** The plan was to take about 25 evacuees. Around 40 showed up, including women and children who needed to get off the island. The crew crammed roughly 40 people below decks, along with two mortar shells that were worth more than all of them combined.

**[VISUAL: The Crevalle diving, slipping away into deep water, the coastline shrinking behind.]**

**NARRATOR:** And then the Crevalle slipped away underwater and ran for Australia. It was not a quiet trip. Submarines in those waters got hunted. But roughly a week later, the Crevalle reached Australia. And it delivered the box.

**[VISUAL: The mortar shells being unpacked on a dock, the rolled documents pulled out, still intact.]**

**NARRATOR:** The most secret document in the Japanese Navy had traveled from a sinking plane, to a beach, to a burial site, up a guerrilla chain, out through an admiral swap, into two mortar shells, onto a submarine, across the ocean, and onto a table in Australia. And the Japanese still had no idea.

**[VISUAL: The documents being carried into a building marked with an Allied intelligence insignia.]**

**NARRATOR:** Now someone just had to read it. Which meant it landed on the desks of the one group of people who could turn a box of wet Japanese paper into a war-changing secret.

## ACT 6: THE TRANSLATORS (15:00 - 17:45)

**[VISUAL: A busy Allied intelligence office in Australia. Rows of desks, Japanese documents everywhere. A sign reads ATIS.]**

**NARRATOR:** The documents went to a unit called the Allied Translator and Interpreter Section. ATIS. This was MacArthur's translation and intelligence outfit, and it was the beating heart of Allied code and document work in the Southwest Pacific.

**[VISUAL: Translators at desks, many of them Japanese American, working carefully through dense naval text.]**

**NARRATOR:** A lot of the people doing this work were Japanese American soldiers, Nisei, whose language skills made this kind of intelligence possible. They were fighting for a country that, back home, had put many of their own families in internment camps behind barbed wire. And here they were, in a room in Australia, translating the enemy's most secret plans, doing some of the most important intelligence work of the entire Pacific war.

**[VISUAL: A translator's pen moving across the page, the Japanese characters resolving into English strategy on the other side.]**

**NARRATOR:** Starting on May 21st, 1944, they went through the Z Plan line by line. And they realized exactly what they were holding. This was Combined Fleet Secret Operation Order Number 73. Japan's actual plan for the great decisive naval battle it wanted to fight.

**[VISUAL: A stamped translation labeled "Limited Distribution Translation No. 4" being carried out of the office fast.]**

**NARRATOR:** Within days, on May 23rd, they had a finished translation. And they moved it fast. Copies went to the top of the American command.

**[VISUAL: A map of the Pacific with the intelligence racing from Australia to Pearl Harbor, then out to the fleet.]**

**NARRATOR:** From Australia, the intelligence went to Admiral Nimitz's headquarters at Pearl Harbor, got refined again, and was pushed out to the commanders who would actually be fighting the next battle. By early June, the Americans steaming toward the Mariana Islands had, in their hands, a translated copy of how Japan intended to fight them.

**[VISUAL: A US admiral on a ship reading a briefing, expression sharpening.]**

**NARRATOR:** Now, here is where a lot of retellings get carried away. So let us be honest about it. Because the truth is a little more careful than the legend.

**[VISUAL: A big glowing myth caption on screen: "THE Z PLAN WON THE BATTLE." Then a big red X drawn across it.]**

**NARRATOR:** The popular version says the stolen Z Plan simply won the Battle of the Philippine Sea. The National Archives, which holds the actual records, is more careful than that. It calls the exploitation of the Z Plan one of the greatest single intelligence feats of the entire war in that theater. But it also says, plainly, that measuring the exact effect on the battle is impossible.

**[VISUAL: The captured Z Plan sitting beside a nearly identical folder labeled "Operation A-Go."]**

**NARRATOR:** Part of why is almost funny. By June, the Japanese had rolled the Z Plan forward into a follow-up called Operation A-Go, and A-Go was essentially unchanged from the plan the Americans had already read. Japan updated the name and barely touched the strategy. So when the two fleets finally met, the Americans had a very good idea of how the enemy liked to fight.

## ACT 7: THE BATTLE THEY SAW COMING (17:45 - 20:15)

**[VISUAL: June 1944. The huge US fleet gathered off the Mariana Islands, invading Saipan. Japanese carriers steaming in from the west.]**

**NARRATOR:** June 1944. The Americans invaded the Mariana Islands, and Japan launched its long-planned decisive battle to stop them. This was the Battle of the Philippine Sea.

**[VISUAL: The Japanese plan in action, carriers sending waves of aircraft while trying to lure the US fleet away.]**

**NARRATOR:** And the Japanese ran the playbook. Big carrier air strikes. The same lure-and-flank idea from the plan. Everything they had left, thrown at the American fleet.

**[VISUAL: Admiral Spruance on his flagship, calm, keeping his fleet close to the invasion beaches instead of charging out to meet the Japanese.]**

**NARRATOR:** In command of the American Fifth Fleet was Admiral Raymond Spruance. And Spruance made a cautious, controversial call. Instead of racing west to hunt the Japanese carriers, he kept his fleet close, guarding the invasion, refusing to be lured away. Some of his own officers wanted to charge. He said no.

**[VISUAL: A careful caption: "The captured plans reinforced US caution. They did not decide the battle alone."]**

**NARRATOR:** Now, we are being honest here, so, historians link that caution partly to the captured Japanese plans, which warned of exactly the kind of end run Japan was trying to pull. The intelligence reinforced what Spruance chose to do. It did not make the choice for him, and it did not fight the battle. His fleet did.

**[VISUAL: Waves of Japanese aircraft flying toward the American fleet. American fighters and anti-aircraft guns waiting for them.]**

**NARRATOR:** And when the Japanese air strikes came in, they flew into a buzzsaw. American pilots were better trained, in better planes, guided by radar that spotted the incoming waves early, protected by a wall of anti-aircraft fire and fresh proximity-fuzed shells that made near misses count. The green Japanese aircrews, rushed into service to replace the veterans Japan had already lost, never had a chance.

**[VISUAL: Japanese planes falling out of the sky in huge numbers over the fleet. American pilots calling it in.]**

**NARRATOR:** Japanese aircraft were shot down in staggering numbers. Over two hundred fell on the morning of June 19th alone. American pilots said it was so lopsided it felt like an old-fashioned turkey shoot back home. And the name stuck.

**[VISUAL: A caption card: "THE GREAT MARIANAS TURKEY SHOOT."]**

**NARRATOR:** They called it the Great Marianas Turkey Shoot.

**[VISUAL: Japanese carriers Taiho and Shokaku taking hits from American submarines and burning.]**

**NARRATOR:** It got worse for Japan below the waves too. American submarines sank two of Japan's fleet carriers during the battle. A third carrier went down as well.

**[VISUAL: A tally on screen. Japanese losses stacking up huge. American losses small by comparison. TONE: STRAIGHT for the cost line.]**

**NARRATOR:** By the end of the two-day battle, Japan had lost three carriers and somewhere in the range of five to six hundred aircraft. And with those aircraft went their aircrews. Japanese naval aviation, the force that had once dominated the Pacific, was effectively broken here, and it never fully recovered. American aircraft losses were a fraction of that, though many US pilots died when their planes ran out of fuel returning in the dark.

**[VISUAL: The Japanese fleet retreating, battered, back across the sea.]**

**NARRATOR:** Japan had thrown its last great naval swing, and missed. The decisive battle it had dreamed about in the Z Plan had happened. It just went the other way.

**[VISUAL: The red Z briefcase from the cold open, sitting in an archive box, quiet.]**

**NARRATOR:** And it all traced back, in part, to a briefcase two fishermen pulled out of the surf.

## ACT 8: THE COST OF A LUCKY BREAK (20:15 - 21:45)

**[VISUAL: A quiet map of Cebu. Not comedic. TONE: STRAIGHT for the human-cost recap.]**

**NARRATOR:** It is easy to tell this as a lucky-break story. The floating briefcase, the mortar shells, the submarine in the night. And it is a genuinely astonishing chain of luck and nerve. But it is worth remembering what the luck cost.

**[VISUAL: Villages on Cebu, the memory of the reprisals, held plainly on screen. Still straight.]**

**NARRATOR:** Hundreds of Filipino civilians were killed on Cebu during the Japanese search for those documents and that admiral. They were not soldiers. They were people whose island happened to be where a plane came down. That price was paid before a single line of the plan was ever translated.

**[VISUAL: Cushing, in his camp, the weight of the trade still on him.]**

**NARRATOR:** And Cushing's decision to hand back Vice Admiral Fukudome to stop the killing was not the clever part of the story. It was the hard part. He gave up his greatest prisoner to protect people he could not otherwise defend. He got to keep the documents. That was the trade.

**[VISUAL: The Nisei translators at their desks in Australia, working late.]**

**NARRATOR:** And the men who turned that soggy box into usable intelligence, many of them Japanese American, did it while their own families were held behind wire back home. They are a big part of why this worked, and they are usually left out of the telling.

**[VISUAL: The Z briefcase in the National Archives today, catalogued and preserved.]**

**NARRATOR:** The whole thing is real, and it is preserved in the records of the United States National Archives. One of the greatest intelligence coups of the Pacific war started with the ocean handing the enemy a briefcase. And it was carried the rest of the way by fishermen, guerrillas, a stubborn mining engineer, a submarine crew, and a room full of translators fighting for a country that had locked up their families.

## PRODUCTION NOTES

**Voice choices:**
- Cold open leads on the single most surprising real image: fishermen pulling the enemy's entire war plan out of the ocean. It hooks in the first five seconds.
- Comedy rides on documented absurdity only: two fleet commanders lost in aircraft in under a year, Japan hiding Koga's death, the plan smuggled inside mortar shells, the enemy reusing the same plan under a new name.
- Fake dialogue kept sparse; the story is naturally strange enough that over-jokeing would cheapen it.
- Two beats are hard TONE: STRAIGHT and must be built that way: the Cebu reprisals + Cushing's return of Fukudome (Act 4, recapped Act 8), and the human cost inside the battle (Act 7 casualty line, Act 8). Muted color, no comedic characters, quiet VO.

**Accuracy corrections held (see fact-check log):**
- Impact NOT overstated. Script explicitly cites the National Archives that the battle effect is impossible to measure, and frames the intelligence as reinforcing Spruance's caution, not deciding the battle. This on-screen honesty is itself a channel-value beat.
- Fukudome given as Vice Admiral (NARA), noting some sources say Rear Admiral.
- The briefcase floated free and was found by civilians (NARA), not stripped off Fukudome.
- Mortar-shell detail kept because it is documented by NARA, not embellished.
- A-Go framed accurately as the follow-on that was essentially unchanged from the Z Plan.

**Title options:**
1. `The Plan Japan Lost in the Ocean | Codename History` (recommended. The floating-briefcase hook is the strongest and most honest curiosity gap, and it fits the standard non-codenamed format the story calls for.)
2. `Japan's Most Secret War Plan Washed Up on a Beach | Codename History`
3. `How Fishermen Captured Japan's Entire Navy Plan | Codename History`

Note: "Z Plan" is the enemy (Japanese) plan name, not an Allied operation codename, so the standard `<hook> | Codename History` format is used, not the `Codename:` format. codename-history runs its own title pass at build time; this is the working title.

**Channel placement:** A strong Pacific-theater deep-dive. Pairs well with a future Nisei linguists / MIS episode and with a Bletchley-style Pacific codebreaking episode. The teased next episode is Operation Mincemeat.

## FACT-CHECK LOG

- **Koga killed March 31, 1944, flying boat lost in a storm, Palau to Davao, no survivors, successor to Yamamoto, death hidden for weeks.** VERIFIED. [National Archives; Wikipedia "Mineichi Koga"; Pacific Wrecks]
- **Fukudome (Vice Admiral, chief of staff) ditched off Cebu ~April 1, 1944, survived, captured by guerrillas.** VERIFIED. [National Archives; Wikipedia "Mineichi Koga"; National Museum of the Pacific War] Note: some sources say Rear Admiral; NARA says Vice Admiral, used here.
- **Z Plan = Combined Fleet Secret Operation Order, issued March 8, 1944, identified by ATIS as Secret Combined Fleet Order No. 73.** VERIFIED. [National Archives; JAVA]
- **Briefcase/portfolio floated free, found by Filipino civilians, dried and buried, passed up to Cushing.** VERIFIED. [National Archives]
- **James Cushing = American mining engineer leading Cebu guerrillas.** VERIFIED. [National Archives; Wikipedia "James M. Cushing"]
- **Japanese reprisals: villages burned, hundreds of civilians killed during the search.** VERIFIED. [National Archives; Wikipedia "James M. Cushing"] TONE: STRAIGHT.
- **Cushing returned Fukudome and surviving airmen ~April 10, 1944, to stop reprisals, kept the documents; Japanese kept the promise.** VERIFIED. [National Archives; Wikipedia "James M. Cushing"] TONE: STRAIGHT.
- **Documents rolled and hidden inside two empty mortar shells; Cushing signaled SWPA April 13.** VERIFIED, documented not embellished. [National Archives]
- **USS Crevalle (Lt. Cdr. Francis D. Walker Jr.) picked up documents + ~40 evacuees (planned 25) off Negros, night of May 11, 1944, delivered to Australia ~a week later.** VERIFIED. [National Archives; National Museum of the Pacific War] Evacuee count 40 (NARA) / 41 (Pacific War Museum); script says "around 40 / roughly 40."
- **ATIS (under Mashbir) translated starting May 21, 1944; finished "Limited Distribution Translation No. 4" May 23; distributed up to Nimitz and out to the fleet by early June.** VERIFIED. [National Archives]
- **Nisei / Japanese American translators worked on it; many had families interned.** VERIFIED. [JAVA; National Archives (Text Message)] Internment context is well-documented general record.
- **Operation A-Go was the follow-on, essentially unchanged from the Z Plan.** VERIFIED. [Wikipedia "Battle of the Philippine Sea"]
- **Battle of the Philippine Sea, June 19-20, 1944; Spruance (US) vs Ozawa (Japan); Spruance kept fleet close / did not chase; caution linked partly to captured plans.** VERIFIED. [Wikipedia "Battle of the Philippine Sea"; search-corroborated Britannica]
- **US advantages: radar early warning, VT/proximity-fuzed anti-aircraft shells, and green Japanese replacement aircrews (some with only ~3 months training).** VERIFIED. [Wikipedia "Battle of the Philippine Sea"]
- **Great Marianas Turkey Shoot; over 200 Japanese aircraft down morning of June 19; Japan lost 3 carriers (Taiho, Shokaku, Hiyo) and ~550-645 aircraft; US losses a fraction, many from fuel-out night landings.** VERIFIED. [Wikipedia "Battle of the Philippine Sea"; Warfare History Network] Script says "five to six hundred" aircraft, within the 550-645 range.
- **Impact NOT overstated.** VERIFIED against source intent. National Archives calls it one of the greatest single intelligence feats in the theater but says the exact battle effect is impossible to measure. Script states this on screen. [National Archives]
