# EPISODE SCRIPT: A TOILET SANK THIS NAZI SUBMARINE

**Target runtime:** ~10-11 minutes (Tight Standard)
**Style:** OverSimplified-style faceless history, deadpan narration, comedy on documented absurdity only
**Story type:** Single-incident true military history (WW2, Kriegsmarine)
**Title (standard tier):** A Toilet Sank This Nazi Submarine | Codename History

> ACCURACY NOTE FOR PRODUCTION: The captain-flushed-it-himself version is a popular internet legend and is NOT supported by the record. Schlitt's own report places him in the engine room; he learned of the leak afterward. The script plays the legend, then corrects it on-screen. Do not cut the correction. Casualty beats (4 dead, 46 captured) are played completely straight and tagged TONE: STRAIGHT. Per the record, none of the 4 deaths were caused by the gas or flooding directly: 1 man died of illness the day before, and 3 drowned in heavy seas after abandoning ship. Do not use NUMA's unverified "it was a cover story / scuttled on purpose" claim anywhere.

> VOICE NOTE: Dry, confident, OverSimplified deadpan. Punchy fragments for timing. GERMANY character voice is a bureaucratic over-engineer, used sparingly. Go quiet and slow for every TONE: STRAIGHT beat. No em-dashes anywhere in narration.

**[VISUAL: Cutaway interior of a WW2 U-boat head. A German sailor stands proudly at a strange, complicated high-tech toilet, one hand on a big brass flush lever, total confidence on his face.]**

**NARRATOR:** This man is about to flush a toilet. He has never been more sure of anything.

**[VISUAL: He pulls the lever. A one-beat pause. His smile holds.]**

**NARRATOR:** Watch his face.

**[VISUAL: The compartment ERUPTS. High-pressure seawater blasts back out of the bowl in a violent geyser, slamming the sailor backward.]**

**NARRATOR:** That is the sound of the ocean coming back up the pipe.

**[VISUAL: Crew scramble in the narrow corridor as a faint green gas begins to curl up from a hatch in the floor.]**

**NARRATOR:** In a few minutes, this brand new submarine will be on the surface, under attack, and finished. It never sank a single enemy ship. It was undone by its bathroom.

**[VISUAL: Slow push on the green gas rising past a flickering bulb.]**

**NARRATOR:** And I promise you, every part of this is real.

**[TITLE CARD: "A Toilet Sank This Nazi Submarine"]**

## ACT 1: A SUBMARINE THAT NEVER WON (0:38 - 2:35)

**[VISUAL: Shipyard, Danzig, 1943. Cranes over a half-built U-boat hull, sparks flying, swastika flags on the gantry.]**

**NARRATOR:** Meet U-1206. A German Type VIIC U-boat. The keel was laid in the summer of 1943, in Danzig.

**[VISUAL: The finished submarine slides into gray water, commissioning flags snapping.]**

**NARRATOR:** She was commissioned in March 1944. A real warship, built to hunt Allied convoys in the North Atlantic.

**[VISUAL: Portrait card, a young Kriegsmarine officer, clean cap, serious eyes. Caption: "Kptlt. Karl-Adolf Schlitt, age 27".]**

**NARRATOR:** By July 1944, she had a new commander. Kapitanleutnant Karl-Adolf Schlitt. Twenty seven years old. Yes, that was really his name. We will come back to it.

**[VISUAL: A wall map of the North Sea. A red line waits to be drawn from Germany up toward Scotland.]**

**NARRATOR:** Now here is the thing about U-1206. Before the trip you are about to watch, she had done nothing. And I mean that as a statistic.

**[VISUAL: A big scoreboard graphic. "SHIPS SUNK: 0". "SHIPS DAMAGED: 0".]**

**NARRATOR:** Zero ships sunk. Zero ships damaged. Ever. In her entire existence, the only thing this submarine would ever defeat was itself.

**[VISUAL: The scoreboard holds. A little cartoon tumbleweed rolls across it.]**

**NARRATOR:** Hold that number in your head. It matters later.

**[VISUAL: Calendar pages tearing off toward April 1945. A tiny European map with Allied arrows closing in from every side.]**

**NARRATOR:** And notice the date. It is now April 1945. The war in Europe has about a month left. Germany is losing on every front.

**[VISUAL: The submarine at a pier, crew loading supplies, taking on around fifty men.]**

**NARRATOR:** On the 6th of April, U-1206 leaves on her first war patrol. Roughly fifty men aboard. Their mission is to go find Allied ships in the North Sea and finally, finally, sink something.

**[VISUAL: Crew inside the cramped control room, hopeful faces, dials and periscope.]**

**NARRATOR:** Imagine being one of those men. The war is basically over. Everyone knows it. And you are being sent out to sea in a metal tube to try to win a fight that is already lost.

**[VISUAL: The submarine slips out to sea under a low gray sky, bow cutting cold water.]**

**NARRATOR:** They will never fire a meaningful shot. Because the most dangerous thing on this submarine was not a torpedo. It was not a British destroyer. It was down the hall, behind a little door.

## ACT 2: THE WONDER-TOILET (2:35 - 4:45)

**[VISUAL: A little steel door in the corridor. On it, an official-looking sign. A dramatic beam of light hits it.]**

**NARRATOR:** Every submarine has a problem nobody likes to talk about. What do you do with the toilet when you are deep underwater.

**[VISUAL: Simple diagram. A submarine at depth, an arrow showing the crushing weight of water pressing in from outside.]**

**NARRATOR:** Down deep, the sea outside is pushing in with enormous pressure. Older boats had a simple fix. You could only use the toilet near the surface, or you stored the waste in tanks and dumped it later. Not glamorous. But it worked.

**[VISUAL: German engineers in a workshop crowded around a gleaming, over-complicated toilet covered in valves, gauges, and levers.]**

**NARRATOR:** Germany, being Germany, decided this was unacceptable. So they built a better toilet.

**[VISUAL: Close on the toilet. It has more valves than a small submarine probably should.]**

**NARRATOR:** A new high-pressure system that could flush your business straight out into the ocean, against all that pressure, even while running deep. On paper, brilliant.

**[VISUAL: GERMANY character, a stern engineer with a clipboard, gesturing proudly at the toilet.]**

**GERMANY (character voice):** "Now the crew may relieve themselves at any depth. Efficiency."

**[VISUAL: The same toilet, now surrounded by a confusing tangle of valve-turn diagrams and arrows numbered 1 through many.]**

**NARRATOR:** There was one small catch. To flush it correctly, you had to open and close a series of valves in exactly the right order. Get the sequence wrong, and you are not flushing outward anymore.

**[VISUAL: A training room. A few sailors sit at desks studying diagrams of the toilet like it is a final exam.]**

**NARRATOR:** It was so complicated that the German navy trained dedicated specialists just to operate it. Let that sink in. This submarine had toilet technicians. Men whose actual job, in the middle of a world war, was to know how the toilet worked. There are ordinary sailors who did not get that much training.

**[VISUAL: A sailor proudly holding a little certificate next to the toilet, GERMANY nodding approval.]**

**GERMANY (character voice):** "You are now certified. Do not touch it without me."

**[VISUAL: The little steel door, closed, ominous light around the edges.]**

**NARRATOR:** So the plan was simple. Never let anyone use this thing without an expert nearby. On a submarine with fifty men and one very fancy toilet, what could possibly go wrong.

## ACT 3: THE WRONG SEQUENCE (4:45 - 6:50)

**[VISUAL: 14 April 1945. The submarine submerged, gauge showing roughly 200 feet. A caption: "~8 to 10 miles off Peterhead, Scotland".]**

**NARRATOR:** April 14th, 1945. U-1206 is submerged about two hundred feet down, off the northeast coast of Scotland, near a town called Peterhead.

**[VISUAL: Depth diagram again, that heavy arrow of pressure pushing on the hull from all sides.]**

**NARRATOR:** Two hundred feet down means the sea outside is pressing on that toilet with the weight of several atmospheres. This is the exact worst moment to make a mistake.

**[VISUAL: The head. Someone at the toilet, shown only from behind, hand reaching for the valves.]**

**NARRATOR:** And someone makes a mistake. We do not actually know who. The record just calls him a crewman. A rating, a mechanic, one of the men. History did not save his name, which, honestly, is probably a mercy. Because he is about to become part of one of the strangest losses of the entire war.

**[VISUAL: Close on the valve panel, numbered steps, a hand hovering, hesitating.]**

**NARRATOR:** Remember, this is a system with a strict order of operations. Open this, then close that, then this. It is basically a small exam, and it is being taken two hundred feet underwater.

**[VISUAL: Hand turning a valve. The wrong valve. A little red X flashes over the sequence diagram.]**

**NARRATOR:** He opens the valves in the wrong order.

**[VISUAL: The geyser from the cold open, replayed. High-pressure seawater and sewage blast back into the compartment.]**

**NARRATOR:** Instead of flushing out, the ocean comes roaring back in. The operator gets blasted with a geyser of seawater and everything he was trying to get rid of.

**[VISUAL: Water pouring down through a grating in the floor into a dark compartment below, packed with battery cells.]**

**NARRATOR:** But the flood does not stop at the floor. Directly beneath the toilet is the battery compartment. And seawater is now pouring down onto the batteries.

**[VISUAL: Chemistry warning graphic. Seawater plus battery, an arrow, a cloud of pale green gas. Simple and clear.]**

**NARRATOR:** Here is where a plumbing problem becomes a poison problem. When seawater hits these batteries, it produces chlorine gas.

**[VISUAL: The green gas curling up out of the floor grating into the crew corridor.]**

**NARRATOR:** Chlorine. The same stuff you are warned never to make by mixing the wrong cleaners under your sink. Except now it is filling a sealed metal tube, underwater, with fifty men inside.

**[VISUAL: Crew pulling on emergency gear, faces tense, waving at the rising gas.]**

**NARRATOR:** The crew cannot breathe this and stay down. Which leaves U-1206 with exactly one option. And it is the one option a submarine hiding off enemy coastline never, ever wants to take.

**[VISUAL: The submarine's nose tilting sharply upward, blowing tanks, racing for the surface.]**

**NARRATOR:** Go up.

## ACT 4: SURFACE, BOMBED, SCUTTLED (6:50 - 8:35)

**[VISUAL: The submarine breaches the surface into gray daylight, water sheeting off the hull, out in the open.]**

**NARRATOR:** U-1206 blows her tanks and claws to the surface. And a submarine on the surface, in daylight, off the coast of Britain in 1945, is not a submarine anymore. It is a target.

**[VISUAL: Low over the water, a British aircraft banking, spotting the dark shape below.]**

**NARRATOR:** It does not take long. British aircraft spot her almost immediately. This is a coastline the Royal Air Force watches constantly, hunting for exactly this. A U-boat on the surface here has minutes, not hours.

**[VISUAL: The plane rolling into an attack run, the surfaced submarine helpless below.]**

**NARRATOR:** And they attack. The boat that spent its whole life sinking nothing is now getting bombed while it sits on the surface, poisoned by its own toilet.

**[VISUAL: Explosions bracketing the hull, water erupting, the submarine rocking hard.]**

**NARRATOR:** The attack does its work. U-1206 is damaged. She can no longer dive. And a U-boat that cannot dive is finished.

**[VISUAL: Schlitt on the conning tower, giving the order, crew already moving.]**

**NARRATOR:** So Kapitanleutnant Schlitt gives the order every submarine commander dreads. Abandon ship. Scuttle her. They sink their own boat on purpose, so the enemy cannot take her.

**[VISUAL: Sailors going over the side into cold, heaving gray water, life rafts, the submarine settling behind them.]**

**NARRATOR:** The crew pours out into the North Sea. And this is the part where the joke stops.

**[VISUAL: The submarine sliding under the waves for the last time, stern rising, gone. Zero enemy ships to her name.]**

**NARRATOR:** U-1206 goes to the bottom off Peterhead. First patrol. Only patrol. Total enemy ships sunk, still zero.

**[VISUAL: Hold on the empty gray sea where the boat just was. Let it sit.]**

**NARRATOR:** But before we laugh at the submarine sunk by its toilet, we need to talk about what it cost. Because it was not free.

## ACT 5: THE COST (8:35 - 9:35)

**[VISUAL: TONE: STRAIGHT. Plain slate, muted. No cartoon elements. A simple count: "50 aboard. 46 survived."]**

**NARRATOR:** Around fifty men left Germany aboard U-1206. Forty six of them were pulled from the water and captured. They spent the rest of the war, and the weeks after Germany surrendered, as prisoners. They made it. Four did not come home.

**[VISUAL: TONE: STRAIGHT. Four small quiet markers, no names, no drama. Slow.]**

**NARRATOR:** And it matters how those four were lost. Because it was not the gas, and it was not the flooding.

**[VISUAL: TONE: STRAIGHT. A single marker set slightly apart. Caption held gently: "the day before".]**

**NARRATOR:** By the record, one man had already died of illness the day before the toilet ever failed.

**[VISUAL: TONE: STRAIGHT. Cold, heavy seas. Three markers on the water. No music swell.]**

**NARRATOR:** The other three drowned in heavy seas after they abandoned ship. They survived the gas. They survived the bombing. And the sea took them anyway.

**[VISUAL: TONE: STRAIGHT. A quiet calendar. April 1945. Then May 8th, 1945 circled softly.]**

**NARRATOR:** The war in Europe would be over in about three weeks. Four men were gone, over a plumbing accident, at the very end of a war that was already lost. Keep that in mind through the rest of this.

**[VISUAL: TONE: STRAIGHT. Slow fade from the gray sea back toward normal lighting.]**

**NARRATOR:** Okay. Now, the part everyone gets wrong.

## ACT 6: THE LEGEND VS THE REPORT (9:35 - 10:50)

**[VISUAL: A phone screen full of memes and captions: "Nazi captain flushed toilet, sank own sub". Laughing emojis.]**

**NARRATOR:** If you have heard this story before, you have heard a specific version. The captain sat down, used the fancy toilet himself, flushed it, and personally sank his own submarine.

**[VISUAL: Cartoon of a smug captain on the toilet reaching for the lever, comedic.]**

**NARRATOR:** It is a perfect story. The commander, undone by his own bathroom. The internet loves it.

**[VISUAL: Record scratch freeze on the cartoon. A big stamp slams over it: "NOT WHAT HIS REPORT SAYS".]**

**NARRATOR:** There is just one problem. That is not what happened.

**[VISUAL: An official-looking German report document, Schlitt's after-action account, a finger tracing a line about the engine room.]**

**NARRATOR:** According to Schlitt's own official report, he was not even in the bathroom. He was back in the engine room, helping to repair a diesel engine, when he was told there was a leak up forward.

**[VISUAL: Split screen. Left: "THE INTERNET SAYS" with the toilet cartoon. Right: "THE REPORT SAYS" with Schlitt at the engine.]**

**NARRATOR:** So here is the honest version. The captain did not flush the toilet. Someone else did. And a toilet did not sink a warship mid-battle either. The toilet forced them up, the aircraft did the damage, and the crew scuttled the boat themselves. A chain of events. Not one legendary flush.

**[VISUAL: The scoreboard one more time. "SHIPS SUNK: 0". Then a new line drops in: "SUNK BY: its own bathroom".]**

**NARRATOR:** And yet. Strip away the myth, and the true version is still absurd. A brand new U-boat, on its first patrol, was lost because of its toilet. That part is completely real. You do not need to exaggerate it. It is already ridiculous exactly as it happened.

**[VISUAL: The over-complicated toilet again, glowing slightly, almost like a villain reveal.]**

**NARRATOR:** Germany built a toilet so advanced it needed its own experts, and that toilet did more damage to this submarine than the entire Royal Navy ever managed.

**[VISUAL: The portrait card of Schlitt returns. Caption: "survived the war, survived captivity".]**

**NARRATOR:** As for Schlitt himself. He survived the sinking. He survived captivity. And he reportedly went on to study law and public service after the war.

**[VISUAL: The surname "SCHLITT" on screen, a little magnifying glass over it, a shrug emoji.]**

**NARRATOR:** And yes, his name really was Schlitt. It sounds close to a certain German slang word. That is a coincidence. History just has a sense of humor, and it does not always ask permission.
