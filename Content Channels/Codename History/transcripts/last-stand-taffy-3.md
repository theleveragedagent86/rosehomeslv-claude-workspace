# EPISODE SCRIPT: THE DESTROYERS THAT CHARGED A BATTLESHIP

**Target runtime:** ~20 minutes (~3,000 narration words at ~150 wpm)
**Style:** OverSimplified format. Comedic animated history. Flat colors, simple characters, exaggerated reactions, fake country/ship dialogue. Human cost played completely straight.
**Story type:** Deep-dive on a single real battle, the Battle off Samar, 25 October 1944, told from the deck of the destroyers.
**Title (standard tier):** `The Destroyers That Charged a Battleship | Codename History`

> ACCURACY NOTE FOR PRODUCTION: This battle is more famous than most channel topics, so the fresh angle is the view from the tin cans, the destroyers and destroyer escorts, not the usual carrier-deck overview. Do NOT let any single ship "single-handedly" turn back the fleet. The record is a combined effect: multiple torpedo runs, relentless air strikes, smoke, weather, and Kurita's own confusion. Every casualty and ship-loss figure is anchored to two or more reputable sources. Keep the jokes on the absurdity of the mismatch and on Kurita's decision-making. Keep the deaths straight.
>
> VOICE NOTE: The comedy lives in the NARRATION. Deadpan delivery. Roast real, documented decisions only. Second-person "you" pulls the viewer in. Ships and admirals get fake dialogue. When the tone shifts to the men in the water, the jokes stop completely.

## ACT 1: THE WRONG PEOPLE FOR THE JOB (0:45 - 3:30)

**[VISUAL: A calm sea off a green island labeled "SAMAR, PHILIPPINES." Six small flat-topped carriers puttering along, looking sleepy.]**

**NARRATOR:** Let's meet the good guys. This is Taffy 3. On paper it sounds intimidating. Six aircraft carriers. In reality, it was the naval equivalent of showing up to a heavyweight fight with six guys named Kevin.

**[VISUAL: One of the little carriers labeled "ESCORT CARRIER." A chart pops up comparing it to a real fleet carrier, which is roughly three times the size.]**

**NARRATOR:** These were escort carriers. Small, slow, and thin-skinned. The kind of ship where the crews joked that the letters CVE stood for "Combustible, Vulnerable, and Expendable." When your own crew nicknames the ship after the three ways it can let them down, that tells you where it sat in the pecking order. Their whole job was to sit off the beach and help the soldiers invading the Philippines. Support work. Nothing fancy. The naval equivalent of catering the event, not headlining it.

**[VISUAL: Three slightly bigger little ships and four even smaller ones circling the carriers protectively.]**

**NARRATOR:** Guarding them were seven escorts. Three destroyers, and four even smaller ships called destroyer escorts. The whole group affectionately called these little guys "the small boys."

**[VISUAL: A destroyer flexing a tiny cartoon muscle. A "5 INCH GUN" label with an arrow, and a little rack of torpedoes.]**

**NARRATOR:** A destroyer is fast and feisty. It carries a handful of five-inch guns and some torpedoes. Against a submarine, it's a menace. Against a battleship, it is a rounding error. A destroyer escort was smaller and slower still. These were the ships America built by the hundred to guard convoys and chase submarines across the Atlantic. Nobody, anywhere, ever designed them to fight a battleship. That would be like sending a golden retriever to arm-wrestle a forklift.

**[VISUAL: The USS Johnston, front and center. A commander stands on the bridge, short, barrel-chested, arms crossed.]**

**NARRATOR:** Which brings us to this man. Commander Ernest Evans, captain of the destroyer USS Johnston. Evans was Cherokee and Creek, born in Pawnee, Oklahoma. At the Naval Academy they called him "Big Chief." The historian Samuel Eliot Morison described him as a fighting Cherokee, short, barrel-chested, loud of voice, a born leader.

**[VISUAL: Flashback. The Johnston's commissioning day. Evans addressing the crew, finger raised.]**

**NARRATOR:** The day his ship was commissioned, a full year before any of this, Evans told his crew exactly who they were dealing with.

**EVANS (character voice):** "This is going to be a fighting ship. I intend to go in harm's way, and anyone who doesn't want to go along had better get off right now."

**[VISUAL: The crew glancing nervously at each other. Nobody gets off.]**

**NARRATOR:** Nobody got off. Remember that line. He absolutely meant it.

**[VISUAL: Wide shot of the sleepy little task unit at dawn. Peaceful. Coffee. A pilot yawning in a cockpit.]**

**NARRATOR:** So that's Taffy 3. Support carriers, small boys, and a captain itching for a fight he was never supposed to get. It's dawn. The sea is calm. And everyone here believes the big scary Japanese fleet is somewhere very far away.

**[VISUAL: The camera pans past the horizon. Just past the edge of the frame, a forest of pagoda-shaped masts is rising out of the mist.]**

**NARRATOR:** They believed wrong.

## ACT 2: THE BIGGEST MEATBALL FLAG HE EVER SAW (3:30 - 6:15)

**[VISUAL: Rewind an entire night. A giant Japanese fleet steaming through a narrow strait in the dark, labeled "CENTER FORCE."]**

**NARRATOR:** To explain what's about to ruin their morning, we back up one night. This is the Japanese Center Force, under Vice Admiral Takeo Kurita. And it is the exact opposite of six guys named Kevin.

**[VISUAL: The fleet lines up for a roll call. Each ship stamps forward as it's named.]**

**NARRATOR:** Four battleships, including the Yamato. Six heavy cruisers. Two light cruisers. And around eleven destroyers. This was the most powerful surface fleet Japan could still put to sea. The Yamato by itself weighed more than every ship in Taffy 3 put together. This was not a fair fight, and it was not supposed to be one.

**[VISUAL: A map. The Center Force slips through the San Bernardino Strait, which is wide open and completely unguarded. A big red arrow curves south toward Samar.]**

**NARRATOR:** And through a long chain of events happening elsewhere in this enormous battle, the American fleet that was supposed to be blocking their exit had gone chasing a decoy hundreds of miles to the north. So the strait was wide open. Kurita sailed straight through it in the dark and turned south, looking for the invasion beaches.

**[VISUAL: Kurita on the Yamato's bridge, squinting at the empty horizon, genuinely confused that nobody stopped him.]**

**NARRATOR:** Nobody was there to stop him. Which was, from his point of view, suspicious, but also great. It is the naval version of finding the vault door wide open and no guards in sight. You take the gold, but you keep glancing over your shoulder the whole time.

**[VISUAL: Back to Taffy 3. Six twenty-two in the morning. A lookout and a patrol pilot both spot masts on the horizon at the same instant.]**

**NARRATOR:** Six thirty-seven in the morning. A Taffy 3 lookout and a patrol pilot spot ships on the horizon. At first, everyone assumes they're friendly. They are extremely not friendly.

**[VISUAL: An ensign on a bridge, eyes wide, yelling into a radio.]**

**NARRATOR:** One of the men who identified them was Ensign William Brooks, and his report is a genuine historical quote.

**BROOKS (character voice):** "I can see pagoda masts. I see the biggest meatball flag on the biggest battleship I ever saw!"

**[VISUAL: On the American bridge, Admiral Clifton Sprague freezes mid-sip of coffee.]**

**NARRATOR:** In command of Taffy 3 was Rear Admiral Clifton Sprague, nicknamed "Ziggy." Ziggy now had to process the single worst piece of news an escort-carrier admiral can receive. The most powerful battleship fleet on earth was eating breakfast in his lap, and the fast American battleships that should have been protecting him were a full night's sail away.

**[VISUAL: The Yamato's giant guns rotate toward the little carriers. A puff. Enormous colored geysers of water erupt around Taffy 3.]**

**NARRATOR:** At around a quarter to seven, the Japanese opened fire. Their shells were dyed different colors so each ship could spot its own splashes. So the first thing Taffy 3 saw was giant fountains of red, green, and yellow water walking toward them. It was, genuinely, one of the most beautiful things you could ever see, and every single color of it could sink them instantly. It is the only firework show in history where the correct response was to scream.

**[VISUAL: Sprague, jaw set, grabbing the radio.]**

**NARRATOR:** Ziggy Sprague had a few seconds to decide what to do with six baby carriers and seven small boys against a fleet built to erase them. He made his choice.

**[VISUAL: Cut to black on his mouth opening. Hold.]**

**NARRATOR:** He decided to fight.

## ACT 3: MAKE SMOKE AND RUN (6:15 - 8:30)

**[VISUAL: The six escort carriers turning away as one, black smoke pouring from their stacks, running for a nearby rain squall.]**

**NARRATOR:** Sprague's plan had two halves. Half one: the carriers run. They turned tail, cranked their little engines to the absolute limit, and made for a rain squall to hide in, pumping out thick black smoke to blind the Japanese gunners.

**[VISUAL: The escort carriers' flight decks erupting with planes taking off in a hurry, some barely armed.]**

**NARRATOR:** And they launched every single aircraft they had. Fighters, bombers, whatever was on deck. Some planes took off with the wrong bombs. Some took off with no bombs at all. It did not matter. Sprague wanted every plane in the sky, buzzing the Japanese fleet like a cloud of furious wasps.

**[VISUAL: The three destroyers and four destroyer escorts, the "small boys," lined up between the carriers and the oncoming Japanese fleet.]**

**NARRATOR:** Half two of the plan was darker. The carriers were slow. The Japanese were fast. Left alone, the battleships would simply run the little carriers down and sink them one by one. Someone had to buy the carriers time, and there was only one way to buy it. Somebody had to go stand in front of the battleships. And the only thing standing between six fleeing carriers and four battleships was seven tiny ships.

**[VISUAL: A radio speaker. The word "SMALL BOYS" crackling out of it.]**

**NARRATOR:** So the order went out to the small boys: get between the carriers and the enemy, lay smoke, and attack. Attack the battleships. With torpedoes. On purpose.

**[VISUAL: Aboard the destroyer escort USS Samuel B. Roberts, the smallest ship of all. Its captain, Copeland, picks up the loudspeaker.]**

**NARRATOR:** On the smallest ship in the group, the destroyer escort Samuel B. Roberts, Lieutenant Commander Robert Copeland got on the loudspeaker and told his crew the truth. His words come straight from the ship's action report.

**COPELAND (character voice):** "A fight against overwhelming odds from which survival could not be expected, during which time we would do what damage we could."

**[VISUAL: TONE SHIFT. Muted colors. The Roberts crew at their stations, quiet, listening. No jokes.]**

**NARRATOR:** Read that again. Survival could not be expected. Every man on that ship heard those words and then went to his battle station anyway. That is what the small boys were being asked to do.

**[VISUAL: Color returns. The USS Johnston, out front, its bow already swinging toward the enemy before any order even reaches it.]**

**NARRATOR:** But one ship wasn't waiting for the order. Commander Evans, remember, had promised a year earlier to go in harm's way. Harm had arrived. And Evans intended to keep his promise.

**[VISUAL: The Johnston at full throttle, alone, charging directly at the entire Japanese battle line.]**

**NARRATOR:** So before anyone told him to, Ernest Evans pointed the Johnston at the biggest fleet in the Pacific, rang up flank speed, and charged.

---

## ACT 4: THE FIRST CHARGE (8:30 - 11:15)

**[VISUAL: The Johnston closing the range fast, zig-zagging through colored shell splashes, laying a wall of white smoke behind it.]**

**NARRATOR:** The Johnston sprinted straight at a Japanese heavy cruiser called the Kumano, weaving through shellfire, trailing smoke to hide the carriers behind it. Evans held his fire until the range was close enough to actually hurt something.

**[VISUAL: The Johnston's little five-inch guns hammering away, scoring hits on the cruiser's superstructure, which bursts into flame.]**

**NARRATOR:** Then the Johnston opened up. Its five-inch guns raked the Kumano's upper decks, setting the superstructure on fire. For a destroyer, this was outstanding shooting. For the war overall, this was a mosquito biting a rhinoceros. But the mosquito wasn't finished, and it turned out this particular mosquito had brought torpedoes.

**[VISUAL: The Johnston swinging broadside. Ten torpedoes drop into the water and streak toward the cruiser.]**

**NARRATOR:** At close range, Evans launched all ten of the Johnston's torpedoes at the Kumano and turned hard away.

**[VISUAL: Underwater view. One torpedo runs true. A huge explosion at the cruiser's bow.]**

**NARRATOR:** And one of them hit. The torpedo blew the entire bow off the Kumano. A destroyer had just crippled a heavy cruiser and knocked it out of the fight. The mosquito had teeth.

**[VISUAL: Celebration on the Johnston's deck, cut short. In the distance, the Yamato's massive turrets swivel toward the little ship.]**

**NARRATOR:** And then the Johnston's luck ran out. It had gotten close enough to sting a cruiser, which meant it had gotten close enough for the battleships to notice it.

**[VISUAL: Three towering geysers straddle the Johnston. Direct hits. The little ship staggers, smoke pouring out.]**

**NARRATOR:** Battleship-caliber shells slammed into the Johnston. They tore into her engine rooms, cut her speed in half, and knocked out half her guns. On the bridge, the blast blew Evans's shirt clean off and took two of his fingers.

**[VISUAL: Evans, bleeding, wrapping a cloth around his hand, refusing to leave the bridge. He keeps giving orders.]**

**NARRATOR:** Evans wrapped his hand, kept command, and kept fighting. A passing rain squall briefly hid the crippled Johnston. He used the time to catch his breath. And then, instead of using the cover to slip away, Evans turned his broken ship back toward the enemy.

**[VISUAL: Behind him, more small boys are now charging in too. Hoel and Heermann and the Samuel B. Roberts, all racing toward the Japanese line.]**

**NARRATOR:** Because he wasn't alone anymore. The other small boys had followed him in. And this is the part the legend usually gets wrong, so let's get it right.

**[VISUAL: A record-scratch freeze. A "MYTH VS RECORD" stamp slaps onto the screen.]**

**NARRATOR:** No single one of these ships turned back the Japanese fleet by itself. What actually happened was messier and, honestly, more impressive. It was all of them at once.

---

## ACT 5: DEATH BY A THOUSAND TIN CANS (11:15 - 14:15)

**[VISUAL: A wide chaotic battle map. Small American ships darting in and out of smoke, torpedoes crisscrossing the water, planes diving everywhere.]**

**NARRATOR:** Here's the actual machine that beat Kurita. It had a lot of moving parts.

**[VISUAL: The destroyers Hoel and Heermann charging battleships, launching torpedo spreads.]**

**NARRATOR:** Part one, the torpedoes. The destroyers Hoel and Heermann and the little Samuel B. Roberts all bored in and threw their torpedoes at the battleships and cruisers. Remember, the Roberts was a destroyer escort, the smallest ship in the fight, and it still steamed to point-blank range and slugged it out with a heavy cruiser. Most of these torpedoes missed. But that was almost the point.

**[VISUAL: A giant Japanese battleship frantically turning sideways to dodge a spread of torpedo wakes, breaking out of its formation.]**

**NARRATOR:** Because when you fire torpedoes at a battleship, the battleship has to turn away to dodge them. And a battleship that's busy dodging is a battleship that's not shooting at your carriers, and not staying in formation. Every torpedo run scattered the Japanese fleet a little more. They started tripping over each other.

**[VISUAL: Part two. The cloud of escort-carrier planes strafing decks, dropping bombs, and making dry attack runs with empty racks.]**

**NARRATOR:** Part two, the airplanes. That cloud of wasps from the escort carriers never stopped. They strafed the decks, dropped what bombs they had, and then, out of ammunition, kept making fake attack runs anyway, just to force the big ships to keep dodging. Picture threatening a battleship with an empty plane, and the battleship believing you. The Japanese couldn't tell the empty planes from the armed ones, so an unarmed pilot pretending to be dangerous was, briefly, exactly as effective as a real one.

**[VISUAL: Part three. Thick smoke and a rain squall rolling across the battlefield, swallowing ships.]**

**NARRATOR:** Part three, the weather and the smoke. Between the funnel smoke, the smoke generators, and a passing rain squall, the Japanese gunners kept losing sight of their targets. A ship you can't see is a ship you can't hit.

**[VISUAL: Kurita on the Yamato's bridge, staring at chaos, thoroughly confused, sweat on his brow.]**

**NARRATOR:** And part four, the biggest one, was inside Kurita's own head. From the Yamato's bridge, none of this looked like a support unit fighting for its life. It looked insane. Nobody sane attacks battleships with destroyers. Nobody launches this many planes off little escort carriers.

**[VISUAL: Kurita imagining, in a thought bubble, a fleet of giant American fleet carriers and fast battleships.]**

**NARRATOR:** So Kurita started to believe he wasn't fighting little Taffy 3 at all. He convinced himself he'd stumbled into the main American fleet, the big carriers and fast battleships, and that if he lingered, they'd sink him. He was, to be clear, completely wrong. The ships he was chewing up were baby carriers and glorified submarine hunters. But he didn't know that. All he could see was a support unit that fought like it had nothing to lose, and to a battleship admiral, that made no sense unless there was a much bigger threat hiding in the smoke.

**[VISUAL: The Yamato itself forced to turn away from a torpedo spread, running north, away from the battle, for several minutes.]**

**NARRATOR:** At one point the Yamato itself had to turn and run north to dodge a spread of torpedoes, and it kept steaming that way for minutes, carrying the fleet's own commander away from the fight he was winning. The largest battleship ever built spent part of the battle essentially fleeing from ships a fraction of its size, with the boss on board, unable to do anything but hold on. His flagship was pointing him at the exit, and taking him there.

**[VISUAL: The battle at its peak. Japanese cruisers on fire from the endless air attacks.]**

**NARRATOR:** And the air attacks kept adding up. Over the morning, three of Kurita's heavy cruisers, the Chokai, the Chikuma, and the Suzuya, were sunk or crippled and finished off, mostly by those relentless little planes. The tin cans and the wasps were slowly taking his fleet apart around him.

**[VISUAL: Kurita, overwhelmed, makes a decision. He raises a signal flag.]**

**NARRATOR:** So a little after nine in the morning, with victory sitting right in front of him, Takeo Kurita gave an order that historians have argued about ever since.

**[VISUAL: The signal: "MY COURSE NORTH." The entire Japanese fleet begins to turn away from Taffy 3.]**

**NARRATOR:** He turned around. He broke off the attack, gathered his scattered ships, and steamed north, away from the carriers, away from the beaches, away from the win. Taffy 3 had been minutes from annihilation. And the Japanese fleet just left.

## ACT 6: THE COST (14:15 - 17:30)

**[VISUAL: TONE SHIFT. The color drains out. Slow, quiet shots. The battlefield after Kurita turns away, full of smoke and burning ships. No jokes from here.]**

**NARRATOR:** Taffy 3 survived. But surviving is not the same as unharmed, and the price of that morning was paid by the small boys and by one of the carriers.

**[VISUAL: The USS Hoel, dead in the water, riddled with holes, sinking.]**

**NARRATOR:** The destroyer Hoel had charged the battleships and taken more than forty hits before she went down. About two hundred and fifty of her men were killed.

**[VISUAL: The USS Samuel B. Roberts, the little destroyer escort, going under by the stern.]**

**NARRATOR:** The Samuel B. Roberts, the smallest ship there, the one whose captain had told them survival could not be expected, fought until she was wrecked and then sank. About ninety of her crew died. The ship earned a nickname that stuck: the destroyer escort that fought like a battleship.

**[VISUAL: The USS Gambier Bay, an escort carrier, listing hard, then capsizing and sinking.]**

**NARRATOR:** The escort carrier Gambier Bay was caught by Japanese gunfire and sank, the only American carrier ever lost to enemy surface fire. About one hundred and forty-seven of her men were killed.

**[VISUAL: The USS Johnston. Dead in the water, surrounded by Japanese destroyers, still firing its remaining gun.]**

**NARRATOR:** And the Johnston, Evans's ship, the one that charged first, was finally overwhelmed. Ringed by Japanese ships, out of torpedoes, out of speed, she kept firing until she was shot to pieces. Evans, still wounded, gave the order to abandon ship.

**[VISUAL: The Johnston rolling over and sinking. Empty water where she had been.]**

**NARRATOR:** She rolled over and went down around ten in the morning. Of her crew of roughly three hundred and twenty-seven men, about one hundred and forty-one survived. Commander Ernest Evans was last seen in the water near his sinking ship. He was never found.

**[VISUAL: A little later that same morning. A single Japanese plane dives deliberately into the flight deck of the escort carrier St. Lo. A massive explosion.]**

**NARRATOR:** And the morning wasn't done. Shortly after the surface fight, a Japanese plane deliberately crashed into the escort carrier St. Lo. She exploded and sank. She is generally recorded as the first American ship sunk by a kamikaze attack. About one hundred and thirteen of her crew were killed.

**[VISUAL: Wide shot. Hundreds of men in life jackets scattered across the open ocean, small in a vast sea.]**

**NARRATOR:** In total, more than a thousand Taffy 3 sailors were killed off Samar that day. And for many of the survivors, the sinking was not the worst part.

**[VISUAL: Men clustered in the water, day turning to night, then to day again. Sharks circling. No rescue in sight.]**

**NARRATOR:** Through a series of mistakes and mix-ups, the rescue ships did not reach them for about two days. Hundreds of men floated in the open Pacific, in the sun, in the dark, some of them taken by sharks, some slipping away from wounds and exhaustion, waiting for help that was slow to come. They were not pulled from the water until the twenty-seventh.

**[VISUAL: Hold on the quiet ocean. Let it sit.]**

**NARRATOR:** That is the real cost of the last stand of Taffy 3.

## ACT 7: THE PROOF ON THE SEAFLOOR (17:30 - 19:15)

**[VISUAL: Color returns, gently. Admiral Nimitz at a desk, reading the after-action report, shaking his head in disbelief.]**

**NARRATOR:** When the reports came in, senior American commanders could barely believe them. Fleet Admiral Chester Nimitz wrote that nothing short of special dispensation from the Lord Almighty could explain how Taffy 3 survived.

**[VISUAL: A simple scoreboard. On one side, the small boys and one carrier, sunk. On the other, Japanese heavy cruisers sunk, and a fleet in retreat.]**

**NARRATOR:** A handful of destroyers and support carriers had traded their lives to stop the most powerful surface fleet Japan had left, and had helped protect the invasion beaches full of soldiers behind them. It should not have worked. It worked.

**[VISUAL: For decades, just an empty patch of ocean labeled with a question mark. A history book. Doubters.]**

**NARRATOR:** For decades, the whole thing sounded almost too heroic to be true. The kind of story that grows in the retelling. And the ships themselves were gone, resting somewhere on the floor of one of the deepest trenches on earth, too deep for anyone to reach.

**[VISUAL: A modern deep-sea submersible descending into pitch black, lights on, sinking past 20,000 feet.]**

**NARRATOR:** And then, in twenty twenty-one, a deep-sea expedition went looking. Miles down, in the crushing dark, they found the wreck of the USS Johnston, Evans's ship, at about twenty-one thousand feet. At the time, it was the deepest shipwreck ever surveyed.

**[VISUAL: The Johnston's hull on the seafloor, guns still trained outward, silt drifting past.]**

**NARRATOR:** Her hull number was still readable on the wreck. Her guns were still trained out toward an enemy that had left almost eighty years earlier. She had gone down fighting, and she had stayed that way in the dark all that time.

**[VISUAL: One year later, another submersible finds a second, even deeper wreck.]**

**NARRATOR:** And the next year, in twenty twenty-two, they found the Samuel B. Roberts, the little destroyer escort, even deeper, at around twenty-two thousand feet. It became the deepest shipwreck ever discovered.

**[VISUAL: Both wrecks side by side on the seafloor, small and dignified in the dark.]**

**NARRATOR:** The two ships that fought the hardest ended up as the deepest wrecks anyone has ever found. And they are the proof. The men who came home told an impossible story, and eighty years later, the ocean floor confirmed every word of it.

## PRODUCTION NOTES

**Fresh angle (per accuracy note):** Told from the deck of the tin cans, the destroyers and destroyer escorts, not the usual carrier-deck retelling. The "small boys" framing is the throughline.

**Voice choices:**
- Comedy stays on the absurd mismatch (destroyers vs the largest battleship ever built), the colored shell-splashes, the empty planes making fake attack runs, and Kurita talking himself out of a win. All documented.
- Fake dialogue used only for verified real quotes (Evans's commissioning speech, Brooks's "meatball flag" report, Copeland's action-report line) or clearly labeled character voice. No invented quotes.
- Human cost (Act 6 and the Copeland loudspeaker beat) played completely straight, jokes fully dropped, tagged TONE: STRAIGHT for the build.

**Accuracy guardrails honored:**
- No single ship "single-handedly" turns back the fleet. Act 4 sets up and Act 5 explicitly corrects this with a "myth vs record" beat: it was combined torpedo runs, air strikes, smoke/weather, and Kurita's confusion.
- Kurita retreating "on the verge of victory" is stated, with the real reasons (believed he faced fleet carriers, lost formation dodging torpedoes, absorbed heavy air pressure).
- All casualty and ship-loss figures hedged with "about" and anchored to 2+ sources.
- Japanese destroyer count given as "around eleven" with the 11 vs 12 discrepancy noted in research.
