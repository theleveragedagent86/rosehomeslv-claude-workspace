Why the recommended wins: "invented" carries the entire thesis in one word and is still defensible, because the claim is about Fuchida's ACCOUNT of the battle, not about the battle happening. It names no one, so the curiosity gap is total. A viewer cannot guess whether "the man" is an admiral, a codebreaker or a writer, and the answer, a pilot who was not flying that day, is the payoff at 4:25. It also reads as a channel-thesis title, which is what this flagship episode is.

- ALT 1 sells the scale of the claim and would likely have the widest cold reach, but it is the least precise. "Lie" is Parshall's word about one passage, and a title that broad invites a comment section arguing about a claim the episode is careful not to make.

## Description

The most quoted eyewitness to the Battle of Midway did not fly that day. He watched from Akagi's bridge, on his feet, days after a surgeon aboard the ship removed his appendix.

Commander Mitsuo Fuchida led the air attack on Pearl Harbor, and in 1955 the US Naval Institute published his account of Midway. It gave the world the image everyone still pictures: Japanese flight decks loaded with planes armed and fueled, five minutes from launching. Japan's own official history, the carriers' air unit records, American B-17 photographs taken that morning, and the American pilot whose bomb killed Akagi all say something different. No attack aircraft were spotted on those flight decks. The strike was still in the hangars, and that is the part that actually killed the ships.

This is the record version of the most important naval battle in American history: what the codebreakers really did, the sixty five minute ordnance reversal, why the torpedo squadrons mattered for a reason nobody puts on a poster, and why fueled planes and loose bombs inside a sealed hangar deck were far more dangerous than a packed flight deck would have been. None of it makes the American victory smaller. The men who flew those dive bombers beat a strong force, and correcting the story takes nothing from them. It is also not a closed argument: Parshall's article drew a full published rebuttal from Martin Bennett, and then a reply. Both are linked below.

## Cropping notes

The episode is a stills-only build at 16:9, and Shorts are 9:16. On previous episodes a center crop could be masked by motion. Here there is none. Only the very first cold-open frame has any movement in the entire episode, so any bad crop reads as a static, obviously chopped image for the full 3 to 7 seconds that frame is held.

Two risk categories, per the brief: frames with ON-SCREEN TEXT near the horizontal edges, and wide frames (maps, full-ship cutaways, side-by-side comparison cards, formations spread across the horizon). Those need regenerating natively at 9:16 rather than cropping.

**Regenerate natively at 9:16, high priority**

- **torpedo-squadrons.** The worst offender of the six. Nearly every frame is composed wide: fifteen TBDs in loose formation over an empty sea, the forward view over a torpedo bomber's nose with four enemy carriers spread across the horizon, roughly thirty Zeros converging seen from a great distance, and forty two vacant parking positions chalked on a flight deck seen from above. A center crop destroys the horizon spread, which is the entire point of every one of those images. Regenerate all of them at 9:16, tighter and taller, and keep them un-stylized and un-graded as the TONE: STRAIGHT tags require.
- **what-was-on-the-decks.** Two frames cannot survive a crop at all. The two-panel MYTH / RECORD comparison card is side by side by construction and must be re-laid out as a stacked vertical, myth on top, record below, with the ON-SCREEN TEXT re-set. The three photographic prints laid out on a lit inspection table is also a horizontal arrangement and needs restacking. The grainy high-altitude reconnaissance frame of a carrier from almost directly above is wide and should be regenerated. The magazine page with the highlighted paragraph and the "Within five minutes" text, and the stopwatch with its six labelled wedges, both carry text that will clip at the sides.
- **hangar-deck.** The full cutaway of a Japanese carrier showing both hangar decks sealed on every side is the most horizontal image in the episode and is unusable cropped. Regenerate as a vertical cutaway, or as a stacked cross-section. The paired hangar interiors, the American hangar with side screens rolled up and the enclosed Japanese hangar, are both wide interiors and should be regenerated at 9:16 so the comparison still reads.

**Regenerate selectively, most frames survive**

- **fuchida-didnt-fly.** Three frames need work: the animated cold-open deck shot (a Zero rolling the length of the deck and lifting off the bow is a horizontal action and does not fit a vertical frame), the straight-down overhead of four carriers in a loose diamond, and the painted packed-deck illustration. The Battleship Row silhouette frame carries "TORA, TORA, TORA" and needs its text re-set inside the safe area. The rest of the short is portraits and interiors, Fuchida's studio portrait, the sick bay, the officer at the bridge window, the writing desk, and all of those center-crop cleanly or even improve vertical.
- **ordnance-shuffle.** One frame must be regenerated: the hangar deck diagram seen from above with torpedo trolleys and bomb carts crossing in opposite directions, which is a wide diagram whose two flows fall outside a center crop. The rest are recoverable, but every ON-SCREEN TEXT timecode in this short, 0715, 0740 and 0820, plus the "OFF. ON. OFF AGAIN." chalkboard card, must be re-positioned into the vertical safe area, and the two SPEECH BUBBLE frames need their bubbles re-anchored so they do not clip.

**Lowest risk**

- **usni-1955.** Almost entirely portrait-shaped source material already: a journal cover, an article title page, a hardback standing on a display stand, a dust jacket author panel. All of these crop to 9:16 well or are natively taller than wide. Only the closing quotation card needs its type re-set for the narrower measure. No regeneration required beyond that card.

# EPISODE SCRIPT: THE MAN WHO INVENTED THE BATTLE OF MIDWAY

**Style:** OverSimplified format. Deadpan comedic history. **STILLS-ONLY BUILD:** a new still image every 3 to 7 seconds, nothing reused, motion only in the very first cold-open shot.
**Story type:** Myth correction. One man's account became the accepted history of the most important naval battle in American history, and the records say he made the centerpiece up.
**Title (standard tier):** `The Man Who Invented The Battle Of Midway | Codename History`

> ACCURACY NOTE FOR PRODUCTION: Every claim in this script traces to research report A (the battle) or report B (Fuchida). Two hard rules. **One:** Fuchida watched from *Akagi*'s **bridge**, not a sickbed. He was grounded and recovering from abdominal surgery. Do not write or draw a hospital bunk. **Two:** the claim is NOT "the decks were empty." The claim is "no full strike was spotted, the attack aircraft were in the hangars, and what was on deck was predominantly fighters." Do not flatten that into a bigger claim than the record supports. Do not use Fuchida's Christian conversion as evidence about his sourcing; it is a separate biographical fact and is not in this script.
>
> VOICE NOTE: The comedy lives in the NARRATION, not just the visuals. Deadpan. Explain a catastrophe like it is the world's dumbest group chat argument. Roast only documented bad decisions. ## PRODUCTION NOTES

**Voice choices**
- Comedy is carried by narration, not visual gags, per channel rule. The ordnance shuffle is played as an office reply-all thread that got out of hand.
- Character-voice lines are used only four times and kept under nine words each so the speech bubbles render legibly: Midway's fake water message, the Japanese intercept operator, Tomonaga's second-strike call, and Nagumo's "is there a carrier or not."

**Accuracy corrections made against the popular telling**
1. Decks were not packed. No full strike was spotted; the attack aircraft were in the hangars; what was on deck was predominantly CAP fighters. Deliberately framed as "not empty, more empty than occupied."
2. Fuchida was on *Akagi*'s bridge, not a sickbed. Script says "on his feet, days after abdominal surgery."
3. Nagumo was aggressive, not indecisive.
4. The AF fresh-water ruse was aimed at Washington, not at Nimitz.
5. Rochefort ran Station Hypo but was not the codebreaker.
6. The torpedo squadrons did not clear the sky by pulling the CAP to sea level. They mattered by denying the Japanese uninterrupted deck time.
7. No Japanese carrier was sunk by an American bomb; all four were scuttled.
8. Gaido and O'Flaherty were killed on 15 June, not on 4 June as the 2019 film shows.
9. The script states out loud that the correction does not diminish the American victory and that the debate remains live (Bennett's published rebuttal and Parshall's reply).
10. The correction rests on three independent legs: *Senshi Sosho*, the *kodochosho* records, and the B-17 photographs. A fourth leg using Lt. Richard Best as an American eyewitness was drafted and then cut at QA loop 1. Do not reinstate it. See the fact-check log.

**Deliberately excluded (report B handling notes)**
- Fuchida's religious life. Not in the script at all, in either direction.
- The claimed Nimitz-to-Fuchida conversation about a third wave. NOT FOUND, excluded.
- Hiroshima. No published challenge exists, so it is not presented as a debunked claim, and it is excluded for runtime.
- The *Missouri* claim. Excluded because at 11 minutes it cannot be given the "unproven, not disproven" caveat it requires. If a longer cut is made, it must carry that caveat and Weidenbach's no-roster statement.
- The *Tora! Tora! Tora!* "exceptional people" scene as character evidence. Excluded per handling note 10.
- The fire-axe detail in the Osmus killing. Excluded, Tier 1 confirmation not found.
