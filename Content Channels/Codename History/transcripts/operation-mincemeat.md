# EPISODE SCRIPT: THE DEAD MAN WHO INVADED EUROPE

**Target runtime:** 15-45 minutes (Deep-dive Operation tier; ~2,700 narration words at ~150 wpm, roughly 18 minutes)
**Style:** OverSimplified format. Comedic animated history. Flat colors, simple characters, exaggerated reactions, fake country dialogue. Comedy on the absurdity; human cost played completely straight.
**Story type:** Single genuinely codenamed operation (real, documented): the 1943 British deception Operation Mincemeat.
**Title (LOCKED, hook-first per package-lock.md):** `The Dead Man Who Fooled the Nazis | Codename History`

> ACCURACY NOTE FOR PRODUCTION: This is semi-famous thanks to Ben Macintyre's 2010 book and the 2021 film. The fresh angle is the straight-played human story of Glyndwr Michael, a real, forgotten, homeless man whose body carried the whole deception, sitting underneath the clever caper. Do NOT repeat the old myth that the body was a willing volunteer or a conveniently unnamed pauper. The record identifies Glyndwr Michael, though some details (whether the poisoning was suicide or accidental, and a later theory that a different body was used) remain debated. Note the identification and its debate honestly. Keep the jokes on the absurdity; keep the facts straight.
>
> VOICE NOTE: The comedy lives in the NARRATION, not just the visuals. Deadpan delivery. The narrator explains a top-secret intelligence operation like it's an elaborate group project that got wildly out of hand. Countries and figures get fake dialogue. The plan gets roasted for how insane it is. Second-person "you" pulls the viewer in. Every beat about Glyndwr Michael himself is played completely straight, quiet and factual, no jokes.

**[VISUAL: Hard cut. A cold London mortuary, three months earlier, early 1943. A sheet-covered body on a slab. Two nervous British officers in suits stand over it, one checking a clipboard.]**

**[VISUAL: The officers dress the corpse in a Royal Marines uniform, tucking a photo of a smiling woman into the breast pocket.]**

**NARRATOR:** They gave the dead man a fake name. A fake rank. A fake fiancee. Fake love letters. A fake overdraft, because nothing says believable British officer like being slightly in debt. An entire fake life, stitched onto a stranger who could not object.

**[VISUAL: A wall of familiar D-Day landing-craft imagery, flat animated. It slides away to reveal one small coat pocket.]**

**NARRATOR:** Everyone knows D-Day. Almost nobody knows that a year before it, the Allies got their first foothold in Europe using the coat pocket of a man nobody claimed.

**[VISUAL: A submarine slides the body into the dark sea off a coastline. Cut to a map of Europe with a giant red question mark over the Mediterranean.]**

**NARRATOR:** Then they dropped him in the ocean and let the Nazis find him. And it worked so well that it may have saved thousands of lives. This is the story of a corpse that went to war. And of the forgotten man who never knew he was in it. And I promise you, every part of this is real.

## ACT 1: THE MOST OBVIOUS INVASION IN HISTORY (1:10 - 3:45)

**[VISUAL: A map of the Mediterranean, early 1943. North Africa glows Allied green. A giant Allied army stands on the coast, cracking its knuckles, looking north.]**

**NARRATOR:** Setup. By early 1943 the Allies had won North Africa. Now they wanted into Europe from the south, and they needed a first stepping stone. So they looked at the map.

**[VISUAL: The camera pans across the Mediterranean and stops, with a spotlight and a ding, on the island of Sicily, sitting right at the toe of Italy.]**

**NARRATOR:** And there was Sicily. Sitting right there. Right between North Africa and Italy. Perfectly placed. Perfectly obvious. The kind of obvious that shows up on a map wearing a neon sign that says invade here.

**[VISUAL: Churchill at a map table, cigar in mouth, pointing flatly at Sicily.]**

**NARRATOR:** Churchill reportedly summed it up. Everyone but a bloody fool, he said, would know it was Sicily.

**[VISUAL: A cartoon German general at his own map table, also pointing at Sicily, nodding smugly.]**

**GERMAN GENERAL (character voice):** "They will attack Sicily."

**[VISUAL: The German general looks directly at the camera.]**

**GERMAN GENERAL (character voice):** "I am not a bloody fool."

**NARRATOR:** And that was the problem. When your next move is that obvious, the enemy fortifies the obvious spot and waits for you to walk into it. The Allies needed to invade the one island everyone knew they'd invade, and somehow convince the Germans to defend everywhere else. Which is a bit like announcing you're robbing the bank on Main Street and getting every guard sent to the suburbs.

**[VISUAL: An amphibious landing craft hitting a heavily fortified beach, waves of defenders waiting. Grim, not comedic. TONE: STRAIGHT briefly.]**

**NARRATOR:** And this was not a game. An amphibious landing against a dug-in, waiting enemy is one of the bloodiest things an army can attempt. If the Germans concentrated their forces on Sicily and were ready, the men wading ashore would pay for it. Every division the Allies could trick away from that beach was lives.

**[VISUAL: The Allied planners staring at the map, sweating, scratching their heads.]**

**NARRATOR:** You cannot un-obvious a giant island. So they were going to have to lie. Big. Not a small fib. Not a called-in-sick, the-dog-ate-it fib. A lie elaborate enough to move armies.

**[VISUAL: A dusty filing cabinet drawer labeled "IDEAS: BAD" creaks open. A single yellowed memo floats out.]**

**NARRATOR:** And here's where it gets ridiculous. The seed of the plan had been sitting in a drawer since the start of the war. A 1939 memo, circulated in Naval Intelligence, brainstorming ways to trick the enemy. It compared deceiving your enemy to fly fishing.

**[VISUAL: The memo, item 28, with a header reading "A Suggestion (not a very nice one)."]**

**NARRATOR:** And tucked inside was item 28, helpfully titled, quote, "A Suggestion. Not a very nice one." The suggestion? Plant fake documents on a dead body and let the enemy find it.

**[VISUAL: The two officers from the cold open read the memo, look at each other, and slowly grin.]**

**NARRATOR:** For four years, that suggestion sat in the drawer, too grim and too weird for anyone to touch. In 1943, two men finally were desperate enough. And the smallest object in this entire story, something you could lose in a blink, is what would eventually tell them whether it had worked. Hold that thought.

## ACT 2: TWO MEN AND A VERY BAD IDEA (3:45 - 6:40)

**[VISUAL: Two British officers stand side by side. One in a naval uniform, one in RAF blue.]**

**NARRATOR:** Meet the team. Lieutenant Commander Ewen Montagu, Royal Navy, a barrister in real life, sitting on a secret intelligence committee. And Flight Lieutenant Charles Cholmondeley, an RAF officer working with MI5.

**[VISUAL: A conference room door with a plaque reading "THE TWENTY COMMITTEE - XX." The XX morphs into two crossed-out figures.]**

**NARRATOR:** They worked for a body called the Twenty Committee. Twenty, as in the Roman numeral XX, as in double cross. Yes, the British spy committee named itself after a pun, and then went home feeling clever about it. This is the level we are operating at.

**[VISUAL: Cholmondeley at a chalkboard, sketching a stick figure lying flat, with an arrow to a swastika-marked coastline.]**

**NARRATOR:** Cholmondeley dusted off the corpse idea. Montagu refined it into an actual operation. The concept was simple and completely unhinged. Take a dead body. Turn it into a fake officer carrying fake secret invasion plans. Drop it where the enemy will find it, read the plans, and believe them.

**NARRATOR:** Think about how many ways that fails. The body has to look like it died the right way. The identity has to survive an enemy who checks. The enemy has to find the papers, believe the papers, and never once suspect that the whole thing was staged for their benefit. It is a magic trick where the audience is the German high command, and if they spot the trick, they now know exactly where you're really going. Most magicians who blow the trick lose a tip. These two would lose Sicily. The downside was not just failure. The downside was gift-wrapping your real plan and mailing it to the enemy.

**[VISUAL: A checklist appears titled "THINGS WE NEED." Line one: "A dead body (fresh-ish)."]**

**NARRATOR:** Which raised one small logistical question. Where do you get a body?

**[VISUAL: The tone quiets. Muted colors. A grey London street in winter. A thin, ragged man alone, no name tag, no fanfare. TONE: STRAIGHT.]**

**NARRATOR:** In late January 1943, a man died in London. His name was Glyndwr Michael. He was Welsh, born in Aberbargoed, the son of a coal-country family. His father had died. His mother had died. He had drifted to the city with no money, no home, and no one, and he was living on the streets.

**[VISUAL: Still muted. A crust of bread on a warehouse floor near King's Cross. No jokes here. TONE: STRAIGHT.]**

**NARRATOR:** He died after swallowing rat poison that contained phosphorus. Whether he took it deliberately, or ate poison-laced bread because he was starving, the records do not tell us for certain. He was thirty-four years old. He died with almost nothing, and almost no one to notice.

**[VISUAL: The colors stay muted a beat longer, then a coroner's office, plain and quiet. A coroner signs a form. TONE: STRAIGHT ends here.]**

**NARRATOR:** His body went to a coroner named Bentley Purchase. And the intelligence men needed exactly what Glyndwr Michael tragically was. Unclaimed, unmissed, and available. A pathologist, Sir Bernard Spilsbury, advised that a body like his could pass for a man who had died at sea.

**[VISUAL: The two officers gently lift the sheet. Neutral, respectful, not comedic.]**

**NARRATOR:** So a homeless man who died alone and forgotten was about to become the most important officer in the British military. He just wouldn't know it. Nobody would, for over fifty years. First, though, they had to build him a life. Every piece of it, down to the woman who loved him.

## ACT 3: BUILDING A MAN WHO NEVER EXISTED (6:40 - 9:55)

**[VISUAL: The tone lifts back to comedic. A recruitment-style poster reading "MAJOR WILLIAM MARTIN, ROYAL MARINES," with the corpse's silhouette saluting.]**

**NARRATOR:** First, the identity. The dead man became Captain, acting Major, William Martin, of the Royal Marines. Why Martin? Partly because there were already several real Marine officers named Martin, so if anyone checked a list, the name would look plausibly real. Hide the fake officer in a crowd of real ones. Attention to detail. This entire operation is one long, unbroken flex of attention to detail.

**[VISUAL: A tailor's dummy being fitted with a Royal Marines uniform. A close-up on the underwear tag.]**

**NARRATOR:** They dressed him in a full uniform. And here's the insane part. They obsessed over things nobody was supposed to see. Underwear tags. Because in the mind of a British intelligence officer, the Germans might just check. And if one detail was wrong, the whole lie collapsed.

**[VISUAL: An identity card being stamped. The photo on it is of a living, breathing man, clearly not the corpse.]**

**NARRATOR:** They needed an ID photo, but you cannot photograph a dead man and have him look alive. Dead faces do not photograph like sleeping ones. So they found a living MI5 officer who happened to resemble him, and put that man's face on the card instead. Somewhere in Britain, an officer's passport photo went off to war without him.

**[VISUAL: A desk covered in tiny props. Theatre ticket stubs, keys, a religious medal, receipts, a book of stamps.]**

**NARRATOR:** Then came the wallet litter. The little pieces of a life. Theatre ticket stubs, so it looked like he'd been out on the town. A book of stamps. Keys. A silver medal. Receipts. A bill from his gentleman's club.

**[VISUAL: A receipt for a diamond engagement ring. Price tag: fifty-three pounds, ten shillings, sixpence.]**

**NARRATOR:** A receipt for a diamond engagement ring, fifty-three pounds, ten shillings and sixpence, because Major Martin was in love.

**[VISUAL: A stern bank letter about an overdraft. Amount: seventy-nine pounds, nineteen shillings, twopence. The corpse's silhouette shrugs sheepishly.]**

**NARRATOR:** And a letter from his bank complaining about his overdraft, seventy-nine pounds and change, because Major Martin, like all the best officers, was also slightly broke. This dead stranger now had debt, romance, and a night at the theatre. Which, honestly, is a fuller social calendar than most of us manage while alive.

**[VISUAL: Two handwritten love letters, hearts doodled in the margins, signed "Pam."]**

**NARRATOR:** They gave him a fiancee. Her name was Pam. They wrote him two loving letters from her, gushing and worried and real, the kind you'd actually keep in your coat.

**[VISUAL: A photograph of a smiling young woman. A caption notes she was a real intelligence-service clerk who lent her picture.]**

**NARRATOR:** And they needed a photo of Pam. So a real clerk from the intelligence services volunteered her own photograph to be the fake fiancee of a dead man she had never met. Sure. Normal Tuesday.

**[VISUAL: A briefcase. Inside, official-looking sealed letters marked "SECRET."]**

**NARRATOR:** Now the actual point of all this. The secret documents. This is what the Germans were really meant to find, and everything else, the fiancee, the overdraft, the theatre tickets, existed only to make these letters believable.

**[VISUAL: A forged letter, general to general, with the words "GREECE" and "SARDINIA" underlined, and a small "(Sicily = feint)" scribbled in the margin.]**

**NARRATOR:** The main letter was written to look like private correspondence between two British generals. And casually, between other business, it revealed the big secret. The Allies were going to invade Greece and Sardinia. And Sicily? Sicily was just a decoy. A feint. A distraction.

**[VISUAL: A second letter from a senior officer, introducing "Major Martin." At the bottom, a labored joke about sardines.]**

**NARRATOR:** A second letter, from a senior commander, introduced Major Martin as an expert, and worked in a pun about sardines. To nudge the Germans, very subtly, toward Sardinia. Subtle like an elbow to the ribs. Yes. The fate of thousands of soldiers partly rode on a sardine joke, and somehow that is the part that worked.

**[VISUAL: An officer carefully placing a single eyelash inside a folded letter, then resealing it.]**

**NARRATOR:** And here is that smallest object I told you to hold on to. The detail I cannot get over. They slipped a single eyelash inside one folded letter before sealing it. So that later, if the eyelash was gone, they'd know the Germans had opened it. An eyelash. As a tripwire. Not a wax seal, not a code. A single eyelash, deployed as counterintelligence. These people thought of everything, and then thought of a few things nobody asked for.

**[VISUAL: The briefcase is chained to the corpse's coat. Cut to a steel canister packed with dry ice, lid closing.]**

**NARRATOR:** The documents were secured to the body. Major William Martin was sealed into a canister packed with dry ice to keep him from decaying. And Major Martin, who was really Glyndwr Michael, who never signed up for any of this, was ready to ship out. From here on, the British controlled nothing. It came down to one submarine, one Spanish beach, and a country that was neutral on paper only.

## ACT 4: A SUBMARINE, A CORPSE, AND A PSALM (9:55 - 12:25)

**[VISUAL: The submarine HMS Seraph at a Scottish dock, the canister being carefully loaded aboard.]**

**NARRATOR:** The delivery vehicle was the submarine HMS Seraph. The target was Huelva, on the coast of Spain.

**[VISUAL: A map. Spain sits neutral in the middle. A little Nazi spy figure lurks near Huelva with a magnifying glass.]**

**NARRATOR:** Why Spain? Because Spain was neutral, but not exactly friendly. It was neutral the way a referee who bets on one team is neutral. There was an active German spy network operating there, and Spanish officials who leaned toward Germany. If a dead British officer with secret papers washed up near Huelva, the odds were excellent those papers would quietly make their way to German intelligence. Spain was the perfect leaky middleman. The British were counting on someone snooping through a dead man's briefcase, which is not a sentence you get to say most weeks.

**[VISUAL: The Seraph submerged, cruising south. A calendar flips toward late April 1943.]**

**NARRATOR:** The Seraph carried its strange cargo south. And in the early hours of the thirtieth of April, 1943, it surfaced quietly off Huelva. Around a quarter past four in the morning.

**[VISUAL: The submarine's commander and officers on deck in the dark. The crew sent below. The canister opened.]**

**NARRATOR:** The commander, Bill Jewell, sent the crew below. Only the officers stayed on deck. They didn't need the whole crew knowing they were carrying a body. They opened the canister and lifted out Major Martin.

**[VISUAL: The officers lower the body gently into the black water. TONE: STRAIGHT.]**

**NARRATOR:** And then something quietly human happened. Before they let him go, the commander read aloud part of Psalm 39. A burial. A real one, for a real dead man, spoken over the side of a submarine, in the dark, before setting him adrift as a weapon. Whatever else this was, they did not just dump him.

**[VISUAL: The body drifts toward the distant shore. The submarine slips back beneath the surface.]**

**NARRATOR:** They eased the body toward the beach and slipped away. Now everything depended on strangers. Spanish fishermen. Spanish officials. German spies. The British had built the perfect lie. They had no control at all over whether anyone would believe it.

**[VISUAL: A Spanish sardine boat at dawn. Fishermen spot something floating and haul it aboard.]**

**NARRATOR:** That same morning, Spanish fishermen found the body and brought it ashore. A British officer, drowned, apparently from a plane crash at sea, with a briefcase chained to him. Exactly as designed.

**[VISUAL: A row of question marks. The briefcase sits on a table, everyone staring at it.]**

**NARRATOR:** And now the British had to wait. Would the Spanish hand the documents to the Germans? Would the Germans open them? Would they read that eyelash-guarded letter? And most of all, would anyone actually believe that Britain would be dumb enough to send its top secret invasion plans across the sea, chained to one guy? Because that was the gamble. The plan only worked if the enemy assumed the British were too incompetent to run the exact plan the British were running.

**[VISUAL: A British intelligence office. Officers hovering by a radio, chewing their nails.]**

**NARRATOR:** For days, they heard nothing. And then the messages started coming in.

## ACT 5: THE NAZIS SWALLOW IT WHOLE (12:25 - 15:25)

**[VISUAL: The briefcase, now open. A close-up on a folded letter. The eyelash is gone.]**

**NARRATOR:** The documents came back to the British through official channels. Returned politely, as if untouched, with a straight face and everything. But the British checked their tripwire. The eyelash was gone. The letters had been opened, read, and resealed. Somebody had looked. They had caught German intelligence with an eyelash. Which had to be a strange thing to feel proud of.

**[VISUAL: A Nazi intelligence office. German officers passing the letters around, nodding gravely, circling "GREECE" on a map.]**

**NARRATOR:** And "somebody" was exactly who they'd hoped. German intelligence got the documents, studied them hard, and reached the conclusion the British had spent weeks manufacturing for them to reach. The Allies were going to hit Greece and Sardinia. Sicily was a bluff. They did not just take the bait. They took the bait, complimented the chef, and asked for the recipe.

**[VISUAL: A telegram lands on Churchill's desk. It reads: "MINCEMEAT SWALLOWED ROD, LINE AND SINKER."]**

**NARRATOR:** Back in London, an officer sent a signal to Churchill. Mincemeat, he wrote, had been swallowed rod, line, and sinker by the right people, and they looked like they were going to act on it. The fish had taken the bait.

**[VISUAL: A map of the Mediterranean. Little German units, labeled with divisions, start sliding away from Sicily toward Greece and Sardinia.]**

**NARRATOR:** And act on it they did. German commanders began moving reinforcements. Units shifted toward Greece. A panzer division redirected toward the eastern Mediterranean. The garrison on Sardinia was strengthened. Defenses spread out across the exact places the dead man's letters had pointed.

**[VISUAL: Sicily, meanwhile, sits comparatively lightly defended, a tumbleweed rolling past a bored German sentry.]**

**NARRATOR:** And Sicily, the actual target, the "everyone but a bloody fool" island, got less attention than it should have. The one general who was not a bloody fool had been carefully turned into one, using a corpse and a sardine pun. The Germans were now bracing for punches that were never coming, in places the Allies were never going.

**NARRATOR:** And here is the strange, brutal beauty of it. The best deception is not the one where the enemy fails to prepare. It's the one where the enemy prepares hard, works hard, moves whole divisions, and does all of it in the wrong place. The Germans were not lazy. They were busy. They were just busy defending Greece and Sardinia against an invasion that lived only inside a dead man's coat.

**[VISUAL: 9 July 1943. A massive Allied invasion fleet appears off Sicily at dawn.]**

**NARRATOR:** On the ninth of July, 1943, the Allies invaded Sicily for real. Operation Husky. The largest amphibious assault yet mounted in the war, landing on the island the enemy had been told to ignore.

**[VISUAL: Allied troops coming ashore. The defense is real but thinner than the Germans could have made it.]**

**NARRATOR:** The landings still faced hard fighting, this was a real war, not a magic trick. But the enemy had been pulled off balance, spread thin, looking the wrong way. The deception had done its job.

**[VISUAL: A quote card, plain and serious: Sir Michael Howard's line about the operation. TONE: neutral, factual.]**

**NARRATOR:** The historian Sir Michael Howard later called Mincemeat the best known, and perhaps the most successful, single deception operation of the entire war. Not because it won the war by itself. It didn't. It was one piece of a much larger campaign of lies. But few deceptions ever fooled the enemy so completely.

**[VISUAL: The corpse's silhouette, still lying quietly in a grave in Huelva, while the battle rages far away.]**

**NARRATOR:** And through all of it, the man at the center of the greatest trick of the war lay in a grave in Spain, under a name that was not his, while the world had no idea who he really was.

## ACT 6: THE MAN WHO NEVER WAS (15:25 - 17:55)

**[VISUAL: A quiet cemetery in Huelva, Spain. A simple headstone reading "WILLIAM MARTIN." TONE: STRAIGHT begins.]**

**NARRATOR:** For decades, that was the end of the story. Major William Martin was buried in Huelva with military honours. A fake officer, mourned by nobody who knew him, because there was no him to know.

**[VISUAL: A shelf of postwar books and a 1950s film poster, all about the clever plan. The dead man is a footnote, faceless.]**

**NARRATOR:** After the war, the story came out, and people loved it. The genius plan. The corpse that fooled Hitler. Books, a film, a legend. But in the retelling, a myth crept in. The body was described as a willing volunteer. Or as some nameless nobody it was fine to use, a man conveniently without a story.

**[VISUAL: The words on screen: "WILLING VOLUNTEER" and "NAMELESS NOBODY," both stamped with a red "MYTH."]**

**NARRATOR:** Both of those are false. And this is the part the channel refuses to skip. He did not volunteer. He never agreed to any of it. And he was not a nobody. He had a name.

**[VISUAL: An archive room in 1996. A researcher pulls a file, and a name comes into focus: GLYNDWR MICHAEL. TONE: STRAIGHT.]**

**NARRATOR:** In 1996, a researcher named Roger Morgan went digging in the public records, and found the answer that had been hidden for over fifty years. The body was Glyndwr Michael. The homeless Welshman. The man who had died alone in London with nothing.

**[VISUAL: The Huelva headstone again. A new line is carefully added at the bottom.]**

**NARRATOR:** And so, in 1997, the truth was finally carved into the grave. Beneath the name Major William Martin, the Commonwealth War Graves Commission added a line. Glyndwr Michael served as Major William Martin.

**[VISUAL: The full headstone, both names now visible together, quiet and dignified.]**

**NARRATOR:** After fifty-four years, the man who never was, finally, was.

**[VISUAL: A balanced text card: "GLYNDWR MICHAEL" with two small honest footnotes beside it.]**

**NARRATOR:** And to be honest with you, because that's the whole point here, a couple of things about him are still debated. Whether the poison was suicide or a starving accident, the record does not settle. And a few researchers have argued a different body was used entirely, though most historians reject that and the official records point to Michael. We tell you the debate exists, and we tell you where the evidence leads. That's the deal.

**[VISUAL: Glyndwr Michael's silhouette, standing upright now, dignified, no uniform, just a man.]**

**NARRATOR:** But strip away the arguments, and here is what is certain. A poor, forgotten man, who mattered to almost no one in life, ended up shaping one of the biggest battles of the Second World War. He never knew. He never chose it. But his body helped pull enemy divisions off a beach, and that may have meant soldiers who lived to go home. The man the world overlooked did more than most of the men who overlooked him.

## PRODUCTION NOTES

**Voice choices:**
- Comedy lives in the narration (the sardine joke, the eyelash tripwire, the "normal Tuesday" clerk beat), not just visual gags. Deadpan throughout the caper.
- Fake country/figure dialogue kept short and dumb (the German general "I am not a bloody fool" bit; the pun payoff).
- Second-person "you" used to pull the viewer in on the tension beats.
- Every act ends on a hook or cliffhanger ("Hold that thought," "the messages started coming in," "and finally was").
- Human-cost beats (Glyndwr Michael's life and death, the Psalm 39 burial, the final act) are played completely straight and marked TONE: STRAIGHT for the shotlist agent.

**Accuracy corrections (see fact-check log):**
- The "willing volunteer" and "nameless pauper" myths are explicitly corrected on screen, per the brief.
- Death-date left as "late January 1943" because sources disagree (24 vs 28 January).
- Churchill's "bloody fool" line framed as "reportedly," since it is a widely attributed but not primary-documented quote.
- German troop movements described as reinforcements diverted toward Greece and Sardinia rather than a precise division ledger, because source counts vary.
- The suicide-vs-accident question and the alternative-body (HMS Dasher) theory are both flagged honestly in Act 6.
