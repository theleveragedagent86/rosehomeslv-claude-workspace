## ACT 1: THE WORST DELIVERY BOY IN GERMANY (0:40 - 2:15)

**[VISUAL: Prussian manor, boy with hunting rifle and a tiny deer trophy.]** (frame: prussia_boy)

**NARRATOR:** Manfred von Richthofen was born in 1892 into Prussian nobility, which meant his childhood was basically three activities: riding horses, hunting things, and being told he was better than everyone. He was extremely good at all three.

**[VISUAL: Young Manfred in cavalry uniform on a proud horse, lance in hand.]** (frame: cavalry_glory)

**NARRATOR:** So when the war started in 1914, he rode off as a cavalry officer. Lance. Sabre. Glory.

**[VISUAL: Same rider, now facing trenches, barbed wire, a machine gun nest. The horse looks concerned.]** (frame: cavalry_trench)

**NARRATOR:** There was just one problem. This war had machine guns, barbed wire, and hundreds of miles of trenches. Which meant the cavalry, the most prestigious branch of the German army, was now completely useless.

**[VISUAL: Manfred, dismounted, sulking next to a cheese wheel and an egg crate. Sign: "SUPPLY DUTY."]** (frame: supply_duty)

**NARRATOR:** Manfred got shuffled into the army's supply branch. The future deadliest pilot in history spent early 1915 behind the lines, handling logistics. So he wrote a letter to his commanding general. And this letter is real, and it is fantastic.

**[VISUAL: The letter, wax seal, one line highlighted.]** (frame: letter)

**NARRATOR:** Quote: "I have not gone to war in order to collect cheese and eggs, but for another purpose." That other purpose being, and I am paraphrasing here, the exact opposite of cheese.

**[VISUAL: An aeroplane passes overhead. Manfred points up, eyes sparkling.]** (frame: looks_up)

**NARRATOR:** The army, incredibly, said yes. In May 1915, Richthofen transferred to the flying service. And within months, he would meet the man who would turn him into a legend. Right before dying in front of him.

## ACT 2: THE APPRENTICE (2:15 - 3:55)

**[VISUAL: A train dining car. Manfred, starstruck, leaning toward Oswald Boelcke: composed, Blue Max at his neck, coffee in hand.]** (frame: train_meeting)

**NARRATOR:** In October 1915, Manfred crossed paths with Oswald Boelcke, Germany's first great ace and the most famous pilot alive. Manfred asked him, essentially, "how do you shoot them down?" Boelcke's answer was basically: get close, aim well, don't miss. Revolutionary stuff.

**[VISUAL: Manfred crashing a training plane nose-first into a field. Instructor facepalms.]** (frame: training_crash)

**NARRATOR:** Inspired, Manfred trained as a pilot. And crashed on his very first solo flight. Keep that in mind the next time you feel bad about learning something new.

**[VISUAL: Boelcke pointing at Manfred in a lineup of hopeful pilots. "YOU."]** (frame: boelcke_picks)

**NARRATOR:** But Boelcke saw something in him. In 1916 he hand-picked Richthofen for his elite new fighter squadron. Boelcke had written the Dicta Boelcke, the first rulebook of air combat. Attack with the sun behind you. Fire only at close range. Never get lured into a dumb chase. Manfred didn't just learn the rules. He worshipped them.

**[VISUAL: A downed British plane smoking in a distant field. Manfred holds a Berlin jeweler's catalog: "SILVER CUP."]** (frame: first_kill_cup)

**NARRATOR:** On September 17th, 1916, Richthofen scored his first confirmed kill. And then he did something extremely Manfred. He ordered an engraved silver trophy cup from a jeweler in Berlin to commemorate it. One cup. Per kill. Hold that thought, because this jeweler is about to have a very busy year.

**[VISUAL: Boelcke's funeral. Rows of pilots, heads bowed, coffin with an Iron Cross. TONE: STRAIGHT.]** (frame: boelcke_funeral)

**NARRATOR:** Then, that October, Boelcke died. Not shot down. His wing clipped a friendly plane mid-fight, in a war where fighter pilots were not given parachutes. He was twenty-five years old. Germany went into mourning. And his student made a quiet decision: if talent could die by accident, then discipline would have to be perfect.

## ACT 3: THE RED PLANE (3:55 - 6:00)

**[VISUAL: Two biplanes circling each other on a dotted spiral path.]** (frame: hawker_duel)

**NARRATOR:** A month later, Richthofen proved he was the heir. He fought a long, spiraling duel with Major Lanoe Hawker, Britain's first fighter ace, a Victoria Cross winner, a man Richthofen himself called "the British Boelcke." Richthofen won. That made eleven.

**[VISUAL: Hangar. A mechanic stares in horror at an Albatros painted BRIGHT RED. Tiny sample of sensible green paint on the floor.]** (frame: red_paint_hangar)

**NARRATOR:** And in January 1917, with a squadron of his own to command, Manfred von Richthofen did the single most metal thing of the entire war. In an age when every pilot desperately tried to be invisible, he painted his plane bright, blazing, unmissable red.

**[VISUAL: British trench. Two soldiers staring up in terror, one pointing: "THAT'S HIM."]** (frame: trench_thats_him)

**NARRATOR:** This was not camouflage. This was an announcement. I am here. You can all see me. It will not help you.

**[VISUAL: A line of red-painted fighters, pilots adding paint with brushes.]** (frame: red_squadron)

**NARRATOR:** His pilots painted their planes red too. Partly style, partly so their boss wasn't the only giant target in the sky. The British started calling him the Red Baron. The name has now outlived everyone involved.

**[VISUAL: Calendar page: APRIL 1917, dripping red, covered in kill tallies. TONE: STRAIGHT for the second half.]** (frame: bloody_april)

**NARRATOR:** Then came April 1917. The month the British would name Bloody April. Richthofen shot down twenty-two aircraft in thirty days, four of them in a single day. And on the other side of those numbers: new British pilots were arriving at the front with barely any flying hours, and that month, a new pilot's life expectancy was measured in weeks. The Red Baron was the best pilot in a sky full of beginners.

**[VISUAL: The Berlin jeweler buried in cups, holding No. 60. Sign: "OUT OF SILVER."]** (frame: jeweler)

**NARRATOR:** Remember the cups? By 1917 he had sixty of them. Then blockaded Germany ran out of silver. The jeweler offered to keep going in cheap substitute metal, and Manfred, a man of standards, simply stopped collecting. The kills continued. The cups did not.

**[VISUAL: Colorful fighters over circus tents on rail flatcars.]** (frame: flying_circus)

**NARRATOR:** By that summer he commanded Jagdgeschwader One: four squadrons packed into tents and trains, moving up and down the front, every plane painted like candy. The Allies called it the Flying Circus. It was the most dangerous circus on Earth.

**[VISUAL: Slow zoom on a calendar: JULY 6, 1917. "THE DAY IT ENDED."]** (frame: july_calendar)

**NARRATOR:** He was twenty-five, famous, and precisely on schedule to survive the war. Then came July 6th, 1917. The day the Red Baron's story secretly ended, ten months before his death.

## ACT 4: THE CRACK (6:00 - 7:30)

**[VISUAL: A British two-seater's tracer clips the red pilot's head. Impact star. TONE: STRAIGHT.]** (frame: fe2d_hit)

**NARRATOR:** In a fight with British two-seaters, a bullet grazed Richthofen's skull. Instantly, he couldn't see. Blind, at altitude, in a plane with no parachute.

**[VISUAL: Near-white frame, faint cockpit outline. TONE: STRAIGHT.]** (frame: blind_white)

**NARRATOR:** His sight came back just in time to pull out and crash-land in a field. He survived. But something was different now.

**[VISUAL: Hospital. Head bandaged. Doctor holds a chart: "DO NOT FLY."]** (frame: hospital)

**NARRATOR:** The wound never really healed. Splitting headaches. Nausea after every flight. People around him said he came back changed: quieter, darker, colder. His doctors told him to stop flying. He was back in a cockpit in nineteen days.

**[VISUAL: Officials sliding a paper marked "SAFE DESK JOB" across a table. Manfred, arms crossed.]** (frame: desk_refuse)

**NARRATOR:** Here's the thing. Germany knew he was worth more alive than any squadron. He was the most famous man in the country after the Kaiser. They begged him to retire and train pilots. He refused, over and over, saying he could not sit safe while every ordinary soldier in the trenches had no choice. Whatever else he was, he meant that.

**[VISUAL: Manfred at a desk at night, candle, crossed-out pages. TONE: STRAIGHT.]** (frame: memoir_night)

**NARRATOR:** He'd written a cocky autobiography in 1917, because the propaganda office wanted one. Later, privately, he took it back, writing that the book was too insolent, that he wasn't that person anymore. And then this line: "I am in wretched spirits after every aerial combat."

**[VISUAL: Giant "80. CONFIRMED VICTORIES."]** (frame: score_80)

**NARRATOR:** The man in the red plane kept flying anyway. By April 1918, his score stood at eighty confirmed victories. The highest of the entire war. Nobody ever beat it.

**[VISUAL: The manual page again. Slow zoom on the circled rule.]** (frame: manual_rule)

**NARRATOR:** Which brings us back to that morning over the Somme, and the question this whole story hangs on. Why did the most disciplined pilot alive break his own rule?

## ACT 5: SIXTY SECONDS (7:30 - 9:10)

**[VISUAL: Morning sky tangled with Camels and triplanes. TONE: STRAIGHT from here through the act.]** (frame: morning_dogfight)

**NARRATOR:** That Sunday morning, April 21st, 1918, the Flying Circus collided with Sopwith Camels of the RAF's 209 Squadron. In the middle of it: a Canadian named Wilfrid May, on his very first combat patrol, under strict orders to stay out of the fight. His guns jammed. He ran for home.

**[VISUAL: May's Camel, guns clicking uselessly. "GUNS: NO."]** (frame: may_flee)

**NARRATOR:** And out of every target in that sky, the Red Baron picked the helpless one. A textbook easy kill. Except this time, when one clean pass didn't finish it, he followed.

**[VISUAL: The red triplane locked behind the fleeing Camel at treetop height.]** (frame: chase_low)

**NARRATOR:** Down to the deck. Across the river Somme. Over Australian infantry lines. And an unusual east wind, the exact reverse of the normal weather, was quietly shoving the whole chase deeper and deeper into Allied territory, faster than he could realize.

**[VISUAL: A Camel diving steeply from above and behind: Roy Brown. "ONE BURST."]** (frame: brown_dive)

**NARRATOR:** May's squadron mate, and his old school friend from Edmonton, Captain Roy Brown, dove in to save him. He fired one short burst at the red triplane, then had to pull up hard or hit the ground. Remember that burst. The whole mystery hangs on it.

**[VISUAL: The ridge. Australian gunners swinging up, tracers converging on the low red plane.]** (frame: ground_gunners)

**NARRATOR:** Because the chase kept going. Along Morlancourt Ridge, every Australian gun in range opened fire on the lowest, slowest, most famous red aircraft in the world.

**[VISUAL: The triplane settling into a rough but controlled landing in a beet field. Soldiers running toward it. TONE: STRAIGHT.]** (frame: plane_lands)

**NARRATOR:** One bullet hit him. One. It came up through his chest from the low right and tore everything on the way. And somehow, dying, he put the plane down in a field, mostly intact. Australian soldiers reached the cockpit within moments. By some accounts he managed one last word: "kaputt." Manfred von Richthofen was dead at twenty-five.

**[VISUAL: The triplane reduced to a skeleton as soldiers carry off red fabric. "GONE IN HOURS."]** (frame: souvenir_strip)

**NARRATOR:** His plane was gone within hours, stripped to the bones by souvenir hunters. The most feared machine of the war ended up as pocket souvenirs. And then the argument started.

## ACT 6: THE WRONG MAN GOT THE CREDIT (9:10 - 10:30)

**[VISUAL: "OFFICIAL CREDIT: CAPT. BROWN." Brown looking deeply uncomfortable.]** (frame: brown_credit)

**NARRATOR:** The Royal Air Force officially credited Roy Brown with killing the Red Baron. One problem. Actually, three problems.

**[VISUAL: Blueprint diagram: Brown's attack line from above-behind-left, big X. The wound path from low right, rising, big check.]** (frame: diagram)

**NARRATOR:** Problem one: geometry. Brown attacked from above, behind, and the left. The bullet that killed Richthofen entered low on the right side of his chest and traveled upward. Those two lines don't meet.

**[VISUAL: Timeline: "BROWN FIRES" ... "~2 MORE MINUTES OF FLYING" ... "FATAL SHOT." "THE WOUND ALLOWS SECONDS."]** (frame: two_minutes)

**NARRATOR:** Problem two: timing. After Brown's burst, the Baron kept flying, and chasing, for roughly two more minutes. Doctors who have studied the wound agree on the brutal math. A man hit like that has seconds of useful consciousness. Maybe a minute. Not two.

**[VISUAL: Sergeant Popkin behind his Vickers gun, tracking the banking triplane, its right side exposed.]** (frame: popkin_vickers)

**NARRATOR:** Problem three: there was a man whose position fits. As the triplane banked over the ridge, its right side swung open to Sergeant Cedric Popkin, an Australian machine gunner firing from the ground. A century of reconstructions, from forensic pathologists to battlefield surveys, keeps landing on him as the most likely shooter. The deadliest fighter pilot of the First World War was almost certainly killed by an infantryman. Brown, to his credit, never once bragged about it, and barely spoke of that day for the rest of his life.

**[VISUAL: Map: front line, big east-wind arrows pushing the tiny red plane across it. "UNUSUAL EAST WIND."]** (frame: wind_map)

**NARRATOR:** So why was he even there to be shot? Part of the answer is that strange east wind, dragging the fight over Allied lines. And part of it may be darker. Modern researchers who study brain-injured pilots point straight at that 1917 head wound: the kind of damage that erodes judgment and locks a hunter onto his prey. Target fixation, in the one man who taught the world never to fixate.

**[VISUAL: The manual rule, ghosted over the final chase. TONE: STRAIGHT.]** (frame: manual_rule)

**NARRATOR:** The Red Baron didn't just die that morning. First, for sixty seconds, he stopped being the Red Baron.
