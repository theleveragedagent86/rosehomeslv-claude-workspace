Clark County Commission Unanimously Approves Switch Data Center Expansion in Southwest Las Vegas

On June 17, 2026, the Clark County Commission voted to approve a new Switch data center in southwest Las Vegas. The vote was unanimous. The project is called "LAS 19." It sits on about nine acres along Warm Springs Road, just west of Decatur Boulevard. The new building is around 57,000 square feet. It will hold what Switch calls EVO AI Factories, which are being built in partnership with the chip company Nvidia.

This approval is a big deal for the southwest valley. Data centers are large buildings full of computers. They use a lot of electricity, and many of them use a lot of water for cooling. Switch says this new facility uses a closed-loop, water-free dry-cooling system. That means it should not draw heavily on the water supply the way some older data centers do. Still, many people who live nearby spoke out against the project before the vote.

To answer the question many homeowners are asking right away: yes, the project was approved, and yes, it is moving forward. But the commission did not give Switch a blank check. The commissioners attached a condition. Switch must help design and build a pedestrian safety barrier along Warm Springs Road. That condition shows the county heard at least some of the worries from the public. In this post, we will walk through what happened, why it matters to people who own homes in Las Vegas, the history behind the data center boom here, and what you can do if you care about how your neighborhood grows.

What Happened

The Clark County Commission met on June 17, 2026. One of the items on its agenda was the Switch LAS 19 data center expansion. Switch is a well-known company based in the Las Vegas area. It runs some of the largest data centers in the world. The LAS 19 project would add a roughly 57,000-square-foot building on a piece of land of about nine acres. The site is along Warm Springs Road, just west of Decatur Boulevard, in unincorporated Clark County in the southwest part of the valley.

The building is meant to house EVO AI Factories. These are special computer systems built for artificial intelligence work. Switch is building them in partnership with Nvidia, the company that makes many of the powerful chips used to train and run AI programs. An "AI factory" is a way of describing a data center that is set up mainly to do heavy AI computing, not just to store files or host websites.

One of the biggest worries about data centers is how much water they use. Many large data centers use water to keep their machines cool. In a desert like Las Vegas, that worry is real. Switch told the county that LAS 19 uses a closed-loop, water-free dry-cooling system. In simple terms, that means the cooling system reuses the same fluid over and over in a sealed loop and does not need a steady supply of fresh water. This was likely a key point that helped the project win support.

The vote was unanimous, meaning every commissioner who voted said yes. But the approval did not happen without pushback. People who live in the area showed up to speak against the project. Some worried about traffic and safety. Some worried about how much power the building would draw. Some simply did not want a large industrial building near their homes. In response, the commissioners added a condition to the approval. Switch must help design and build a pedestrian safety barrier along Warm Springs Road. That means Switch is not just building its facility. It also has to help make the road safer for people walking nearby.

The approval is also a signal. It shows that the data center buildout in the Las Vegas area is still going strong, even as more residents push for limits on how much power and water these buildings can use. Commissioner Tick Segerblom said the county should think about creating a future data center ordinance, which is a local law that would set rules for these projects. He put it plainly when he said, "I don't think we can sustain this very long." That comment from a sitting commissioner is a sign that even the people approving these projects are starting to ask hard questions.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

If you own a home in the southwest valley, this news touches you directly. The area near Warm Springs Road and Decatur Boulevard is a mix of homes, businesses, and open land. When a large building like a data center goes in, it changes the feel of the area. It can change traffic patterns. It can change what you see from your street. And over time, it can change how buyers feel about the neighborhood.

Let us start with the good news for nearby homeowners. The water-free cooling system matters a lot in a desert. Las Vegas gets most of its water from Lake Mead, and water supply is one of the biggest long-term worries in the region. A data center that does not drink up local water is far easier to live next to than one that does. That single design choice may help calm some fears, and it sets a standard that future projects may be measured against.

Now the concerns. Data centers use huge amounts of electricity. As more of them are built, the strain on the power grid grows. If demand for power keeps climbing faster than new power supply, it can put upward pressure on electric rates over time. That affects every household bill, not just the ones next to the data center. This is part of why Commissioner Segerblom raised the question of whether the current pace can last.

There is also the matter of home values and quality of life. A data center is not a noisy factory, but it is a large industrial-style building. Some buyers do not want to live near one. Others do not mind, especially if the building is well screened and the company is a good neighbor. The pedestrian safety barrier condition is a real win here. It shows the county is willing to make developers give something back to the community. Safer streets and good buffers can protect both safety and property values.

For people thinking about buying or selling near Warm Springs and Decatur, the smart move is to stay informed. Know what is being built nearby. Ask about traffic plans, screening, and any conditions the county attached. A well-managed data center with strong conditions is very different from an industrial use with no rules. The details matter, and they can affect both how you live and what your home is worth down the road.

Background and History

Las Vegas has quietly become one of the largest data center markets in the country. The region offers cheap land compared to coastal cities, a low risk of earthquakes and hurricanes, and a business-friendly tax setup. Nevada has no state income tax, which appeals to companies. Over the past several years, the valley has drawn billions of dollars in data center investment.

Switch is a central part of that story. The company built its Core Campus along South Decatur Boulevard, and over time it grew that campus into one of the biggest data center sites in the world. Switch has long promoted the idea that its facilities run on renewable energy and use advanced cooling. The LAS 19 project fits the pattern of Switch expanding its footprint in the same general area where it is already established.

The data center rush has picked up speed because of artificial intelligence. AI programs need enormous computing power to train and run. Companies like Nvidia make the chips that power this work, and demand for AI computing has exploded. That is why you now hear the phrase "AI factory." These are buildings designed to do nothing but heavy AI computing. The partnership between Switch and Nvidia on EVO AI Factories is part of this larger wave.

At the same time, the public mood has started to shift. A few years ago, most data center projects sailed through with little notice. Now, residents show up to meetings and ask hard questions about power and water. Henderson, a neighboring city, has even looked at a moratorium, which is a temporary pause on new data centers. The pushback at the June 17 meeting, and Commissioner Segerblom's call for a future ordinance, fit this changing mood. The valley is starting to weigh the jobs and tax money against the long-term costs to power, water, and quality of life.

What Happens Next

With the unanimous approval in hand, Switch can move forward with the LAS 19 project on the Warm Springs Road site. The next steps will likely include detailed building permits, final engineering, and construction. The condition the commission attached means Switch must also work with the county on the design and building of the pedestrian safety barrier along Warm Springs Road. Watch for that barrier to take shape as part of the overall site work.

The bigger thing to watch is the talk of a future data center ordinance. Commissioner Segerblom has said the county should consider one. If the county moves ahead, it could create new rules for how much power and water data centers can use, how they must be screened from homes, and what they must give back to the community. Such an ordinance would not undo projects already approved, but it could shape every project that comes after. [NOT VERIFIED] No specific ordinance has been adopted yet, and the timeline for any such law is not clear.

Residents who care about this issue should keep an eye on the Clark County Commission agenda. Future data center projects in the southwest valley and elsewhere will continue to come up for votes. Each one is a chance for the public to weigh in. The June 17 vote showed that public comment can lead to real conditions, like the safety barrier, even when a project is approved.

For the housing market, expect the southwest valley to keep drawing both data centers and homes. The two will continue to grow side by side. How well the county manages that mix, through conditions and possible new rules, will help decide whether the area stays a desirable place to live.
