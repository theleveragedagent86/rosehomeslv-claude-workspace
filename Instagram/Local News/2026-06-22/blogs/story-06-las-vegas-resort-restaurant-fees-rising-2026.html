Las Vegas Resort and Restaurant Fees Keep Climbing in 2026

If you have felt that a trip to the Las Vegas Strip costs more than it used to, you are right. In June 2026, news reports made it official. The average resort fee in Las Vegas now sits at about $42.36 per night. That is up roughly 6 percent from a year ago. On top of that, parking fees have grown at more hotels, and the restaurants inside those resorts are paying up to 18 percent more for some meats, poultry, and eggs. Those higher food costs get passed straight to the people eating the meals.

This story is not just about tourists. It hits locals too. Plenty of Las Vegas residents drive to the Strip to eat dinner, catch a show, park for a concert, or meet friends at a bar. When parking charges return and menu prices jump, that affects your weekend plans and your wallet. It also shapes how visitors feel about our city, and tourism touches almost every job and home value in the valley.

There is a bright spot worth knowing about. Resorts World Las Vegas was noted for removing its resort fees and temporarily ending parking charges. That move stands out in a year when most properties are adding charges, not dropping them. Meanwhile, there are growing calls for a resort-fee moratorium, which means a freeze or pause on these extra charges. Below, we break down what is happening, why it matters to people who live here, and what you can actually do about it.

What Happened

In June 2026, local news outlet 8 News Now and gambling industry site Casino.org both reported on the rising hidden costs of a Las Vegas trip. The headline number was the resort fee. A resort fee is a daily charge that a hotel adds on top of the room rate. It is supposed to cover things like Wi-Fi, the pool, the gym, and local phone calls. The catch is that you pay it even if you never touch the pool or the gym.

The new average resort fee is about $42.36 per night, up roughly 6 percent from last year. That is just the average. Some of the fanciest properties charge much more. Reports list places like ARIA, Bellagio, Caesars Palace, Wynn, Encore, Venetian, Palazzo, Fontainebleau, and Waldorf Astoria at around $55 per night before tax. With tax added, that can climb past $62 per night. For a three-night stay, the fees alone can add well over $180 to a bill, on top of the price you thought you were paying.

Parking is the second piece. For years after 2016, many Strip resorts charged for parking. Some had paused or trimmed those fees, but reports in 2026 show parking charges expanding again at more properties. That means even a quick visit to eat or shop can cost you before you walk through the door.

The third piece is food. Restaurants inside the resorts are facing cost increases of up to 18 percent on some meats, poultry, and eggs. When a steakhouse pays more for beef and a breakfast spot pays more for eggs, those costs show up on the menu. A plate that cost $40 last year might cost $46 or more now. Add a resort fee and paid parking, and a simple dinner out turns into a much larger expense than many families planned for.

All of this fed a loud "Vegas is too expensive" backlash that became one of the biggest local-business controversies of the year. The frustration is so strong that there are continued calls for a resort-fee moratorium. One company moved in the opposite direction of the crowd. Resorts World Las Vegas was noted as removing its resort fees and temporarily ending parking charges, a clear bet that no fees might win more guests than nickel-and-diming them.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

It is easy to think resort fees are a tourist problem. They are not. Las Vegas locals use the Strip all the time. People drive down to eat at a favorite restaurant, watch a residency show, take family visitors sightseeing, or grab drinks for a birthday. When parking fees come back and food prices rise, those everyday outings cost more for the people who live here, not just the people flying in.

There is a bigger reason this matters too. Tourism is the engine of the Las Vegas economy. Visitors fill hotel rooms, eat in restaurants, gamble, shop, and tip workers. A huge share of local jobs depend on that flow of guests. When people online start saying Vegas is too expensive, some of them choose another vacation spot instead. Fewer visitors can mean fewer work hours for servers, housekeepers, valets, dealers, and cooks. That ripple reaches household budgets across the valley.

Home values and the housing market are tied to this story as well. When the tourism economy is strong, jobs are steady, and people feel confident buying homes. When tourism softens because guests feel priced out, that confidence can wobble. A healthy Strip helps keep the whole local economy steady, and a steady economy supports steady home prices. So a fight over resort fees is really a fight over the health of the thing that pays a lot of valley paychecks.

There is also a fairness angle that frustrates residents. Many people feel that a fee should be optional or clearly shown up front, not buried until checkout. Federal rules that took effect in 2025 now require hotels to show resort fees up front, totaled before you finish booking online. That helps with surprise, but it does not lower the price. A clearly displayed $55 fee is still $55. So even with better honesty, the core complaint remains: the trip simply costs more, and locals feel it every time they head to the Strip.

Background and History

Resort fees are not new. They started spreading across Las Vegas more than a decade ago. The idea was to advertise a low room rate to win the booking, then add the rest of the cost as a separate daily fee. This helped hotels hide the true price of a room and avoid paying full commissions to online travel sites that base their cut on the room rate alone. Over the years, the fees crept higher and higher.

Paid parking has its own history. For decades, free parking was a proud part of the Las Vegas deal. That changed around 2016, when major operators began charging for parking. Locals pushed back hard. Some properties softened the rules over time, offering free parking to local residents or to loyalty club members, but the charges never fully went away. The 2026 reports show parking fees spreading again, which reopened old wounds for residents who remember when parking was simply free.

Calls for a resort-fee moratorium have come up before too. Lawmakers, consumer groups, and frustrated travelers have argued for years that the fees are misleading. The 2025 federal rule on showing fees up front was a partial answer, but it did not ban the fees or cap them. So in 2026, the debate is back with fresh energy, fueled by the rising averages and the bigger "Vegas is too expensive" conversation playing out online and in local news. What Happens Next

The big question is whether more resorts follow the Resorts World example or keep raising fees. Resorts World removing its resort fees and temporarily pausing parking charges is a real-world test. If that no-fee approach pulls in more guests and more spending, other properties may feel pressure to match it. If guests do not reward the move, the rest of the Strip may decide fees are here to stay.

Watch for the moratorium debate to grow louder. As more news coverage highlights the climbing averages, expect consumer advocates and possibly some local or state officials to push for limits or pauses on these charges. Whether any of that turns into a real rule remains to be seen. Pricing decisions on the Strip are mostly made by private companies, so change may come from competition faster than from law. Food prices are tied to forces beyond Las Vegas. The reported increases of up to 18 percent on some meats, poultry, and eggs reflect wider supply costs. If those costs ease, menu prices could level off. If they stay high or rise, expect restaurants to keep adjusting prices, portions, or menus. Either way, smart visitors and locals will keep hunting for the spots that still offer fair value, and operators will notice where the crowds go.

Finally, keep an eye on how this affects overall visitor numbers in Clark County. If the "too expensive" message sticks, the resort companies have a strong reason to act, because their own revenue depends on keeping rooms and restaurants full. The next few months of booking data and tourism reports will tell us a lot about which way the Strip is heading.

Ryan's Take

I have lived and worked in this valley long enough to know that the Strip is more than a tourist playground. It is the heartbeat of our local economy. When I talk with buyers and sellers, the health of tourism comes up more than people expect, because steady visitor spending means steady jobs, and steady jobs mean a steady housing market. So I watch fee stories like this one closely.

Here is my honest read. Resort fees and surprise parking charges are short-term wins that risk long-term trust. Every time a guest feels tricked at checkout, we lose a little goodwill. That is why I think Resorts World dropping its resort fees is the smarter long game. People remember who treated them fairly, and they come back. Fairness is good business, and it is good for our whole community.

For locals, my advice is simple. Do not let the noise scare you off the Strip, but do plan ahead. Know which properties waive parking or fees for residents, use your loyalty perks, and pick restaurants that still respect your dollar. And if you are thinking about buying or selling a home here, remember that a strong, fair tourism economy supports your property value. That is the bigger picture behind every resort fee headline.

What You Can Do

You have more control than the headlines suggest. Start by checking whether your favorite Strip property waives parking or fees for Nevada residents or loyalty club members. Many do, and a quick sign-up can save you money on every visit. Always confirm the total price, including resort fees and tax, before you book a room, since the new federal rule means that full number must now be shown up front.

When dining out, look at the menu and prices before you sit down, and ask about any service or seating charges. Consider eating at off-Strip spots or local favorites, which often deliver better value. If you want to reward fair pricing, support places like Resorts World that dropped resort fees, since spending follows fairness and operators pay attention. You can also make your voice heard by backing the calls for a resort-fee moratorium through consumer groups and local officials.
