Incumbent Bustamante Adams Leads in CCSD School Board District F Primary

Incumbent Trustee Irene Bustamante Adams is leading the Clark County School District Board District F primary race. Preliminary results posted on June 10, 2026, the day after the June 9 election, show her with 49.3% of the vote. That puts her just under the line she needs to win the seat outright. CCSD facility management director Rob Hutchinson came in second with about 32.6%. Small-business owner Laurence Neely finished third with 18.1%.

Here is the key thing to understand. In this race, a candidate needs more than 50% of the vote to win in the primary. Bustamante Adams is close, but she has not crossed that line yet. If the final count keeps her under 50%, the top two finishers move on to the November general election. That would set up a head-to-head between Bustamante Adams and Hutchinson in the fall.

Two other school board seats will not be decided until November. In District D, Trustee Brenda Zamora faces challenger David Gomez. In District G, Trustee Linda Cavazos faces challenger Paul Casey. Those races have no primary because each one already has just two candidates.

Why should you care about a school board race if you do not have kids in school? Because the board controls every contested policy and budget vote in the district. School quality shapes home values, neighborhood demand, and even how easy it is to sell a house later. In this post we will break down the results, explain what they mean for Las Vegas families and homeowners, and walk through what comes next.

What Happened

On Tuesday, June 9, 2026, Clark County voters went to the polls for the primary election. School board races were on the ballot along with other local contests. The next day, June 10, election officials posted preliminary results. Those early numbers are what we are reporting here. They can shift a little as mail ballots and provisional ballots are counted, but they give a clear picture of where the race stands.

In the District F contest, three candidates were running. Irene Bustamante Adams is the sitting trustee, which means she already holds the seat. She led with 49.3% of the vote. Rob Hutchinson, who works as a facility management director for CCSD itself, came in second at about 32.6%. Laurence Neely, a small-business owner, finished third with 18.1%.

The math here matters a lot. Nevada uses a rule for many local offices where a candidate must win more than half of all votes cast to take the seat in the primary. If someone clears 50% plus one vote, the race is over and that person wins. No general election is needed for that office. But if no one reaches that bar, the two candidates with the most votes advance to November. Then voters choose between just those two.

Bustamante Adams is sitting at 49.3%. That is very close to the cutoff but not over it. Whether she wins outright depends on how the remaining uncounted ballots break. If the final tally pushes her above 50%, she keeps her seat and avoids a fall campaign. If it leaves her below 50%, she and Hutchinson move on to the general election. Neely, in third place, would be eliminated either way.

It is worth noting that an incumbent leading with nearly half the vote in a three-way race is a strong showing. It tells you many voters already know her and chose to back her again. At the same time, more than half of voters picked someone else, split between two challengers. That split is part of why she may have fallen just short of the outright win.

The other two seats up this cycle, District D and District G, did not appear on the June primary ballot. Each of those races has only two candidates, so there was nothing to narrow down. Those will be settled in the November general election. In District D, incumbent Brenda Zamora faces David Gomez. In District G, incumbent Linda Cavazos faces Paul Casey.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

The CCSD Board of Trustees is the group of elected officials who set the rules for one of the largest school districts in the entire country. Clark County School District serves hundreds of thousands of students across the valley. The board votes on the budget, on policies, on school boundaries, on start times, on busing, and on big decisions about hiring and spending. Every one of those votes can affect your daily life, even if you do not have a student in the system.

Start with home values. Good schools are one of the biggest drivers of demand in any neighborhood. When buyers shop for a house, many of them look up the assigned schools first. Strong schools push prices up and make homes sell faster. Struggling schools can drag prices down and keep homes on the market longer. The people on the school board help decide how money is spent across schools, which shapes that quality over time.

Next, think about taxes and budgets. The school district is funded in large part by property taxes and state money. How the board manages that budget affects how far those dollars go. A board that spends wisely can deliver better results without asking for more. A board that struggles with money may push for funding measures down the road. Either way, homeowners feel it.

There is also the day-to-day side. Recent board decisions in Clark County have touched things parents notice right away, like changing school start times for the 2026-27 year and looking at an opt-in busing model to save millions of dollars. Those are exactly the kinds of choices a board makes. Who sits on the board shapes how those choices come out.

Finally, the makeup of the board matters as a whole. One trustee is one vote. But the balance of the full board decides which way close calls go. If incumbents hold their seats, the board keeps its current direction. If challengers win, the board can shift. The District F result, plus the District D and District G races in November, will together tell us whether Clark County keeps the current board or moves in a new direction. For families and homeowners alike, that direction is worth watching.

Background and History

School board members in Clark County are elected by district. The county is divided into geographic trustee districts, and voters in each one choose the person who represents them. That is why you see labels like District F, District D, and District G. Each seat covers a different slice of the valley. You vote only in the race for the district where you live.

Irene Bustamante Adams is the incumbent in District F, meaning she already serves on the board. Before facing this primary, she held the seat she is now defending. Incumbents often have an edge because voters recognize their names and have seen their record. Her 49.3% showing in a three-way race fits that pattern of incumbent strength, even though it left her just short of an outright win.

Her challengers came from different backgrounds. Rob Hutchinson works as a facility management director for CCSD. That gives him an inside view of how the district runs its buildings and operations. Laurence Neely is a small-business owner, a background that often appeals to voters who want a focus on budgets and accountability. Each candidate offered voters a different lens on how to run the district.

The two-step election process is not new. Nevada has long used primaries to narrow crowded fields, then a general election to make the final pick when no one wins a majority. For races with only two candidates, like District D and District G this year, there is no need to narrow anything down, so those skip straight to November. This system is meant to make sure the winner has broad support rather than just a small slice of a divided vote.

It also helps to remember that school board service is often less visible than other elected jobs, yet the stakes are large. These trustees oversee a budget and a student population bigger than many cities. Decisions made in board meetings ripple out to classrooms, neighborhoods, and home values across the valley.

What Happens Next

The next step is the final count. The numbers reported on June 10 are preliminary. Election officials still have to finish counting mail ballots and any provisional ballots before they certify the results. Those final numbers will decide one important question in District F: did Bustamante Adams cross 50%, or not?

If the final count puts her above 50%, she wins the seat outright. The race ends, and she keeps her place on the board with no fall campaign needed. If the final count keeps her under 50%, she and Rob Hutchinson advance to the November general election. Laurence Neely, in third, would not move forward in that case.

Either way, November is going to be a big month for the school board. The District D race between Brenda Zamora and David Gomez will be decided then. The District G race between Linda Cavazos and Paul Casey will be decided then too. And if District F did not end in the primary, that contest will be on the November ballot as well. So voters could be choosing one, two, or three school board seats this fall, depending on how District F finalizes.

For voters, the practical takeaway is simple. Watch for the certified results to confirm the District F outcome. Then mark your calendar for the November general election, because that is when the other seats, and possibly District F, will be settled. Local election offices will publish the official candidate lists and your assigned polling place ahead of time.

The bigger picture to watch is the board's balance after November. Three incumbents are defending seats this cycle. How many hold on, and how many are replaced, will shape the direction of the district for years to come.

What You Can Do

First, confirm which trustee district you live in. You vote only in the race for your own district, so this matters. Your county election office can tell you your district and your assigned polling place. Knowing this ahead of time saves confusion on election day.

Second, watch for the certified District F results. The preliminary count had Bustamante Adams at 49.3%, just under the line for an outright win. The final certified numbers will tell you whether that race is over or heading to November. Once it is official, you will know exactly what is on your fall ballot.

Third, mark the November general election on your calendar. The District D race and the District G race will be decided then. District F may be on the ballot too if no one cleared 50%. Make a plan now for how you will vote, whether in person, early, or by mail.

Fourth, get to know the candidates. Read their stated positions, watch a board meeting if you can, and think about how their choices would affect your schools and your neighborhood. An informed vote is a stronger vote.
