Oakland Athletics Hold Groundbreaking for $1.75 Billion Las Vegas Strip Ballpark

The Oakland Athletics will hold a ceremonial groundbreaking on June 23, 2026, for their roughly $1.75 billion domed ballpark on the south end of the Las Vegas Strip. The new stadium is being built on the former Tropicana site, the famous corner where the old Tropicana resort stood for almost 70 years. Team owner John Fisher and Major League Baseball Commissioner Rob Manfred are both expected to attend the event. This is a big moment, because it turns years of talk and paperwork into a real building that people can finally see rise from the ground.

If you live in Las Vegas, this is one of the largest projects to hit the Strip in years. The short answer to the biggest question is this: yes, the ballpark is really happening, the foundation work is already finished, and the project is still on schedule to open before the 2028 baseball season. The Athletics have already spent about $400 million so far. The team has been playing its home games in a minor league park in Sacramento while it waits for the Las Vegas home to be ready.

This blog will walk you through what is happening on June 23, why the ballpark matters for your home value and your neighborhood, how we got here after a long fight, and what comes next on the timeline. I will also share my honest take as a local real estate agent and give you simple steps you can take if you want to follow the project or get ready for the changes it will bring to the south Strip. Let us dig in.

What Happened

On June 23, 2026, the Athletics are holding a ceremonial groundbreaking for their new Las Vegas ballpark. A ceremonial groundbreaking is the public event where leaders grab shovels, turn over some dirt, pose for photos, and officially mark the start of a project. In this case, the heavy work has actually been going on for a while. The team confirmed that the foundation work is already complete. That means the deep concrete supports that hold up a huge building are in place, and the project can now move to the parts you can see from the road.

The price tag is about $1.75 billion. Some news outlets have reported a number closer to $2 billion, but the figure the team and most reports lead with is $1.75 billion. The Athletics have spent about $400 million on the project to date. That spending covers things like design, site prep, demolition of the old Tropicana, and the foundation work already finished.

The ballpark is going up on the former Tropicana site at the corner of Las Vegas Boulevard and Tropicana Avenue. The old Tropicana resort was closed and then torn down to make room. The stadium will be a domed ballpark, which means it has a permanent roof to keep fans and players out of the desert heat. In a city where summer days can pass 110 degrees, a roof is not a luxury. It is the only way to play comfortable baseball here in July and August.

Two big names are expected at the event. The first is John Fisher, the team's owner. The second is Rob Manfred, the Commissioner of Major League Baseball, who is the top official for the entire sport. When the commissioner shows up to a groundbreaking, it signals that the league is fully behind the move and wants the public to see that support.

The stadium does not stand alone. It anchors a much larger redevelopment of the whole former Tropicana site and the south end of the Strip. Bally's Corporation owns the rest of the land around the ballpark and plans to build a new resort there in the future. So the shovels on June 23 are really the first piece of a multi-billion-dollar makeover for that corner. The ballpark plan calls for a capacity in the range of 33,000 seats, which would make it one of the smaller, more intimate parks in the major leagues. Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

You might wonder why a baseball stadium should matter to you if you are not a sports fan. The honest answer is that a project this size touches your life whether you ever buy a ticket or not. A $1.75 billion build on the Strip reaches into jobs, traffic, home values, and the daily feel of the south valley.

Start with jobs. Building a stadium takes thousands of construction workers over several years. Once it opens, it needs people to run it: ticket takers, food and drink workers, cleaning crews, security, parking staff, and game-day workers. A major league team also brings in front-office jobs and draws visitors who spend money at nearby hotels, restaurants, and shops. More spending in the area means more demand for local services, and that ripples out to many families.

Next, think about home values. When a marquee project lands near a neighborhood, homes within a reasonable drive often see steady interest. Buyers who work at or near the stadium want to live close. Investors look for rental homes and short-term rental spots near a new draw. People who follow the Athletics may want to plant roots near their team. That extra demand can support prices in the south valley, especially in areas like the southwest, Enterprise, and the communities near the airport and the south Strip.

Traffic and noise are the other side of the coin. Game days bring crowds, and a 33,000-seat venue near one of the busiest intersections in the city will change how cars move around Tropicana Avenue and Las Vegas Boulevard. If you live nearby, you will want to plan for busier roads on event nights. The upside is that big projects often come with road and transit upgrades, which can help the whole area in the long run.

There is also pride and identity. Las Vegas spent decades as a city without major league teams. Now it has the Golden Knights in hockey and the Raiders in football, and baseball is on the way. Each team makes the city feel more like a true big-league town and less like only a tourist stop. That shift helps draw families and workers who want a full set of pro sports nearby, and a stronger draw for new residents tends to support a healthy housing market over time.

Background and History

The road to this groundbreaking was long and far from smooth. The Athletics have deep roots in Oakland, California, where they played for more than 50 years. For years the team and the city of Oakland fought over a new stadium. When those plans fell apart, the team began looking for a new home, and Las Vegas rose to the top of the list.

In 2023, the team reached deals to move to Las Vegas and chose the Tropicana site for the new park. The Nevada Legislature approved a public funding package to help pay for the stadium, which sparked plenty of debate. Some residents and groups argued that public money should not go toward a private sports team. Supporters said the project would bring jobs, tourism, and a major league team that puts the city on the national stage. The fight included talk of a possible voter referendum to block the funding, but the move pushed forward.

The old Tropicana resort, which opened in 1957, was one of the most famous names on the Strip. It closed in April 2024 and was demolished to clear the land. Watching that classic resort fall, then seeing a ballpark planned in its place, says a lot about how fast the Strip keeps reinventing itself. The corner has gone from old-school casino glamour to a new chapter built around sports.

While the new park is being built, the team has been playing in Sacramento at a minor league field. The team has not used the Oakland name in the usual way during this stretch, since it left Oakland and has not yet planted in Las Vegas. This in-between period is unusual in pro sports and shows just how much is riding on getting the Las Vegas home finished on time.

What Happens Next

The groundbreaking on June 23 is the starting gun for the most visible phase of the build. With the foundation finished, the next steps involve raising the steel frame, building the seating bowl, and putting up the dome and walls. Over the next many months, you should expect to see cranes, steel, and concrete take the shape of a real stadium on that corner.

The big target date is the opening before the 2028 season. Major league baseball seasons usually start in late March or early April, so the team is aiming to have the park ready for play in early 2028. To hit that goal, crews need to keep a steady pace and avoid major delays from weather, supply shortages, or labor problems. The team has said the project remains on schedule, which is a good sign at this stage.

Alongside the stadium, watch for movement on the larger redevelopment. Bally's Corporation owns the rest of the former Tropicana land and plans a future resort there. The timing of that resort is separate from the ballpark, so it may come later. Still, the two projects together will reshape the south Strip over the coming years. [NOT VERIFIED] A firm start date for the Bally's resort portion has not been clearly set in public reports, so view it as a future plan rather than a fixed schedule.

You can also expect more news around team branding, ticket plans, and road and transit changes as the opening gets closer. Local agencies will likely study how to handle game-day traffic near one of the busiest corners in the valley. If you live or work nearby, those updates are worth following, because they will shape your daily drive once games begin.
