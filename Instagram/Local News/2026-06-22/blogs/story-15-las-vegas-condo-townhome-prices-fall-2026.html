Condo and Townhome Prices Fall as Listings Rise, Diverging From Single-Family Market

If you have been priced out of buying a house in Las Vegas, here is some news that may give you a real opening. While single-family home prices in Southern Nevada keep setting new records, the condo and townhome part of the market is moving in the opposite direction. The median price for an attached home (that means a condo or townhome that shares a wall with a neighbor) fell 3.9% over the past year, landing at $295,000 in May 2026. That is below the record of $315,000 the segment hit back in October 2024.

At the same time, fewer of these homes are selling and more of them are sitting on the market. Sales of condos and townhomes dropped 8.9% compared to a year earlier. The number of attached homes for sale rose 5.1%, climbing to 2,639 units. When prices go down, sales slow, and listings pile up all at once, it usually means buyers have more power than they did before.

This is a big deal because the single-family median price in the valley is now around $490,000. For a lot of working families, first-time buyers, and younger residents, that number is simply out of reach. Condos and townhomes priced under $300,000 are often the most realistic way into homeownership. When that part of the market softens, it can swing the door open for buyers who thought they had no shot in 2026. Let me walk you through exactly what the numbers say, why they matter for your wallet, and what you can do about it right now.

What Happened

The latest report on the Southern Nevada housing market, covered by the Las Vegas Review-Journal, showed a clear split between two parts of the market. On one side, you have single-family houses. On the other side, you have condos and townhomes, also called attached homes. These two groups are usually expected to move together. In May 2026, they did not. They went in opposite directions.

Here are the key facts for the condo and townhome side. The median price fell 3.9% from one year earlier, dropping to $295,000. The word "median" means the middle price. Half of the homes sold for more and half sold for less. That $295,000 figure is now below the segment's all-time high of $315,000, which was set in October 2024. So in about a year and a half, the typical attached home in the valley lost roughly $20,000 in value at the median level.

Sales also slowed down a lot. The number of condos and townhomes that actually closed fell 8.9% compared to the same month a year before. Fewer buyers are pulling the trigger on these homes. When sales drop like this, it tells you that demand has cooled off in this specific part of the market.

Meanwhile, the supply of attached homes for sale grew. Inventory rose 5.1%, reaching 2,639 units available. More homes for sale plus fewer buyers is the kind of combination that pushes prices down and gives the people who are buying more room to negotiate. Sellers in this category are now competing with each other for a smaller pool of buyers.

Now compare that to the single-family side. Detached houses in the valley hit a record median price of about $490,000 in the same period. That side of the market stayed hot, with buyers still chasing a limited number of houses. So you have one part of the market setting records while another part slips backward. That kind of split is what experts call a "divergence." It does not happen often, and it is worth paying close attention to.

Why would condos cool off while houses heat up? A few things are likely at play. Higher mortgage rates make every monthly payment more expensive, which hits budget-focused buyers the hardest. Many condo and townhome communities also carry homeowners association (HOA) fees, and in some cases those fees have climbed. On top of that, lenders can be stricter about approving loans in certain condo complexes. All of these factors can scare off the very buyers who would normally shop in this price range.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

For a lot of people in the valley, this split in the market is not just an interesting fact. It directly affects whether you can afford to buy a home this year. Let me break down who this matters to and why.

First, think about first-time buyers. If the typical house costs $490,000, that is a heavy lift. To buy at that price, you usually need a large down payment, a strong income, and the ability to handle a big monthly mortgage. Many renters in Las Vegas simply cannot get there yet. But a condo or townhome at $295,000 is a different story. The down payment is smaller. The monthly payment is lower. And now that prices in this segment are falling, the math is getting a little friendlier. This could be the first real chance some buyers have had in years.

Second, think about people who already own a condo or townhome. If you bought near the October 2024 peak of $315,000 and you need to sell now, you may find that your home is worth less than you paid. That is a tough spot. It does not mean you have lost money for good, because home values move in cycles and can recover. But it does mean you should be careful about timing and pricing if you are thinking of selling soon. You may need to price your home competitively to get it sold in a market with more listings and fewer buyers.

Third, think about renters who are watching the market. A softer condo segment can be a signal that affordability is slowly improving at the entry level. If you have been on the fence, this is the kind of shift that can tilt the decision toward buying, especially if you plan to stay put for several years. Owning builds equity over time, while rent checks build nothing for you.

Fourth, this matters for the whole local economy. Las Vegas needs homes that working families can actually afford. Service workers, teachers, healthcare staff, and hospitality employees keep this city running. When the entry-level part of the market softens, it can ease some of the pressure on these households. A healthy housing market needs options at every price level, not just at the top.

Finally, keep in mind that not every condo or townhome is the same. Some communities are well run, with healthy reserves and reasonable HOA fees. Others have problems that can make financing hard or push up your monthly costs. So while the overall trend is a price drop, the right choice depends a lot on the specific community you are looking at.

Background and History

To understand why this is happening, it helps to look back at how the Las Vegas market got here. Over the last several years, the valley saw a huge run-up in home prices. Low mortgage rates during the early 2020s let buyers borrow cheaply, which pushed demand and prices way up across every type of home. Condos and townhomes rode that wave too, climbing to their record median of $315,000 in October 2024.

Then mortgage rates went up and stayed higher. By June 2026, rates in Nevada were holding in the range of roughly 6.5% to 6.8%. Higher rates make borrowing more expensive, which cools demand. But here is the key point: the cooling did not hit every part of the market the same way. Single-family homes kept climbing because the supply of houses stayed tight and buyers kept competing for them. The attached-home market, which serves more budget-focused buyers, felt the squeeze of higher rates and extra costs more sharply.

Condos and townhomes have always behaved a little differently from detached houses. They tend to attract first-time buyers, downsizers, and investors. They often come with HOA fees that cover shared spaces and maintenance. And financing them can be trickier, because lenders look at the health of the whole community, not just the individual buyer. When money gets tight or rules get stricter, this segment can lose steam faster than the single-family side.

Las Vegas has seen splits like this before, though they do not happen every cycle. The current divergence is a reminder that "the housing market" is really many smaller markets stacked together. The headline number you see in the news, like the $490,000 single-family record, does not tell the whole story. Underneath it, different price tiers and home types can be heading in completely different directions at the same time.

What Happens Next

Nobody can predict the market with perfect accuracy, so I will stick to what the trends suggest rather than make promises. Based on the current numbers, here is what could reasonably play out in the months ahead.

If mortgage rates stay near where they are, around 6.5% to 6.8%, the pressure on the condo and townhome segment is likely to continue for a while. With more listings and fewer buyers, prices in this part of the market may keep drifting lower or hold flat before they find a bottom. That would be good news for buyers and a challenge for sellers in this category.

If rates start to come down later in the year, that could change the picture. Lower rates make monthly payments cheaper, which can bring budget-focused buyers back into the market. If that happens, the extra demand could slow or even reverse the price drop in the condo segment. So watching rate news is one of the smartest things a buyer or seller can do right now. The growing supply of attached homes is also worth watching. If inventory keeps climbing past 2,639 units, buyers will have even more choices and even more negotiating power. If listings start to shrink instead, it would signal that the segment is firming up. Either way, the inventory number is a useful early warning sign for where prices are heading.

One more thing to watch is the gap between the two segments. Right now there is a wide spread between the $295,000 condo median and the $490,000 single-family median. That gap of nearly $200,000 is large. Over time, gaps like this can pull buyers toward the cheaper option. If enough buyers shift toward condos and townhomes to save money, that demand could eventually help stabilize prices in the segment.

Ryan's Take

In my view, this is one of the more interesting market stories I have seen in a while, and it is mostly good news for buyers who felt shut out. When the headlines scream about record prices, it is easy to assume there is no way in. But the data tells a more hopeful story underneath. There is a real, growing pool of homes under $300,000 in Southern Nevada, and right now those sellers have to compete for you.

That said, I always tell my clients to look past the price tag. A condo or townhome can be a fantastic first home, but only if the community behind it is solid. I want to see the HOA budget, the reserve funds, any special assessments, and whether the complex is approved for the loan you plan to use. A low price means nothing if you get hit with a surprise assessment or cannot get financing. I have seen great deals and I have seen traps, and the difference is in the details.

My honest advice: if you are a buyer with a steady income and a plan to stay put for at least a few years, this softer condo market is worth a serious look. You may be able to negotiate on price, ask for help with closing costs, or get repairs handled before you close. For sellers in this segment, price it right from day one. In a market with rising listings, the homes that sit are usually the ones that started too high.

What You Can Do

If this market shift has you thinking about your next move, here are some practical steps you can take right now.

If you are a buyer: Get pre-approved for a mortgage so you know your real budget. Ask your lender specifically about condo financing, since some complexes have extra rules. When you find a place you like, request the HOA documents and read them carefully. Look at the monthly fee, the reserve fund, and any planned special assessments. Make a list of communities you like and track how long their listings sit, because slow-moving listings often mean more room to negotiate.

If you are a seller: Price your home to match today's market, not last year's peak. Look at what similar homes in your community have actually sold for recently, not just what they were listed at. Make small fixes and clean up before photos go online, because first impressions matter more when buyers have choices. Be ready to negotiate on price or closing costs to get the deal done.

For everyone: Keep an eye on mortgage rates and monthly inventory reports, since both are early signals of where prices are headed. And do not rely on the headline number alone. Your price tier and home type may be moving in a very different direction than the overall market.
