Las Vegas Seeks Developer for New Two-Sheet Ice Arena in Northwest Valley

The City of Las Vegas wants to build a brand new ice arena in the northwest valley, and it is asking builders to step forward with their plans. The city opened a request-for-proposals process, often called an RFP, for a two-sheet ice arena on about five acres of city-owned land. The site sits near Deer Springs Way and Centennial Parkway, just off the 215 Beltway. If you live in the northwest and your kids play hockey, or you skate yourself, this is the short answer you have been waiting for: more ice is coming, and the city is moving to make it happen.

City leaders say the reason is simple. There is not enough ice for everyone who wants to use it. Councilwoman Francis Allen-Palenske, who started the request, put it plainly. "We don't have enough ice for the demand in hockey. In Southern Nevada, it's overwhelming," she said. The chosen spot is at least 10 miles from the closest existing rinks, and it sits in the middle of a fast-growing area with roughly 3,500 new homes nearby. That mix of new families and long drives to ice is exactly the problem the city is trying to solve.

The city extended the deadline for proposals to June 15, 2026. Officials expect to pick a winning plan by the end of the year. A new public ice facility would give Clark County's growing youth and adult hockey community something it badly needs: more time on the ice, closer to home. Below, we break down what happened, why it matters for northwest homeowners, the history behind the ice shortage, what comes next, and what you can do if you care about this project.

What Happened

In April 2026, the City of Las Vegas opened a formal request-for-proposals process for a new two-sheet ice arena. A two-sheet arena means the building would hold two separate sheets of ice, not just one. That is a big deal, because two sheets roughly double the amount of skating time a single building can offer. More sheets mean more games, more practices, and more open skate hours for the public.

The city set aside about five acres of land it already owns. The location is in the northwest part of the valley, near Deer Springs Way and Centennial Parkway, close to the 215 Beltway. That area has grown quickly over the past several years, with roughly 3,500 new homes built or planned nearby. Right now, the nearest ice rinks are at least 10 miles away. For a family rushing to a 6 a.m. practice, those 10 miles can feel like a marathon.

An RFP is a public invitation for companies to send in detailed plans. Builders and operators describe how they would design, pay for, build, and run the facility. The city then reviews the proposals and picks the one that best fits its goals. This is different from the city simply building the arena itself. By using an RFP, the city can bring in private money and expertise while still owning the land and shaping the project.

Councilwoman Francis Allen-Palenske initiated the request. She represents part of the northwest and has pointed to the clear gap between how many people want ice time and how little is available. "We don't have enough ice for the demand in hockey. In Southern Nevada, it's overwhelming," she said. Her words match what many local hockey parents have been saying for years.

The original deadline for proposals was later pushed back. The city extended it to June 15, 2026, to give interested developers more time to put together strong, complete plans. Extending a deadline is common with large projects, and it often signals that the city wants serious, well-thought-out submissions rather than rushed ones. City officials say they expect to choose a winning proposal by the end of 2026.

It is important to be clear about where things stand. As of now, no builder has been selected, no design has been approved, and no opening date has been set. The city is still in the early step of collecting and reviewing ideas. What we know for sure is the location, the size goal of two sheets, the roughly five acres of city land, the June 15, 2026 deadline, and the year-end target for picking a plan. Anything beyond that, such as exact cost or opening year, is not yet confirmed. [NOT VERIFIED]

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

If you live in the northwest valley, this project could change daily life in real ways. Hockey is one of the fastest-growing sports in Southern Nevada, and ice time is hard to find. Parents often drive across the entire valley, sometimes more than once a day, to get their kids to practice. A new arena near Deer Springs and Centennial would cut those drives sharply for thousands of families. Less time in the car means more time at home, less gas money spent, and fewer early-morning treks across town.

There is also a money side to this for homeowners. New amenities like ice rinks, parks, and recreation centers tend to make nearby neighborhoods more attractive. When buyers shop for a home, they look at what is close by. A family-friendly facility within a short drive can be a selling point. It is fair to say that a well-run public arena could support home values in the surrounding area over time, though the exact effect on any single home depends on many factors. [NOT VERIFIED]

The arena would not just serve kids. Adult hockey leagues are popular in Las Vegas, and open public skate sessions draw people of all ages. Figure skating, learn-to-skate classes, and even birthday parties all need ice. A two-sheet building can host many of these at once, which spreads out the demand and gives more people a chance to get on the ice at a reasonable hour. Right now, the most popular time slots fill up fast, and many families end up skating very early or very late.

There is a community-building angle too. Youth sports give kids a healthy place to spend time, make friends, and learn teamwork. They also bring parents together. A neighborhood arena can become a local gathering spot, much like a community center. For a part of the valley that has added thousands of homes in a short time, having shared places to meet neighbors matters. New rooftops alone do not make a neighborhood feel like home. Shared spaces do.

Finally, this is a sign of how the city is thinking about the northwest. By committing land and inviting builders, leaders are saying they see this area as a place worth investing in. That kind of public attention often comes alongside other improvements, such as roads, retail, and services. For current and future residents, that broader picture is worth watching.

Background and History

The push for more ice in Las Vegas is not new. Interest in hockey jumped sharply after the Vegas Golden Knights began play in the National Hockey League in 2017. The team reached the Stanley Cup Final in its very first season and later won a championship. Big crowds, sold-out games, and constant local buzz turned many families on to the sport. Kids who watched the Golden Knights wanted to play, and youth hockey sign-ups climbed.

But the number of ice sheets in the valley did not keep up with that surge in interest. Southern Nevada is a desert, and ice rinks are expensive to build and run because they need heavy cooling systems and lots of energy. For a long time, the region had only a handful of sheets serving a metro area of more than two million people. That math simply does not work when demand keeps rising.

The shortage got worse when existing rinks faced problems. As reported in a related story, the Hylo Park ice arena in North Las Vegas closed for about 14 months, taking valuable ice sheets out of service during that time. When even one rink goes offline in a region that already has too few, the squeeze on the remaining rinks becomes painful. Practice times shift earlier and later, leagues compete for slots, and public skate hours shrink.

At the same time, the northwest valley itself has boomed. Areas near the 215 Beltway, including the Centennial Parkway corridor, have filled with new master-planned neighborhoods. Thousands of homes have gone up, bringing young families who want activities close by. Yet the rinks stayed clustered in other parts of the valley, leaving the growing northwest with long drives and little local ice.

City and county leaders have heard the complaints for years. The current RFP is the clearest step yet toward fixing the gap in the northwest. By offering city-owned land and inviting private builders, the city is trying to lower the biggest barriers, which are land cost and risk. This approach has been used for other public facilities, and it lets the city share the heavy lifting with companies that know how to build and run arenas.

What Happens Next

The next milestone is the June 15, 2026 proposal deadline. After that date, the city's staff will review every plan that was submitted. They will look at things like design quality, cost, the builder's experience, how the arena would be paid for, and how it would be run day to day. This review takes time because the city wants to choose a partner that can actually deliver and operate the facility for the long term.

City officials say they expect to pick a winning proposal by the end of 2026. Choosing a proposal is not the same as breaking ground. Once a plan is selected, there are usually more steps, such as contract negotiations, final design work, permits, and approvals by the City Council. Each of these steps can take months. So even with a winner named by year-end, actual construction would likely start later. [NOT VERIFIED]

Residents should expect chances to weigh in along the way. Big public projects often include community meetings and City Council sessions where people can speak. These moments matter, because public input can shape details like hours, pricing, parking, and traffic. If the northwest wants this arena to truly serve local families, showing up and speaking up is one of the most effective things residents can do.

It is worth setting realistic expectations on timing. Building a two-sheet ice arena is a major project. Even under a smooth path, it commonly takes a couple of years from selecting a builder to opening the doors. No opening date has been announced, and anyone claiming a firm date right now would be guessing. The honest answer is that the project is real and moving, but the finish line is still down the road.
