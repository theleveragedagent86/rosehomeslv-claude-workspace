Task Force Unveils Traffic Safety Plan After 427 CCSD Students Struck by Cars

A countywide group in Clark County just shared a new plan to keep students safe near schools. It comes after 427 people age 18 and under were hit by cars near schools during the 2025-26 school year. That is a jump of more than 350 percent over the year before. Four of those students died. This matters to every family in Las Vegas because it touches the walk, the ride, and the drive that kids make every single school day.

The group is called the School Traffic Safety Working Group. It brought together local leaders to study why so many kids were getting hurt. The numbers shocked a lot of people. About half of the victims were riding e-scooters or e-bikes when they were struck. That points to a fast change in how kids get to and from school. The plan they released is meant to slow cars down, make crossings safer, and teach both drivers and students how to share the road.

For parents, this is not just news. It is about the corner your child crosses. It is about the street outside the middle school. It is about whether the route to campus feels safe enough to let a kid walk or ride alone. In this article, we will break down what the plan says, why it matters for Las Vegas neighborhoods, how we got here, what comes next, and what you can do right now.

What Happened

The School Traffic Safety Working Group is a countywide team. It formed to study a scary spike in crashes involving young people near schools. When the group looked at the 2025-26 school year, they found that 427 people age 18 and under had been struck by cars near schools. The year before, the number was far lower. This new count is a rise of more than 350 percent. That is not a small bump. It is a huge leap in a single year.

The details make the story even more serious. About half of the students who were hit were on e-scooters or e-bikes. These small electric rides have become very popular with kids across the valley. They are quick, cheap, and fun. But they also move fast, and drivers are not always looking for them. Four students were killed during that school year. Behind each of those numbers is a family that will never be the same.

So the group put together a list of recommendations. The plan is broad, and it tries to hit the problem from several sides at once. Here are the main ideas the group shared.

First, the plan calls for extra police presence during the first two weeks of school. Those early days are some of the most dangerous. Traffic patterns reset, new drivers join the roads, and kids are still learning their routes. More officers near campuses can slow cars and remind everyone to pay attention.

Second, the plan asks for new signage and pavement repairs. Faded crosswalk paint and missing signs make streets harder to read. Fresh markings and clear signs help drivers know exactly where students cross.

Third, the group wants protected bike lanes. These are lanes with a real barrier between bikes and cars, not just a painted stripe. Since so many victims were on e-bikes and e-scooters, this change could make a big difference.

Fourth, the plan suggests adding school-zone content to the driver's license exam. The idea is simple. If new drivers have to learn the rules about school zones to pass the test, they will know them before they ever drive near a campus.

The group did not form by accident. It came together after a tragedy that hit close to home. McKenzie Scott, an 18-year-old student at Arbor View High School, was struck and killed while walking in a marked crosswalk. A marked crosswalk is supposed to be the safe place to cross. Her death pushed leaders to act and to look at the bigger pattern behind it.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

This plan is not just a school district issue. It reaches into daily life for families all over the valley. If you have kids, you probably think about the route to school more than you realize. You watch the clock, the traffic, and the weather. Now you also have to think about whether that route is safe from cars.

Las Vegas grew fast, and a lot of our neighborhoods were built around cars. Wide streets and long blocks are common. Those streets move traffic well, but they can be hard for a child on foot or on a scooter. When a road is built for speed, drivers tend to drive fast. That is a tough mix near a school where kids are coming and going.

The rise of e-scooters and e-bikes adds a new layer. Many parents bought these rides to help kids get to school without a car. That seemed like a smart, healthy choice. But the crash numbers show a hidden risk. These rides are fast and quiet. A driver turning right may never see a scooter coming up the sidewalk. Half of the victims in this report were on one of these devices. That should make every family stop and think about helmets, speed, and safe routes.

There is also a money side that many people miss. Safe streets near schools help home values. Buyers with kids look closely at how their children will get to class. A neighborhood with clear crosswalks, calm traffic, and safe bike lanes feels like a place to raise a family. A street known for close calls does the opposite. Over time, that reputation can follow a whole area.

It is worth noting how spread out our valley is. Kids in Summerlin, Henderson, the northwest, and the east side all face different streets. Some go to school on quiet cul-de-sacs. Others cross six-lane roads that carry heavy traffic all day. A plan that works has to fit each of these places. That is why the working group looked at the whole county and not just one campus. The goal is safer routes for every student, no matter which side of town they call home.

Then there is the simple human side. Four families lost a child in one school year. Hundreds more had to rush to a hospital or an urgent care. Those events ripple out to classmates, teachers, and neighbors. When a plan like this works, it does not just save money or lift home values. It keeps kids alive and keeps our community whole. That is why this news should matter to everyone, not only to parents.

Background and History

To understand this plan, it helps to see how we got here. Clark County is home to one of the largest school districts in the country. The Clark County School District, or CCSD, serves hundreds of thousands of students across the Las Vegas valley. That means a huge number of kids are on the move near schools every single morning and afternoon.

For years, school zones have used the same basic tools. Yellow signs, flashing lights, painted crosswalks, and crossing guards at some corners. These tools help, but they were built for a slower time. They were designed when most kids either walked short distances or rode a school bus. They were not designed for a wave of fast electric scooters and bikes.

The spike in crashes during the 2025-26 school year forced a new look. A more than 350 percent jump is not something leaders could ignore. Something had changed on our streets, and the old tools were not keeping up. That is when the School Traffic Safety Working Group came together to dig into the data and find real answers.

The death of McKenzie Scott gave the effort a face and a sense of urgency. She was 18 and attending Arbor View High School. She was killed in a marked crosswalk, the exact spot where students are told they will be safe. When a marked crossing fails, it shakes public trust. It tells families that the rules on paper are not enough. Her case became a turning point that helped push this group to form and to act.

Nevada has also been part of a national conversation about pedestrian safety. Across the country, more people on foot have been struck in recent years. Bigger vehicles, faster roads, and busy, distracted drivers all play a role. Clark County is not alone in facing this problem. But the size of the local spike made it clear that our valley needed its own plan, built for our streets and our kids.

What Happens Next

The plan is a set of recommendations, so the next step is turning ideas into action. Some parts can move quickly. Extra police presence during the first two weeks of school is something local agencies can plan for right away. New signs and fresh crosswalk paint are also fairly simple to schedule, though they take time and money.

Other parts will take longer. Protected bike lanes need design work, funding, and construction. You cannot add a barrier to a busy road overnight. These projects often move one street at a time, starting near the schools with the worst crash records. Families should expect these changes to roll out over months and even years, not all at once.

The idea to add school-zone content to the driver's license exam would need action at the state level. Changing what appears on the test usually involves state agencies and possibly lawmakers. That means it could take time and public input before it becomes real. Still, it is a low-cost change that could shape how every new driver thinks about school zones.

Watch for the school district, the county, and local cities to talk about funding. Big safety plans need budgets. Meetings, votes, and grant money will decide how much of this plan actually gets built. This is a good time for parents to speak up. Public comment at board and council meetings can help move a project up the list, especially for a school near you.

The start of the next school year will be an early test. If you see more officers, fresh paint, and clearer signs near campuses in the fall, that is the plan in action. Keep an eye on your own child's route and note what changes and what does not.

It will also help to track the numbers. The 427 count came from one school year of data. Leaders will want to see if the next year brings that number down. If it does, the plan is working and deserves more funding. If it does not, the group may need to try new steps. Either way, parents should keep asking questions and keep the pressure on. Progress on street safety often depends on people who refuse to let the issue fade after the headlines pass.

Ryan's Take

As a local real estate agent, I look at neighborhoods through the eyes of the families who live in them. Safe school routes are a bigger deal than most buyers say out loud. When I show homes to parents, one of the first questions is often about schools. Right behind that comes the quiet question of how their kids will actually get there.

Streets shape home value in ways that are easy to feel but hard to see on a listing. A home near a calm, well-marked school route tends to hold its value and draw family buyers. A home near a street known for close calls can be a harder sell. Buyers notice fast traffic, missing sidewalks, and blind corners, even if they do not mention it during the tour.

This plan, if it gets built, is good news for family neighborhoods. Protected bike lanes, fresh crosswalks, and calmer traffic near schools make an area more livable. Those upgrades tend to lift buyer confidence over time. If you own a home near one of these routes, changes like these can quietly help your investment while keeping your own kids safer.

My advice is simple. If you are buying near a school, walk the route yourself before you sign. Drive it at drop-off time. See how fast cars move and whether the crossings feel safe. Those few minutes tell you more than any brochure. Safe streets are part of what makes a house a home you can grow into.

What You Can Do

You do not have to wait for the whole plan to help protect your kids. There are steps you can take right now. Start with the ride itself. If your child uses an e-bike or e-scooter, make helmets a firm rule. Check the brakes and lights. Talk about safe speeds and about slowing down near driveways and corners.

Next, walk or ride your child's route with them. Look for the safest crossings, even if they add a few minutes. Point out spots where cars turn or speed up. Teach your child to make eye contact with drivers before crossing, and to never trust that a car will stop just because the light says so.

Get involved with your school and your neighbors. Ask your school about crossing guards and drop-off plans. If a crosswalk near you is faded or a sign is down, report it to the county or your city. These small fixes are exactly what the new plan calls for, and squeaky wheels get attention.

Finally, use your voice. Show up to school board or city council meetings when traffic safety is on the agenda. Public comment moves projects. If a dangerous corner near your home gets named, it is more likely to get fixed. Your five minutes at a meeting could help protect kids for years.
