Bail Raised to $100K for Driver Accused of Killing CCSD Special-Ed Teacher

A Clark County judge has raised bail to $100,000 for the man accused of causing a crash that killed a Clark County School District special education teacher. The driver, Zachary Zwick, 28, now faces an upgraded charge of reckless driving resulting in death after prosecutors said a final crash-data report showed he was going 103 mph in a 45 mph zone on Boulder Highway.

This case matters to Las Vegas families for two reasons. First, it is about a teacher who spent his career helping students who needed the most support. Jeffrey Buchbaum was 55 years old. His death has hit the school community hard. Second, the crash happened on Boulder Highway, one of the busiest and most dangerous roads in the valley. That road runs past homes, bus stops, and shopping centers that thousands of people use every day. When a road earns a reputation for deadly speed, it changes how neighbors feel about their own streets. It can even change how buyers look at homes nearby.

In this article we will walk through what happened, why the higher bail is a big deal, and what the raised charges mean. We will also look at the bigger picture of road safety along the Boulder Highway corridor and the long plan to redesign it. If you live near this stretch of road, or you are thinking about buying or selling a home in the area, this story touches your daily life in real ways.

What Happened

The crash took place in May on Boulder Highway in Las Vegas. According to prosecutors, Zachary Zwick, 28, was driving at a very high speed when the wreck occurred. A final crash-data report showed that his vehicle was traveling 103 mph. The posted speed limit on that part of the road is 45 mph. That means he was going more than twice the legal limit. At that speed, a driver has almost no time to react, and any crash becomes far more likely to be deadly.

Jeffrey Buchbaum, 55, was killed in the crash. He was a special education teacher for the Clark County School District. Special education teachers work with students who have learning needs, physical needs, or behavior needs. These teachers often build strong bonds with the children and families they serve. Losing one is a hard blow for any school. The district community has been mourning his death since the spring.

Two other passengers were also hurt in the crash. Court records describe their injuries as substantial bodily harm. That is a legal term used in Nevada to describe serious injuries, such as broken bones or wounds that create a lasting problem. In plain words, more than one life was changed forever by what happened that day, not just one.

When the case first started, Zwick faced charges tied to the crash. But once investigators finished the crash-data report and confirmed the 103 mph speed, prosecutors moved to upgrade the charge. He is now charged with reckless driving resulting in death. In Nevada, that charge can carry a sentence of up to 20 years in prison. That is a much more serious level than a basic traffic case, and it reflects how the speed data changed the picture.

At the hearing on July 9, 2026, a judge agreed to raise Zwick's bail to $100,000. Bail is the amount of money a person must post to be released from jail while a case moves through the courts. Judges set bail based on how serious the case is and how likely the person is to show up for future court dates. Raising the amount to $100,000 signals that the court now views this as a very serious matter. The case was set to continue in court after that hearing.

You might wonder why a real estate expert is writing about a court case. The reason is simple. Roads, safety, and schools are three of the biggest things that shape where people want to live and how much homes are worth. This one story touches all three at the same time.

Boulder Highway is not a small side street. It is a major road that stretches for miles across the east and southeast parts of the valley. It carries a huge amount of traffic every day. It also runs right past neighborhoods, apartment complexes, bus stops, and stores. Many families walk near it to reach a store, a job, or a bus. When drivers treat a busy road like a race track, the danger does not stay on the road. It spreads to everyone who lives, works, and walks nearby.

For homeowners, road safety is part of the value of a property. Buyers ask real questions when they tour a home. Is the street safe for kids? Can I cross the road to the store without fear? Is there a lot of speeding at night? When a corridor becomes known for deadly crashes, some buyers get nervous. That can slow down sales or push offers a little lower in the blocks closest to the trouble spots. On the other hand, when a road gets safer over time, the opposite can happen. Calmer traffic and better crossings can make a neighborhood more appealing.

Schools matter here too. CCSD is the largest employer in Southern Nevada, and it is by far the largest school district in the state. When a teacher is killed, the loss ripples out to students, families, and coworkers across the valley. Special education teachers are already in short supply across the country. Every one of them who is lost leaves a gap that is hard to fill. Families who care about strong schools pay attention to news like this. There is also a plain human reason this matters. Behind the legal terms is a man who went to work to help children learn, and who did not come home. Two other people were badly hurt. When we talk about speed limits and road design, we are really talking about keeping our neighbors alive. That is worth our attention no matter where you live in Clark County.

Background and History

Boulder Highway has a long and complicated history. It is one of the oldest major roads in the valley. For decades it served as a main route between Las Vegas and the Boulder City area. It was built in an era when the goal was to move cars quickly, not to protect people walking or biking. Over the years, homes, motels, and shopping centers grew up along its edges. But the road itself stayed wide, fast, and hard to cross on foot.

Because of that design, Boulder Highway has been one of the most dangerous roads in the region for years. Traffic safety groups and local leaders have pointed to it many times as a place where too many people are hurt or killed. The mix of high speeds, wide lanes, and many people crossing on foot has made it a repeat trouble spot. This crash is a painful example of a problem that safety experts have warned about for a long time.

To fix these problems, the Regional Transportation Commission of Southern Nevada has been leading a project called Reimagine Boulder Highway. The plan is to change the road from a fast, car-first route into a safer street that works for everyone. The idea is to add features like better crossings, safer bus stops, more lighting, trees, and separated paths for people walking and biking. The goal is to slow traffic down in a smart way and to give people safe places to cross. This case does not exist in a vacuum. It fits into a wider push across Clark County to make streets safer, especially near schools and busy corridors. The district and local traffic teams have worked on safety plans for years, and speeding remains one of the hardest problems to solve. A road can be redesigned, but drivers still have to choose to slow down. That is the part no plan can fully control.

What Happens Next

For the court case, the next steps will play out over the coming months. With bail set at $100,000, Zwick must post that amount to be released while the case continues. If he cannot, he stays in custody. The case will move through the normal steps of the Nevada court system. That usually means more hearings, the sharing of evidence between both sides, and then either a plea deal or a trial. Because the charge is reckless driving resulting in death, which can carry up to 20 years in prison, this is a high-stakes case for everyone involved.

It is important to remember that a charge is not the same as a conviction. Zwick has the right to a fair process, and the state must prove its case. The crash-data report showing 103 mph is strong evidence, but the courts will still weigh everything before any final outcome. We will follow the case and share updates as the court schedule moves forward.

On the road-safety side, the Reimagine Boulder Highway project is expected to keep moving through its planning and building stages. Big road projects take years, and they often happen in pieces. Some parts may get new crossings and lighting sooner, while other parts wait for funding. Neighbors along the corridor may see construction, lane shifts, and detours as the work goes on. In the long run, the goal is a road where a crash like this is far less likely.

Local leaders and safety groups will likely use this case as one more reason to push the project forward and to add more enforcement against extreme speeding. Cases that draw strong public attention often lead to calls for change, both in how roads are built and in how speed laws are enforced.
