Housing Bill Would Bar Large Institutional Investors From Buying More Homes

A big change may be coming for anyone trying to buy a home in Las Vegas. Congress just passed a new law called the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act. Nevada Congresswoman Susie Lee led the push for it. Inside this large bill is one rule that could reshape our local market. It would stop the biggest housing investors from buying more single-family homes. To be exact, any company that already holds 350 or more homes would be barred from buying additional single-family houses.

This matters a lot here. Reporting has found that corporate investors already own roughly one-quarter of the homes in the Las Vegas metro area. That is a huge share. When one out of every four houses is owned by a big firm instead of a family, everyday buyers feel the squeeze. The bill passed the Senate by a vote of 85 to 5 and the House by a vote of 358 to 32. Those are strong, bipartisan numbers. Yet the story is not over. President Trump abruptly canceled the signing ceremony, and the measure was left sitting on his desk. Below, I will break down what happened, why it hits Las Vegas harder than most cities, and what you can do about it as a buyer or homeowner.

What Happened

Congress passed the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act. This is a wide-ranging housing bill, and it covers many topics. But the part getting the most attention is a first-of-its-kind rule aimed at large investors. For the first time at the federal level, the bill sets a broad limit on how many single-family homes big firms can keep buying. The rule targets institutional investors that already hold 350 or more homes. Once a firm crosses that line, it would be barred from buying additional single-family houses.

Nevada Congresswoman Susie Lee championed this effort. She fought to get the investor rule included and to move the full bill through Congress. The votes were not close. The Senate approved it 85 to 5. The House approved it 358 to 32. That kind of margin is rare in Washington today. It shows that lawmakers from both major parties agreed something needed to change about who is buying up America's homes.

The two largest owners named in the reporting are institutional firms called Pretium and Invitation Homes. These are not local families or small landlords. They are large companies that own thousands of houses across the country. Under the new rule, firms of that size would no longer be able to add more single-family homes to their piles.

It helps to understand the difference between these firms and a normal buyer. When a family buys a home, they usually need a loan, an inspection, and a few weeks to close. A large investment firm can often skip much of that. It can pay cash, waive checks, and close fast. Sellers love a clean, quick offer, so these firms often win even when a family offers a similar price. Multiply that across hundreds of homes and you can see how a few big companies can gobble up a large slice of a market in just a few years. The 350-home threshold in the bill is meant to catch exactly these giant buyers while leaving small, local landlords alone.

Here is the twist. After both chambers passed the bill, President Trump abruptly canceled the signing ceremony. The measure was left on his desk instead of being signed right away. That means the investor rule is not law yet. A bill only becomes law once the President signs it, or in some cases if he lets it sit for a set number of days without acting. So for now, the future of this rule is uncertain. The vote was clear, but the final step has not happened. This is an important detail. It is easy to hear "Congress passed it" and think the change is already in place. It is not, at least not as of this writing.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Las Vegas is one of the cities where this rule could matter most. Corporate investors own roughly one-quarter of homes in the Las Vegas metro. Think about your own street. If you have twenty houses on it, that math suggests around five could be owned by big firms rather than families. Those homes often become rentals. When a house becomes a corporate rental, it usually stops showing up on the market for regular buyers for a long time.

The pressure has grown recently. In the third quarter of 2025, investors accounted for 34 percent of all single-family home purchases statewide. That was the highest share in five years. So more than one out of every three homes sold in Nevada went to an investor, not to a family planning to live there. When you are a first-time buyer trying to win a house, you are often bidding against a company with deep pockets and all-cash offers. That is a hard fight to win.

There is another local angle worth naming. Las Vegas has a lot of working families who rent because they cannot win a home to buy. When big firms own so many houses, they also help set the rent for the whole valley. If those firms keep growing, rents tend to climb, and it gets harder to save for a down payment. So this is not only a buyer issue. It touches renters too. A cap on the largest owners could ease that pressure from both sides over time, giving renters a better shot at one day owning and giving buyers a fairer chance at the homes that come up for sale.

This is why the 350-home cap could change things for local buyers. If the biggest firms are barred from buying more single-family homes, they step back from the bidding. That could open up more entry-level houses for families, teachers, nurses, casino workers, and young couples who have been priced out. It could also slow the fast rise in rents, since fewer homes would be pulled off the ownership market and turned into corporate rentals.

I want to be fair and clear here. This rule would not force any firm to sell the homes it already owns. It only stops the largest holders from buying more. So the change would be gradual, not overnight. Big investors would still own a large share of our housing for years. But over time, if they are no longer adding to their count, everyday buyers would face less competition on the homes that do come up for sale. In a market as tight as ours, even a small shift in competition can make the difference between winning a home and losing it.

Background and History

To understand this bill, it helps to know how we got here. After the housing crash around 2008, home prices in Las Vegas fell hard. Many houses went into foreclosure. Large investment firms saw a chance. They bought up thousands of cheap homes across the valley and turned them into rentals. Las Vegas became one of the top targets in the country for this kind of buying. That is a big reason corporate investors now own such a large share of our homes.

Over the years, that share kept growing. Firms like Invitation Homes and Pretium built giant portfolios of single-family rentals. For a while, few rules stood in their way. A family buying one home had to compete with a company buying hundreds at a time. Local leaders and residents grew frustrated. They watched starter homes get scooped up by out-of-state firms, then rented back to the very people who wanted to own them.

Until now, there was no broad federal law limiting this. Some states and cities tried their own smaller steps, but nothing at the national level took direct aim at the largest buyers. That is what makes the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act stand out. It includes the first broad federal restriction on institutional investors of this type. The 350-home threshold is a way to separate the truly large firms from small local landlords. A person who owns two or three rental homes is not the target. The rule is built for companies holding hundreds or thousands of houses. This history is why the strong bipartisan votes matter so much. Lawmakers from both parties saw the same problem in their home states, and Las Vegas was one of the clearest examples of it.

What Happens Next

The next step is the most important one, and it is not settled. Because President Trump canceled the signing ceremony, the bill is sitting on his desk. There are a few paths from here. He could still sign it into law. He could refuse to sign it, which is called a veto. Or the bill could become law without his signature if he takes no action for a set period, depending on how the timing works. Until one of those things happens, the investor rule is on hold. [NOT VERIFIED] The exact deadline for the President to act on this specific bill was not stated in the reporting I reviewed.

If it does become law, there would likely be a period before the rules fully take effect. Federal agencies usually need time to write the fine print and explain how a new rule will be enforced. So even in the best case for buyers, you should not expect big investors to disappear from the market next week. The change would roll out over months, not days.

If the bill stalls or gets vetoed, the current situation continues. Big firms could keep buying single-family homes in Clark County with no federal cap. Investors could remain a large share of buyers, as they were at 34 percent statewide in the third quarter of 2025. I will keep watching this closely and update you as the story moves. This is one of those rare policy changes that could touch the price and supply of the exact homes my clients are trying to buy, so it is worth following week by week.

There is also a chance the rule changes shape even if it moves forward. When a big bill becomes law, agencies decide the details of how it is enforced. They will need to define how the 350-home count is measured, how often it is checked, and what happens if a firm gets close to the line. Those choices can make a rule stronger or weaker in practice. For example, a firm might try to spread its homes across many smaller companies to stay under the count. How the government handles moves like that will decide how much the rule really helps buyers. I mention this so you know the headline number is only the start of the story.

My best advice is to plan for both outcomes. If the rule takes hold, buyers may find a little more room over the coming year. If it does not, the market stays as competitive as it has been. Either way, being prepared beats guessing. Buyers who are ready with financing and a clear plan will do well no matter which path this bill takes.
