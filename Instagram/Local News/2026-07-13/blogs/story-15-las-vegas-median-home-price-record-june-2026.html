Las Vegas Median Home Price Holds at Record $490,000 in June

The price of a typical home in Las Vegas is still sitting at the top of the mountain. Las Vegas Realtors, the local group that tracks home sales, reported that the median price of an existing single-family home sold in June was $490,000. That is exactly the same as the all-time high set back in May. It is also up 1.0 percent from June of last year. In plain words, home prices did not fall, and they did not really climb either. They just held steady at the highest point we have ever seen in Clark County.

The median is the middle price. Half of the homes sold for more than $490,000, and half sold for less. It is a good way to see the whole market at once, better than picking one house or one street. When that middle number stops moving, it tells us something important. It means the fast run-up in prices has slowed down to a crawl. For anyone who owns a home here, is trying to buy one, or is thinking about selling, this is a big deal. Let us walk through exactly what the June report said, what it means for regular families in Southern Nevada, and what smart buyers and sellers should do right now.

What Happened

Las Vegas Realtors, sometimes called GLVAR or LVR, put out its monthly report on the local housing market. The report covers June 2026. Here are the numbers that matter most.

The median price of an existing single-family home that sold in June was $490,000. That number did not change at all from May. Both months tied for the highest median price ever recorded in the valley. Compared to June of 2025, the price is up 1.0 percent. That is a very small gain. For years, Las Vegas saw prices jump by much bigger amounts each year. A 1.0 percent rise over a full year shows the market has cooled off from those wild days.

Sales were busy, though. A total of 2,823 existing homes, condos, and townhomes sold during the month. That covers every type of resale property, not just houses. The most eye-catching number was in single-family home sales. Those were up 18.3 percent compared to June of last year. That is a large jump. It tells us that even with prices at record levels, plenty of people are still buying houses here. Demand did not dry up.

The other key number is supply. Southern Nevada had about 3.1 months of housing supply. Supply is measured in months. It answers a simple question: if no new homes came onto the market, how long would it take to sell everything that is for sale right now, at the current pace of sales? At 3.1 months, the answer is a little over three months.

Why does that matter? Real estate agents often use a rough rule. About six months of supply is thought of as a balanced market, where buyers and sellers have about equal power. Less than six months usually favors sellers. More than six months usually favors buyers. At 3.1 months, Las Vegas is still a seller-friendly market on paper. But that number has been slowly growing. More homes have been coming up for sale. So even though sellers still hold an edge, that edge is not as sharp as it was a year or two ago. The market is drifting toward the middle.

It also helps to remember what the report does not say. The 1.0 percent yearly gain is a valley-wide average across every neighborhood. Some parts of town went up more than that, and some went down. A record median does not mean every home rose in value. It means the middle of the whole market landed at $490,000. Your own street could be doing better or worse than the headline. That is why looking at the big number alone can fool you if you are pricing one specific house.

Put the pieces together and you get a clear picture. Prices are at a record but flat. Sales are strong. Supply is low but rising. This is a market that is full of activity but no longer racing higher on price. That mix rewards people who plan carefully and punishes people who assume the old boom rules still apply.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

These numbers are not just for people who work in real estate. They touch almost every household in Clark County in one way or another. Let us break down who feels this and how.

If you already own your home, a record-high median is good news for your bottom line. The value you have built up in your house, called equity, is likely near its peak. If you bought a few years ago, you are probably sitting on a nice gain. That equity can help you move up to a bigger home, downsize to something easier to care for, or borrow against your house for repairs or a family need. A flat price also means you are not likely to lose value in the near term. Your biggest investment is holding its ground.

If you are trying to buy your first home, the picture is harder. A $490,000 middle price is a lot of money for a working family. To afford the payment on a home near that price, a household usually needs a solid income and a good chunk saved for a down payment. Rising interest rates over the past few years have made monthly payments even bigger. So the record price is a real wall for many renters who want to become owners. The good news buried in the report is that supply is rising. More homes for sale means more choices and a little more room to negotiate than buyers had before.

If you are a renter, home prices still reach into your life. When buying is out of reach for many people, more of them keep renting. That keeps demand for rentals high, which can push rents up. But a flat home market can also slow the pace of rent increases over time, because the panic to buy before prices climb again has faded.

And if you are thinking about selling, the flat median is a warning as much as a reward. Your home may be worth more than ever, but buyers are not in a frenzy anymore. They are careful. They compare. With supply growing, your home has more competition on the market. Price it too high and it may just sit there. That leads us to the history behind these numbers and where they came from.

Background and History

To understand why a flat record price is such a big deal, it helps to look back at the road Las Vegas has traveled. The valley has one of the most up-and-down housing markets in the whole country. It has seen giant booms and painful busts.

During the housing crash around 2008 and the years right after, home values in Las Vegas fell hard. Many families owed more on their homes than the homes were worth. It took years for the market to heal. Slowly, through the 2010s, prices climbed back. Then came a huge surge in the early 2020s. Low interest rates and a wave of people moving to Nevada from higher-cost states pushed prices up fast. Homes that sold for one price in the spring might sell for thousands more by fall.

That kind of speed cannot last forever. Interest rates rose to cool down the whole economy. Higher rates make monthly mortgage payments bigger, which shrinks how much buyers can pay. That took some of the heat out of Las Vegas. Instead of prices crashing, though, they mostly leveled off near the top. The record $490,000 median we see now is the result of that leveling. Prices did not fall back to earth. They just stopped climbing so fast.

The rise in single-family sales, up 18.3 percent from last year, shows that buyers adjusted to the new normal. Many people who waited on the sidelines, hoping for a big price drop, finally decided to jump in. Life events like new jobs, growing families, and retirements do not wait forever. At the same time, more owners decided the time was right to list their homes, which is why supply crept up toward 3.1 months. This mix of steady prices, active buyers, and growing choices is what shapes the market today.

What Happens Next

No one can say for sure where prices go from here, and anyone who claims to know for certain is guessing. But we can watch a few signposts that usually point the way.

The first signpost is interest rates. Mortgage rates have the biggest short-term effect on what buyers can afford. If rates ease even a little, monthly payments shrink, and more buyers can jump into the market. That extra demand could push prices up again or at least keep them firm. If rates rise, the opposite happens. Fewer buyers can afford the payment, and sellers may have to trim prices to make a deal.

The second signpost is supply. Right now Southern Nevada sits near 3.1 months. If that number keeps climbing toward five or six months, the market would shift more in favor of buyers. More homes for sale means sellers must compete harder, and that usually means softer prices and more deals. If supply stays low, sellers keep their edge and the record price likely holds.

The third signpost is jobs and people. Las Vegas keeps growing. New residents and new jobs bring fresh demand for housing. As long as people keep moving here and finding work, there will be steady pressure under home prices. A strong local economy is a floor that helps keep a crash from happening.

There is also the season to keep in mind. Summer is usually one of the busier stretches for home sales, since many families like to move before a new school year starts. As fall and winter arrive, sales often slow down a bit, and homes can take longer to sell. That does not mean prices fall. It just means the pace cools with the weather. So do not read too much into one slower month later this year if it comes. The bigger trends of rates, supply, and jobs matter far more than any single month.

My best read is that we are in for a stretch of steady, slow motion rather than a sharp swing in either direction. A record but flat median, strong sales, and slowly rising supply all point to a market that is finding its balance. That is actually a healthier place to be than the wild boom of a few years back. Steady markets are easier to plan around for both buyers and sellers.

Ryan's Take

Here is how I see it as a broker who works these neighborhoods every week. A record price that stops moving changes the game for both sides, and most people have not caught up to it yet.

If you are selling, the days of naming a wild price and getting it are over for now. Buyers are patient, and with supply rising they have more homes to pick from. The homes that sell fast and for top dollar are the ones priced right on day one and shown in great shape. Overprice by even a little and your home can sit while fresher listings pass it by. A home that sits too long often ends up selling for less than if it had been priced correctly from the start. Get the number right early.

If you are buying, do not wait for a crash that may never come. Prices are flat, sales are strong, and the local economy is steady. That is not the setup for a big drop. But you do have more room to negotiate than buyers had a year ago, and more choices on the shelf. Use that. Ask for repairs, closing help, or a fair price on a home that has been listed a while. The power has shifted just enough to give careful buyers real leverage. My advice for both sides is the same: know the real numbers for your exact street and price honestly.

What You Can Do

You do not have to guess your way through a market like this. Here are simple, useful steps you can take today, whether you own, rent, or are ready to make a move.

  • Learn your home's real value. The valley-wide median is $490,000, but your home is not average. Its worth depends on your zip code, your street, your square footage, and your home's condition. Ask for a free, honest home value review before you make any plans.
  • Check your equity. If you have owned for a few years, you may have more equity than you think. That money can help you move up, downsize, or handle a big life change.
  • Get your budget straight before you shop. If you want to buy, talk to a lender first and find out your real monthly payment at today's rates. Knowing your number keeps you from falling for a home you cannot comfortably afford.
  • Do not chase headlines. A record price does not mean you missed your chance, and a flat market does not mean a crash is coming. Base your decision on your own life, your job, and your family, not on fear.
  • Watch supply and rates. Keep an eye on whether more homes come up for sale and where mortgage rates head. Those two things will shape your next best move.
