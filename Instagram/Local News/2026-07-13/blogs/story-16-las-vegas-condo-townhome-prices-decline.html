Las Vegas Condo and Townhome Prices Slide 4.3% Year Over Year

Condo and townhome prices in Las Vegas went down over the past year, and that is real news for anyone trying to buy their first home. In the June report from the Greater Las Vegas Association of Realtors, the median price for local condos and townhomes was $292,000. That is down 4.3 percent from June 2025. It is also well below the record high of $315,000 that this part of the market hit back in October 2024. So while single-family house prices keep pushing to new highs, the smaller and cheaper homes are moving the other way.

This split matters. For years, almost every kind of home in the valley got more expensive at the same time. Now the market is telling two different stories at once. Houses are still hot, but condos and townhomes have cooled off. That is unusual, and it opens a door for buyers who felt locked out. A condo or townhome is often the cheapest way to stop renting and start owning. When that piece of the market softens, budget buyers get a little more room to breathe.

Below we break down exactly what the June numbers show, why they matter to families across Clark County, how we got here, and what may happen next. We also share what you can do right now if you are watching this market and wondering if this is your moment.

What Happened

The Greater Las Vegas Association of Realtors, often called GLVAR, puts out a report every month. It uses sales data from the local Multiple Listing Service, which is the shared system real estate agents use to list and track homes. The June report came out in early July 2026, and it showed a clear split in the market.

Here are the key facts. The median price of local condos and townhomes in June was $292,000. That is down 4.3 percent compared to June 2025. The median is the middle price, so half of the condos and townhomes sold for more than that and half sold for less. This median is also below the record high of $315,000 that the segment reached in October 2024. In other words, condo and townhome prices have given back some of their gains and are now sitting a good bit under their peak.

Sales of condos and townhomes did rise, but only a little. They were up just 1.2 percent from the year before. That is a small move. It tells us that buyers are still active in this part of the market, but they are not rushing in. Demand is steady, not hot.

The report also showed how long these homes take to sell. Condos and townhomes spent an average of 55 days on the market. Single-family houses, by comparison, spent about 40 days on the market. That is a real gap. Homes that sit longer usually give buyers more power to ask for a lower price or extra help with closing costs. When something takes 55 days to sell, the seller often becomes more willing to make a deal.

Put these numbers together and a picture forms. Prices are down year over year. Sales are barely up. Homes are taking longer to sell. Each of these signals points the same way. The condo and townhome market has shifted toward buyers. That is very different from the single-family house market, where prices in the valley are still hovering near record highs and homes move faster.

It is worth saying clearly what a 4.3 percent drop means in dollars. On a $305,000 condo, a 4.3 percent drop is about $13,000. That is not pocket change for a first-time buyer. It can mean a smaller down payment, a lower monthly loan, or the difference between qualifying for a home and not qualifying at all.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

For a lot of people in Clark County, a condo or townhome is the front door to owning a home. These places usually cost less than a stand-alone house. They often need less yard work. Many first-time buyers, young families, and folks looking to downsize start their search here. So when this part of the market gets cheaper, it touches a very large group of local residents.

Think about a renter who has been paying more each year on a lease. For that person, the gap between renting and owning is what stops them. A lower condo price shrinks that gap. It can bring the monthly cost of owning closer to what they already pay in rent. When prices fall 4.3 percent and homes sit on the market for 55 days, buyers gain leverage. They can negotiate. They can ask for repairs. They can request help with closing costs. A year or two ago, in a red-hot market, that kind of asking often got you nowhere.

This also matters for current owners. If you own a condo or townhome and you are thinking about selling, these numbers are a heads-up. Your unit may take longer to sell than a single-family house down the street. You may need to price it carefully from day one. Homes that are priced too high tend to sit, and homes that sit tend to sell for less in the end. A smart price and good photos matter more now than they did during the frenzy.

There is another group to keep in mind. Some buyers use condos and townhomes as rental property. When purchase prices drop but rents stay steady, the math on a rental can look better. That said, condos often come with monthly homeowners association fees, and those fees can change. Anyone buying for investment needs to read the association budget closely. [NOT VERIFIED] The report did not break out how association fees have moved this year, so buyers should check each building on its own.

Finally, this split market is a reminder that "Las Vegas real estate" is not one single thing. The valley has many markets inside it. Houses and condos can move in opposite directions at the same time. Where you look, and what type of home you look at, changes your whole experience as a buyer or seller. A headline that says home prices are near record highs is true for houses, and at the very same moment a condo shopper can find prices that are down from a year ago. Both things are real. Knowing which market you are actually in is what keeps you from making a costly mistake.

Background and History

To understand today, it helps to look back. Condo and townhome prices in the Las Vegas area climbed for a long stretch. The segment hit its record high median of $315,000 in October 2024. That was the top of the mountain for this part of the market. Since then, the median has drifted down to the current $292,000. That is a drop of about $23,000 from the peak, or roughly 7 percent below the record high.

Why did prices climb so much in the first place? A few reasons. The Las Vegas valley kept growing. People moved here from other states looking for lower costs and no state income tax. At the same time, mortgage rates were very low for part of that period, which let buyers afford more. When money is cheap to borrow, buyers can bid higher, and prices rise. Builders also could not put up new homes fast enough to keep up with demand. Fewer homes plus more buyers equals higher prices.

Then rates went up. Higher mortgage rates make monthly payments larger, and that cools buyer demand. Condos and townhomes feel this shift in a special way. Many condo buyers are first-time buyers who are stretching to afford anything at all. When payments rise, these buyers are the first to step back. That is one reason the condo and townhome segment softened while single-family houses, often bought by move-up buyers with more cash, held firmer.

There is also the matter of loans. Some condo buildings can be harder to finance than a single-family house. Certain loan programs have rules about how many units in a building can be rentals, or whether the homeowners association is on solid financial footing. When financing is trickier, the pool of buyers shrinks, and that can hold prices down. This has long been part of the condo story, not just in Las Vegas but across the country. [NOT VERIFIED] The June report did not detail lending rule changes, so this is general market context rather than a new finding.

So the current dip is not a shock out of nowhere. It is the result of higher rates meeting the most price-sensitive buyers in the market. Prices ran up fast, then the cheapest segment gave a little back.

What Happens Next

No one can predict the market with perfect accuracy, so treat what follows as a careful read, not a promise. Still, the June data points to a few likely paths.

First, watch mortgage rates. Rates are the single biggest lever on the condo and townhome segment. If rates ease, monthly payments fall, and first-time buyers can come back into the market. That extra demand could firm up prices or even nudge them back up. If rates stay high or climb, the segment could stay soft, and homes could keep taking longer to sell. Because condo buyers are so sensitive to payment size, this part of the market may react to rate moves faster than single-family houses do.

Second, watch the days on market number. Right now condos and townhomes take about 55 days to sell, versus 40 for houses. If that gap gets wider, it means sellers are having a harder time, and buyers will keep gaining leverage. If the gap shrinks, it means the segment is heating back up. This one number is an easy way to feel the market's pulse each month.

Third, watch supply. If builders and current owners list a lot of condos and townhomes at once, more choices for buyers can keep prices in check. If listings stay tight, even soft demand can hold prices steady. Supply and demand always push against each other.

My honest read is that the condo and townhome segment is likely to stay buyer-friendly in the near term, as long as rates remain high. That does not mean prices will crash. A 4.3 percent dip is a shift, not a collapse. It means the balance of power has moved toward buyers for now. Windows like this do not stay open forever. When rates ease, the crowd that has been waiting on the sidelines tends to rush back, and soft prices can firm up quickly.

It also helps to keep the bigger picture in mind. Even with this dip, condos and townhomes are still a major part of how the valley grows. New residents keep arriving, and many of them start out looking for an affordable place to land. That steady flow of new buyers gives the segment a floor. So while the near-term looks soft, the longer-term demand for lower-cost homes in Clark County is not going away. The question is mostly about timing and about where rates settle.

Ryan's Take

Here is how I see it. For a first-time or budget buyer in Clark County, this is one of the more interesting openings I have seen in a while. Single-family house prices are still near record highs, so those keep getting harder to reach. But the condo and townhome side has softened. Prices are down 4.3 percent year over year, and these homes are sitting for about 55 days. That combination hands real bargaining power to buyers who are ready.

What would I watch if I were shopping right now? Three things. First, the homeowners association. Read the budget, ask about the reserve fund, and find out if any big repairs or special charges are coming. A cheap price with a shaky association is not really a deal. Second, financing. Ask your loan officer early whether the specific building qualifies for the loan you want, because not every condo is easy to finance. Third, days on market. If a unit has been listed a while, you may have room to negotiate on price or ask the seller to cover some closing costs.

I am not telling anyone to rush. I am saying the door is open a little wider than it has been. Soft markets do not last forever. When rates come down, buyers tend to pour back in, and quiet windows close fast. If owning a home has been your goal, this is a good time to run the numbers and get ready, so you can move when the right unit shows up.

What You Can Do

If this market has your attention, here are simple steps to take. None of them cost much, and each one puts you in a stronger spot.

Get your budget clear first. Before you fall in love with a place, talk to a lender and get pre-approved. Pre-approval tells you the real price range you can afford, and it shows sellers you are serious. In a market where homes sit for 55 days, a strong, ready buyer stands out.

Learn the homeowners association rules for any condo or townhome you like. Ask for the monthly fee, the reserve fund balance, and any planned special charges. Ask how many units are rentals, since that can affect your loan. A little homework here saves big surprises later.

Track the numbers each month. Keep an eye on the median price, sales counts, and days on market. When you know the trend, you can tell a fair price from a high one, and you can negotiate with confidence.

Do not chase every listing. In a buyer-friendly market you can be patient and picky. Wait for the right unit, then use the softer conditions to ask for a fair price and helpful terms.
