Las Vegas Approves $17.45 Million Sale of Desert Pines Golf Course for 1,500 New Homes

The Las Vegas City Council has approved a roughly $17.45 million land deal and a development agreement that will turn the Desert Pines Golf Course in east Las Vegas into a new neighborhood with more than 1,500 homes. The nearly 100 acre course closed for good in mid-July 2026 after about 30 years, and the site is set to become one of the largest housing projects on the east side in years.

The buyer is developer McCormack Baron Salazar, and the full plan carries a price tag of about $500 million. It calls for a mixed income neighborhood spread across 17 parcels, along with retail space and a workforce training center built with the College of Southern Nevada. For a part of town that has waited a long time for new investment, this is a very big deal.

What Happened

The Las Vegas City Council signed off on two key items. First, it approved the sale of the city owned Desert Pines Golf Course land for about $17.45 million. Second, it approved a development agreement with McCormack Baron Salazar that spells out how the land can be used and what the developer must build. Together, these votes clear the way for the project to move forward.

The golf course sits on nearly 100 acres in east Las Vegas. It operated for about 30 years before it shut down permanently in mid-July 2026. The land will not sit empty for long. The developer plans a full neighborhood, not just a subdivision of look alike houses. The plan splits the site into 17 parcels, which allows for different home types and price points across the property.

The headline number for residents is the housing. The project promises more than 1,500 homes. It is described as a mixed income neighborhood, which means homes aimed at a range of budgets rather than only high end buyers. That mix matters in a market where prices have climbed fast and many working families feel priced out.

The plan goes beyond homes. It includes retail space so residents can shop and eat close to where they live. It also includes a workforce training center built in partnership with the College of Southern Nevada. That center is meant to help local residents learn skills and find better paying jobs. The total investment is estimated at about $500 million, and groundbreaking is expected in 2027.

It helps to picture the scale of what is coming. Nearly 100 acres is a large piece of land in a built out part of town. Splitting it into 17 parcels lets the developer build in stages and mix home styles, from smaller attached homes to standalone houses. A $500 million investment on a single site is the kind of number that reshapes a neighborhood for a generation, not just for a few years. Deals of this size on city owned land do not come along often in east Las Vegas.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Las Vegas has a housing shortage, and it shows up in the prices people pay every month. When supply is tight, both rents and home prices go up. A project that adds more than 1,500 homes at once puts a real dent in that shortage. It gives buyers and renters more choices, and more choices tend to ease price pressure over time.

The mixed income part is what sets this project apart for everyday families. A lot of new construction in the valley leans toward higher price points. This plan is built to include homes for a range of budgets. For teachers, nurses, service workers, and young families who have watched prices run away from them, a mixed income neighborhood is a rare chance to buy or rent closer to the center of the valley.

Location is a big part of the value here. East Las Vegas is close to downtown, the medical district, and major roads. Many workers in these areas have long commutes because they cannot afford to live nearby. New homes on the old golf course could shorten those drives. Shorter commutes mean more time at home, lower gas costs, and less traffic on the roads everyone shares.

There is also the jobs angle. The workforce training center with the College of Southern Nevada is not just a nice add on. It gives residents a place to build skills right in their own neighborhood. Pair that with new retail jobs and construction work, and the project could lift the whole area, not just the people who move into the new homes.

For current homeowners near the site, change on this scale usually points one way over time. New homes, new stores, and fresh investment tend to pull up the value of the older homes around them. Buyers who could not get into a brand new house may still want to live near one, and that demand can lift prices on nearby resale homes. There can be short term bumps from construction traffic and noise, but the long term direction for the area looks positive.

Renters have a stake here too. When a market adds homes at different price points, it gives renters more room to breathe and, for some, a real path to owning. A mixed income plan is not a promise that every home will be cheap, but it is a signal that the project is meant to serve regular working households and not just the top of the market. In a valley where so many renters feel stuck, that signal counts.

Background and History

Golf courses have been part of the Las Vegas landscape for decades. Many were built when land was cheap and water worries were smaller. Desert Pines opened and ran for about 30 years, and for a long time it was a green space in the heart of the east side. Over the years, though, the math on golf courses in the desert has changed. They use a lot of water and a lot of land, and both are now in short supply.

At the same time, Southern Nevada's housing crunch has only grown. The region keeps adding people, but it does not add homes fast enough to keep up. Much of the valley is boxed in by federal land, mountains, and other limits, so there are only so many places to build. That has pushed leaders to look hard at land already inside the city that could hold more homes.

Closing a golf course to build housing fits that trend. It takes a large piece of land that is already served by roads, water lines, and power, and it puts that land to a use the region needs more. City owned land like Desert Pines gives leaders a direct way to steer what gets built. By selling to a developer under a detailed agreement, the city can require a mix of home prices, retail, and community features instead of leaving it all to the market.

This is not the only place the valley is trying to squeeze out more homes. Clark County has moved to nominate federal land for affordable housing, and North Las Vegas has advanced large new communities like KB Home's Village 2 at Tule Springs. The Desert Pines deal is part of a bigger push across the region to find land and build on it before the shortage gets worse.

The nostalgia is real, and it is worth naming. For about 30 years, Desert Pines was a place where people learned the game, played weekend rounds, and marked birthdays and reunions. Losing a course that has been part of the east side for that long stings for the golfers who loved it. At the same time, the desert makes hard choices unavoidable. Water is scarce and getting scarcer, and land that can hold homes is running low. When a city has to choose between a thirsty golf course and 1,500 homes, the housing need is hard to argue against.

What Happens Next

The next big milestone is groundbreaking, which is expected in 2027. Between now and then, the developer will work through design plans, permits, and site prep. A project this size does not rise overnight. Expect the work to roll out in phases across the 17 parcels rather than all at once. That means the neighborhood will fill in over several years, not in a single season.

Residents in east Las Vegas should watch for public updates as the plans get more detailed. Things like the exact home price ranges, how many homes will be for sale versus for rent, and the timeline for the retail and training center will come out as the project moves forward. City meetings and developer announcements are the places to look for those details.

The workforce training center is one part of the plan I would keep a close eye on. Building it with the College of Southern Nevada ties the neighborhood to real job training, and the timing of when it opens will say a lot about how the whole project is going. If the homes, the stores, and the training center come together on a steady schedule, this could become a model for how the valley reuses old land. If pieces slip, residents will want to know why and hold the developer to the plan that was approved.

For now, the golf course is closed and the land is changing hands. The votes by the City Council were the hard part in many ways, because they set the rules and the sale in motion. The coming months will be about turning the approved plan into real drawings, real permits, and eventually real homes on the ground.

There are a few things worth watching closely as the project takes shape. One is how the retail and the training center line up with the housing, because those pieces are what turn a subdivision into a real neighborhood. Another is the pace of the market itself. Interest rates, buyer demand, and building costs can all speed up or slow down a project of this size. A plan approved in 2026 with a 2027 groundbreaking will play out over years, and the details can shift along the way.
