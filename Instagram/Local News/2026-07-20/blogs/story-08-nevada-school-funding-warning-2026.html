Officials Warn Nevada Schools Will Keep Struggling Without New Funding

Nevada's Commission on School Funding is warning that schools across the state, including here in Clark County, will keep struggling unless lawmakers find new money for classrooms. The commission is preparing an updated recommendation to lift Nevada's education funding toward the national average, and the message is blunt. Without more revenue, districts will keep falling behind.

This matters to every family in Las Vegas and Henderson. The Clark County School District is one of the largest in the country, and the money it gets per student shapes class sizes, teacher pay, and the programs your kids can take. Right now the per-pupil allocation for the coming year is $15,362, and the commission says that number needs to climb to keep up with the rest of the nation.

The short version is this. Nevada has been trying to catch up to how much other states spend on each student, but progress has been slow. The commission wants lawmakers to speed it up. This post breaks down exactly what the commission said, why it hits close to home in Clark County, and what you can do about it as a parent, a homeowner, or a future buyer.

What Happened

The Nevada Commission on School Funding met in early July 2026 to shape a new recommendation for state leaders. The Las Vegas Sun reported on July 2, 2026, that the commission is preparing to ask lawmakers to raise education funding until it reaches the national average. The group made it clear that Nevada schools will continue to suffer without new funding sources.

Here are the numbers that drive the warning. The per-pupil allocation for the coming school year sits at $15,362. The commission says the state should aim for $16,043 per pupil in the 2027-28 school year. That is the next short-term target. Looking further out, the commission estimates Nevada would need to spend about $23,191 per pupil by 2037 to reach the middle of the pack nationally.

The gap is real. Reporting tied to the commission's work shows Nevada trails the national average by roughly $3,663 per student. To close that kind of shortfall, the commission has outlined a plan that would cost around $1.9 billion. That is a large ask, and it sets up a tough fight in the next legislative session.

Part of the frustration comes from recent history. In 2025, lawmakers raised the base per-pupil amount by only $2 in one year and $70 in the next. Those increases came in below inflation. In plain terms, the buying power of each school dollar shrank even as the sticker number ticked up. That is why the commission keeps sounding the alarm.

The commission's job is to study the numbers and hand lawmakers a clear target. It is not a group that can spend money on its own. It can only recommend. So when the commission says schools will keep suffering without new revenue, it is putting the choice squarely in front of the Governor and the Legislature. The goal it keeps repeating is simple to state and hard to reach. Get Nevada to at least the national middle for what it spends on each child.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

School funding is not an abstract topic in Clark County. It touches your daily life whether or not you have kids in the district. CCSD teaches more than 300,000 students, and the money that flows to those campuses decides how crowded the classrooms are and how many teachers stay on the job. When funding lags, class sizes grow and programs get cut.

For parents in neighborhoods like Summerlin, Henderson, Spring Valley, and the northwest valley, the stakes are simple. Better funded schools tend to hold onto good teachers. They can offer more electives, tutoring, and support for kids who need extra help. When the money is tight, those are the first things to feel the squeeze, and families notice.

Money also flows unevenly under the new formula, which sends extra dollars to students who cost more to teach. That includes English learners, low-income students, and children with disabilities. Clark County has a large share of these students, so the size of the state's investment matters here even more than in smaller, wealthier districts. When the base amount stays low, the extra help never stretches as far as it should.

There is also a money angle that reaches every homeowner. School quality is one of the biggest drivers of home values in any market, and Las Vegas is no different. Buyers with children shop for homes based on the schools nearby. When a zone has strong, stable schools, demand for homes there stays high. When schools struggle, that can weigh on how fast homes sell and what they sell for.

Renters feel it too. Many families rent while they save to buy, and they choose apartments and rental homes based on the school their kids will attend. A funding crunch that hurts a local campus can push families to move, which shifts demand block by block. In a valley that is growing fast, these choices add up.

Teacher pay is the other piece residents should watch. Nevada has fought to fill open teaching jobs for years, and pay is a big part of why. When the state cannot raise per-pupil funding, districts have less room to compete for teachers with nearby states and with private employers. Empty classrooms get covered by substitutes or by long-term vacancies, and that hits the quality of a child's day right away.

There is a bigger picture for the local economy as well. Companies looking to move to Southern Nevada study the schools before they commit. A strong school system helps recruit employers and the good jobs they bring. When funding falls behind year after year, it becomes one more hurdle in the pitch to grow beyond tourism and gaming. That growth is what keeps our housing market healthy over the long run.

Background and History

Nevada has wrestled with school funding for decades. For years the state used an old system called the Nevada Plan to send money to districts. Critics said it was hard to follow and did not send enough dollars to the students who needed the most help. That long-running complaint set the stage for a big change.

In 2019, lawmakers passed a new approach known as the Pupil-Centered Funding Plan. The idea was to make funding simpler and to send extra dollars for students who cost more to teach, such as English learners, low-income kids, and students with disabilities. The Commission on School Funding was created to guide this shift and to advise the Legislature on how much money schools really need.

The switch took effect in the 2021-22 school year, and the commission has been tracking the results ever since. It reports to the Legislature, studies what schools need, and recommends targets. But it holds no purse strings. That design means the commission can warn and advise all it wants, yet the money only moves when elected leaders vote for it. That gap between advice and action is at the heart of this story.

The commission has repeatedly pointed to the same problem. Nevada spends less per student than most states, and the new formula did not come with enough new money to fix that. Education advocates have called the formula a big step forward in fairness while warning that the funding levels still fall short. That tension is exactly what is playing out again in 2026.

The trend line does show progress, just slow progress. A few years back, the typical per-pupil allocation from the state sat around $14,247 for the 2023-24 school year. This coming year it is $15,362. That is real movement. The catch is that the national average keeps rising too, so Nevada has been climbing a hill that keeps getting taller. That is why the commission frames its targets in future years, all the way out to 2037.

What Happens Next

The commission's updated recommendation is meant to guide the next round of budget talks. Nevada's Legislature meets every two years, so the big funding decisions land in session years. That means the push to reach the national average will run through the Governor's budget and the Legislature, where lawmakers will weigh schools against every other state need.

Watch for the debate over where new money would come from. Raising per-pupil spending by hundreds of dollars, and eventually thousands, requires new revenue. That could mean new or higher taxes, a shift in how existing dollars are spent, or a mix of both. None of those choices are easy, and each one draws strong reactions from taxpayers and business groups.

Nevada also leans heavily on tourism and gaming taxes, which rise and fall with the economy. That makes education funding sensitive to slow years on the Strip. Part of the long-running debate is whether the state should build a steadier base for school money so a downturn does not force cuts in the classroom. Expect that question to come up again as lawmakers weigh the commission's plan.

Closer to home, keep an eye on the CCSD budget. The district builds its yearly spending plan around what the state provides. If the state number stays flat, CCSD faces hard calls on staffing, programs, and pay. If new funding comes through, families could see relief in class sizes and classroom resources. The commission's report is the first domino in that chain.

There is a timing wrinkle worth noting. Because the commission's target of $16,043 is aimed at the 2027-28 school year, the real decision point lands in the next legislative session. Between now and then, expect school groups, parent advocates, and the teachers union to press their case in public. If you want to shape the outcome, the months before the session are when your voice counts the most.

Ryan's Take

As a local real estate expert, I watch school news closely because it moves the housing market more than most people realize. When buyers call me about Summerlin, Green Valley, or the northwest, one of their first questions is almost always about the schools. Strong, well-funded schools help hold and grow home values. A funding gap that drags on can quietly cool demand in the zones that feel it most.

My honest read is that this warning is worth taking seriously, but it is not a reason to panic about buying or selling here. Las Vegas keeps drawing families and jobs, and that demand is not going away. What I tell my clients is to factor school quality into their choice of neighborhood, not just the house itself. If the state finally closes the funding gap, the zones that improve the most could see the biggest lift in buyer interest over the next few years.

I also remind sellers that school ratings can change over time, and buyers do their homework now more than ever. If you own in an area where the schools are strong, that is a selling point you should lean into. If you are shopping, look past the headline warning and study the specific campus your kids would attend. The valley is full of good schools and strong pockets, and a smart buyer can find real value by matching the right neighborhood to their family's needs.

What You Can Do

If school funding matters to you, the most direct step is to follow the Commission on School Funding and share your view with your state lawmakers. The commission's meetings and reports are public through the Nevada Department of Education. When the next legislative session opens, contact your Assembly member and state senator and tell them where you stand on education dollars. Local voices carry weight in these budget fights.

You can also get involved closer to home. Attend a Clark County School Board of Trustees meeting, or watch it online, to see how the district plans to spend what it gets. Parent groups and school community councils are another way to push for the programs your kids need. These smaller rooms often decide how limited dollars are used at your child's campus.

If you are buying or selling a home, do your homework on the schools in each zone you consider. Look at the specific campus, not just the general area, since quality can vary block by block in the valley. Knowing the school picture helps you make a smart long-term choice, and it is one of the biggest factors in how a home holds its value over time.

It also helps to stay informed year round, not just at election time. Sign up for updates from CCSD and from your child's school so you hear about budget votes and program changes before they happen. When the community shows up and pays attention, districts and lawmakers listen. Small, steady involvement often does more than a single big push.

Finally, talk to a local expert when a decision ties into your home. School zones, boundary changes, and neighborhood trends all shape what a property is worth today and years from now.
