Boulder City Votes to Fight a Data Center on Public Land That Could Use Three Times Its Own Energy

The Boulder City Council voted unanimously on Tuesday, July 14, 2026, to appeal the federal government's approval of a large data center on public land just outside town. The Bureau of Land Management, or BLM, signed off on the Townsite Data Center on June 26, and city leaders say the project could draw far more power than the entire city uses today.

The site sits on about 80 to 81 acres of federal land in the Eldorado Valley, roughly three miles southwest of Boulder City homes. Council members worry about strain on local power and water, and they are upset the BLM approved the plan without asking the city first. Officials plan to file their notice of appeal before July 27.

What Happened

On July 14, 2026, the Boulder City Council took a rare and forceful step. Every member voted to appeal the BLM's decision to allow Townsite Solar 2 LLC to build a data center on federal land inside the city's own jurisdiction. The appeal now heads to the Interior Board of Land Appeals, a federal body known as the IBLA that reviews land decisions made by the BLM.

The BLM approved the project on June 26. What surprised many residents is how the approval happened. The land had already been cleared back in 2023 for a very different use. Townsite Solar 2 first got federal approval to build a solar power and battery storage facility that would draw about 19 megawatts of power. In 2025, the company asked to amend that grant and build a data center instead. Rather than order a new environmental review, the BLM transferred the older 2023 study to the new plan. Officials decided the data center was close enough to the original solar project that a fresh review was not needed.

The BLM defended that call. The agency said the data center is essentially like the original solar project because both are industrial uses that are mostly electrical in nature. Both would sit on the same acreage, use the same footprint, and connect to outside power through the same utility corridor. To the BLM, that made the two projects similar enough to reuse the older review. To Boulder City, a jump from 19 megawatts to 167 megawatts is not a small tweak. It is a completely different scale of project with very different demands on the land, the grid, and the water supply.

The numbers tell a different story. The original solar plan called for about 19 megawatts. The approved data center could demand up to 167 megawatts of power. That is a huge jump. The project would also include a battery energy storage system, an electrical substation, and biodiesel emergency backup generators. During the council meeting, one estimate warned the center could use three times more energy than the entire city of Boulder City.

Local leaders did not hide their anger. Councilman Steve Walton called the situation "absolutely out of line, just ridiculous." Mayor Joe Hardy said the BLM approved a major project inside the city's boundaries without ever contacting local officials to be part of the decision. The city argues the BLM failed to consult Boulder City or give the public a real chance to weigh in, steps the city says are required under federal law. The city also points out that it would be the one providing local utilities and emergency response to the site.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Boulder City may be a small town, but this fight reaches every corner of Clark County. Data centers use enormous amounts of electricity and water. When one project can pull as much as 167 megawatts, that demand does not stay in one place. It pulls on the same regional power grid that serves homes in Henderson, Summerlin, and the rest of the valley. More demand can mean higher pressure on the system and, over time, higher bills for everyone.

Water is the other worry. Southern Nevada is in a long drought, and Lake Mead sits near record low levels. Data centers often need water for cooling. Any new project that adds to regional water demand raises red flags for residents who already watch every drop. When a town three miles from a proposed plant says it is worried about power and water, families across Clark County should pay attention too.

There is also a bigger question here about who controls land near your neighborhood. Boulder City is calling itself a test case for data centers built on federal land next to communities. If the BLM can approve a giant project without fully consulting the local city, the same thing could happen near other Clark County towns. Federal land wraps around much of Southern Nevada. What happens in the Eldorado Valley could set the pattern for future projects near Las Vegas, North Las Vegas, and beyond.

For homeowners, projects like this can shape property values and quality of life. A data center is not a loud neighbor in the usual sense, but the substations, generators, and truck traffic that come with it change the feel of an area. Buyers ask about noise, views, and long-term plans for nearby land. When residents feel shut out of these decisions, trust in local planning takes a hit, and that uncertainty can ripple into the housing market.

It is also worth remembering how fast this trend is moving. Data centers are popping up across the Southwest to feed the boom in artificial intelligence and cloud computing. Nevada, with its cheap land, sunshine, and existing power lines, is an obvious target. That means more of these fights are likely, and more Clark County residents will find themselves asking the same questions Boulder City is asking right now. Getting the rules right today helps every neighborhood that comes next.

Background and History

To understand why Boulder City is so protective of the Eldorado Valley, you have to know how much the area means to the town. Boulder City owns a large stretch of land there and leases it out for energy projects. The city has leased or placed under option more than 11,000 acres, roughly 18 square miles, for energy development over the years. This is one of the biggest utility-scale solar zones in the country.

That land is not just open desert. It is a financial engine. The city's solar leases generated about 17 million dollars in revenue in the 2024 to 2025 fiscal year. That is close to 35 percent of the city's annual budget. Boulder City famously has no property tax of its own, and money from these energy leases helps pay for parks, public services, and daily operations. So when a project in the valley changes shape, the stakes are both financial and personal for the town.

Boulder City has embraced clean energy for years. The Townsite Solar plant began generating power in January 2022, and the city agreed to buy a set amount of energy from it each month. Solar has been a good fit for the valley because it uses the strong desert sun and lines up with the town's identity as a renewable energy pioneer. A 19 megawatt solar and battery project fit that story. A 167 megawatt data center is a very different kind of neighbor, and that shift is at the heart of the dispute.

The frustration also grew because the change felt quiet. A project first sold to the public as clean solar power turned into a power-hungry computing center through an amended paperwork request, not a fresh public process. To residents who supported the original solar plan, the switch felt like a bait and switch, and that is a big reason the council meeting drew such strong reactions.

Context helps here. The Eldorado Valley is one of the largest utility-scale solar zones in the country, with installations spanning thousands of acres and feeding clean power to homes across Nevada. Boulder City built its reputation on being a renewable energy pioneer, and residents take pride in that. So a plan that trades a modest solar project for a massive data center does not just raise technical questions. It touches the town's sense of who it is and what its desert land is for.

What Happens Next

The immediate step is the appeal itself. Boulder City officials said they plan to file the notice of appeal before July 27. Once filed, the case moves to the Interior Board of Land Appeals. The IBLA reviews whether the BLM followed the rules when it approved the amended grant, including questions about environmental review and public input. This process can take months, and there is no guarantee of a quick answer.

While the appeal plays out, expect more public meetings and more debate. The Nevada Independent has reported that the fight has already turned heated, with strong language and real fear among residents. Neighboring agencies, state leaders, and other Nevada communities will be watching, because the outcome could shape how future data centers get approved on federal land near towns. Boulder City has made clear it wants this to be a turning point, not a one-off.

For anyone tracking the story, the key things to watch are the appeal filing date, any response from the BLM, and whether the developer offers new details about water use, power sourcing, or community benefits. If the IBLA agrees the BLM skipped required steps, the approval could be paused or sent back for a fuller review. If the appeal fails, the project could move forward, and other communities may face similar decisions soon after.

The timing matters for the broader region too. Lake Mead is nearing an all-time low, and officials are studying whether Hoover Dam can keep producing as much power as it once did. A new project that demands up to 167 megawatts lands in the middle of that squeeze. Every large power user added to the grid raises the same question for Southern Nevada: where will the extra electricity and water come from, and who pays for it? Those answers will shape not just this appeal but energy policy across the valley for years.

Ryan's Take

As a local real estate expert, I watch land decisions like this closely, because what happens on the edges of our towns eventually reaches our neighborhoods. Boulder City is doing what a lot of residents wish more governments would do. It is asking to be part of a decision that affects its own power, water, and future. That is not anti-growth. It is common sense. Big projects deserve a real conversation with the people who live nearby.

My honest view is that data centers are coming to Southern Nevada whether we like it or not. They power the apps and services we all use. But where we build them, how much water and electricity they use, and how much say local communities get are fair questions. For buyers and homeowners, the lesson is simple. Always look at what sits on the land around a home, and ask what could go there next. In a region this dependent on shared water and power, a project miles away can still touch your monthly bills and your peace of mind. Boulder City's stand is worth following no matter where in the valley you live.

What You Can Do

If you want to stay informed or get involved, start with the source. Boulder City posts updates about the data center on its official website at bcnv.org, including a dedicated page about data centers and the city's electric service. City Council meetings are open to the public, and residents can sign up to speak or submit comments. Watching one meeting is the fastest way to understand where the process stands and what leaders are deciding next.

You can also follow trusted local coverage from the Las Vegas Sun, the Nevada Current, FOX5 Vegas, and KTNV Channel 13 to keep up with the appeal. If you own or are buying a home anywhere near federal land in Clark County, it is smart to ask about long-term land plans in the area. A good agent can help you research nearby parcels, zoning, and pending projects before you commit.

Most of all, pay attention to the public comment periods. Decisions like this often move fast, and the window to speak up can be short. Whether you support or oppose a project, your voice carries more weight when you show up early and stay engaged. The Boulder City appeal is a reminder that residents who ask questions can push big agencies to slow down and listen.

If you are house hunting, treat land research as part of your homework, not an afterthought. Look up nearby parcels, check who owns them, and find out whether any are federal land that could be leased or developed. Ask the city or county about pending applications in the area. A quiet stretch of desert today can become a substation or industrial site tomorrow, and knowing that ahead of time helps you make a confident, informed decision about where to buy.
