Lake Mead Nears All-Time Low as Hoover Dam Power Cuts Are Studied

Lake Mead is about to fall below its lowest level ever recorded, and that has officials studying how much power Hoover Dam can keep making for Clark County. The Bureau of Reclamation now expects the lake to drop under its old record of 1,040.58 feet, a mark set back in 2022, by the end of next month. That is a big deal for every household in Southern Nevada, because Lake Mead is where most of our drinking water comes from and Hoover Dam is a source of cheap, clean electricity.

The concern is simple. When the water gets lower, the dam cannot spin its turbines as hard, so it makes less power. If Hoover Dam cannot make enough, local agencies like Boulder City may have to go buy electricity on the open market at a higher price. Lower water and less cheap power both land on the same place in the end, which is your tap and your utility bill.

What Happened

On July 16, 2026, Fox5 Vegas reported that the Bureau of Reclamation expects Lake Mead to sink below its record low. The old record was 1,040.58 feet, and it was set in 2022 during the worst stretch of the long drought. Federal forecasters now believe the lake will pass under that number by the end of the following month. In plain terms, the biggest reservoir in the country is about to be emptier than it has ever been since it first filled.

The forecast does not stop there. Reclamation projections show a slow, steady decline over the next two years. The lake is expected to keep dropping to roughly 1,009.69 feet by June 2028. That is another 30 feet or so below where the record sits today. Each foot of drop means less water in storage and a smaller cushion for the millions of people who depend on the Colorado River.

The falling level is what raised the alarm about Hoover Dam. The dam makes electricity by letting water fall through big turbines. The higher the water sits behind the dam, the more force it has and the more power it can make. As the surface drops, the dam loses some of that push. Engineers and water agencies are now studying just how much generating power could be lost and what that means for the customers who count on it.

Boulder City sits right next to the dam and buys some of that Hoover power. City leaders were told that if the dam cannot meet demand, they may have to buy electricity elsewhere. Open market power usually costs more than the low rate Hoover Dam has offered for decades. The same worry ran through a separate Boulder City fight over a proposed data center that could use a huge amount of energy, which only sharpened the focus on power supply in the area.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Lake Mead is not some far away lake. It is the main water supply for the Las Vegas Valley. The Southern Nevada Water Authority pulls close to 90 percent of the region's drinking water from the Colorado River through Lake Mead. That water reaches homes in Summerlin, Henderson, Spring Valley, North Las Vegas, Green Valley, and every neighborhood in between. When the lake makes news, it touches your kitchen sink and your backyard.

The good news is that Southern Nevada planned ahead. The water authority built a deep intake, often called the third straw, plus a low lake level pumping station. Those projects let the region keep drawing water even if the lake sinks very low. So a record low does not mean the taps run dry tomorrow. It does mean the pressure is on to keep saving water and to keep the long term supply steady.

The power side hits your wallet in a different way. Hoover Dam has long been one of the cheapest sources of electricity in the West. Cheap hydropower helps hold down costs for the public agencies that buy it. If that cheap power shrinks and cities have to buy more expensive electricity on the open market, those higher costs can trickle down to residents and local budgets over time. It is the kind of slow squeeze that shows up in bills and rates, not all at once, but bit by bit.

Renters feel this too, even if they never see a water bill from the authority. Landlords, apartment owners, and homeowner associations all pay for water and often for shared electricity. When those costs climb, they can show up in rent, in HOA dues, and in the price of keeping community pools and green spaces open. So the story reaches people in condos and rentals just as much as it reaches homeowners with big yards.

There is also the bigger picture for anyone who owns a home here or plans to buy one. Water and power are the backbone of growth in the desert. Builders, employers, and families all need to know the region can supply what a growing valley uses. Headlines about record low water can make people nervous about the future of the market. The reality on the ground is calmer than the scary number sounds, but the trend is real and worth watching.

Background and History

Hoover Dam was finished in the middle of the 1930s. It stands 726 feet tall and blocks the Colorado River right at the Nevada and Arizona line. The water that backs up behind it forms Lake Mead, which is the largest reservoir in the United States by capacity. When it is full, Lake Mead holds enough water to serve the region for years. For most of the last century, it was a symbol of how the desert Southwest tamed a wild river and grew big cities in dry land.

Then came the drought. Since about the year 2000, the Colorado River basin has been stuck in a long dry spell that many scientists call a megadrought. Less snow in the Rocky Mountains means less water flowing down the river each spring. Year after year, more water was taken out than nature put back in. Lake Mead slowly dropped, and a pale white band of mineral deposits, known as the bathtub ring, grew taller along the shore. That ring is a plain sign of how much water is gone.

The seven states that share the Colorado River split its water under old rules and agreements. Nevada has one of the smallest shares, about 300,000 acre feet a year, even though it is one of the fastest growing states. That small share pushed Southern Nevada to become a national leader in water recycling and conservation. The region reuses nearly all the water used indoors and has torn out huge amounts of decorative grass to save more.

To picture how far the lake has fallen, it helps to know that a full Lake Mead sits at about 1,229 feet. The record low from 2022 is roughly 189 feet below that full mark, which is why the reservoir looks so different than it did a generation ago. In 2021, the federal government declared the first ever official shortage on the Colorado River. That declaration set off a tiered system of cuts, and Nevada and Arizona were the first states asked to take less water starting the next year. Nevada's cut was small because its share is already small, but the message was clear that the old way of using the river could not last.

The dam itself has warning lines built into the math. As the water drops toward roughly 950 feet, Hoover Dam reaches what experts call minimum power pool, the point where it can no longer safely make electricity. Below that, near about 895 feet, the reservoir hits what is often called deadpool, where water can no longer pass through the dam at all. The lake is still far above those marks, and no one is predicting deadpool anytime soon. But those numbers are why every foot of decline gets studied so closely.

The 2022 record low was a wake up call for the whole basin. It pushed the states into tense talks about who should cut back and by how much. Those talks are still going on, and a short term deal to ease the cuts is reportedly close. The record about to be broken this summer is the next chapter in that long story.

What Happens Next

The near term timeline is short. Federal forecasters expect the record to fall by the end of next month, so the milestone is likely just weeks away. After that, the projections point to a slow slide down to about 1,009.69 feet by June 2028. Water managers will keep a close eye on winter snow in the Rockies, because a strong snow year can slow the decline and a weak one can speed it up.

On the power side, expect more study and planning. Agencies that buy Hoover Dam electricity, including Boulder City, will look at how much generation might be lost and where they would get replacement power if needed. The Bureau of Reclamation manages the dam and adjusts how it operates as levels change. None of this happens overnight, but the work of lining up backup power and protecting the water supply is already underway.

Closer to home, expect Southern Nevada to keep pushing conservation. The region is in the middle of removing decorative grass under a state law that phases out nonfunctional turf in the coming years. Every acre of grass that comes out saves water that would otherwise be lost to evaporation and runoff. Those local steps do not control the whole river, but they stretch the share Nevada gets and show the rest of the basin that the valley is serious about saving.

The wider fix depends on the seven Colorado River states. They are close to a short term, two year agreement that could ease some of the pressure while they hammer out longer term rules. Watch for news on that deal in the coming weeks and months. A firm agreement would not refill the lake, but it would bring some order and shared sacrifice to how the river is managed while the drought drags on.

Ryan's Take

As someone who helps families buy and sell homes across the valley, I get asked about water more than almost anything else. My honest answer is that a record low headline sounds scary, but it does not mean Las Vegas is running out of water. Southern Nevada has spent years and a lot of money getting ready for exactly this. The deep intake, the recycling, and the grass removal are real tools that keep the taps flowing even when the lake is low. Few places in the country plan their water as carefully as we do.

What I watch more closely is the power and cost side. Cheap Hoover Dam electricity has quietly helped this region for generations. If that gets pricier, the effects show up slowly in local budgets and rates. For buyers, this is not a reason to run from the market. It is a reason to understand the desert you are moving to, support smart water use, and pay attention to how our leaders manage both water and power. A community that plans well is a community worth investing in.

What You Can Do

The most direct thing you can do is save water at home. The Southern Nevada Water Authority offers a Water Smart Landscapes rebate that pays homeowners for each square foot of grass they replace with desert friendly plants and rock. Swapping a thirsty lawn for smart landscaping can cut a yard's outdoor water use by a large amount, and outdoor watering is where most homes lose the most. You can also fix leaks, install a smart sprinkler timer, and follow the mandatory seasonal watering days set for your address. Small changes across hundreds of thousands of homes add up to a lot of saved water, and that helps protect the supply for everyone.

It also pays to understand where your water goes back. Southern Nevada recycles nearly all of the water used indoors, cleans it, and returns it to Lake Mead, which earns the region credits to pull more out. That is why saving water outdoors matters so much more than saving it indoors here. When you shorten your sprinkler runtime or pull out a strip of grass, you are protecting water that never comes back to the lake. It is one of the clearest ways a single household can help the whole valley.

You can also stay informed and speak up. Boulder City meetings, county commission meetings, and water authority updates are open to the public, and they cover the choices that shape our water and power future. If you care about how much energy new projects like data centers use, those meetings are where the decisions get made. Showing up or writing in is how residents make their voices heard.

Finally, if you are buying or selling in the valley, know the facts so you can speak with confidence. Water and power questions come up in almost every deal, and clear, calm information beats fear every time.
