CCSD Breaks Ground on $76 Million Matt Kelly Elementary Rebuild in the Historic Westside

The Clark County School District broke ground on Monday, July 13, 2026, on a brand new campus for Matt Kelly Elementary School in the Historic Westside of Las Vegas. The current school has stood on the same spot since 1960, and the district is replacing it with a modern two-story building that will hold about 440 students.

The project costs $76 million and is paid for through the district's 2015 Capital Improvement Program. The new school is scheduled to open for the 2027-2028 school year. For a neighborhood that has waited a long time for this kind of investment, the groundbreaking is a big deal.

What Happened at the Groundbreaking

On Monday, July 13, 2026, district leaders gathered on the Matt Kelly Elementary site to mark the official start of construction. A groundbreaking is the traditional first step for a big building project. It is the moment when the shovels come out and the plan finally becomes real dirt and steel.

The old Matt Kelly Elementary has served children in the Historic Westside since 1960. That is 66 years of use. A school built in 1960 was designed for a very different era, with older wiring, older plumbing, and a layout that does not match how schools work today. Rather than keep patching an aging building, the district decided to build a full replacement on the property.

Think about how much has changed since 1960. Classrooms now need reliable power for computers, tablets, and smart boards. Buildings need modern heating and cooling to handle Las Vegas summers, better insulation, and updated safety features. A campus from the 1960s was never built for any of that. A ground up rebuild lets designers plan every classroom, hallway, and shared space around the way children learn in 2026 and beyond.

The new campus will be a two-story building. Two stories let a school fit more classroom space on a smaller footprint, which matters on a tight urban lot. When it is finished, the school will serve about 440 students. That keeps Matt Kelly as a neighborhood elementary, close enough that many kids can walk or take a short ride to class.

A groundbreaking is also a milestone worth marking for what it represents. Big public projects move through many stages before a shovel ever hits the ground, including planning, design, budgeting, and approvals. Reaching the construction phase means all of that groundwork is finished and the money is committed. For the families who use this school every day, July 13 was the moment the promise turned into action.

The price tag is $76 million. That money comes from the 2015 Capital Improvement Program, a long term building fund that Clark County and the district set up to repair and replace aging schools across the valley. The target opening is the 2027-2028 school year, which means construction will run through 2026 and 2027 before students move in.

It helps to put those numbers in plain terms. A $76 million school is a major public project, on par with the cost of a large civic building. Spread across a campus that will serve about 440 students, that spending shows the district is not cutting corners. A two-story replacement built from the ground up is a full reset, not a quick fix. When it is done, the children of the Historic Westside will learn in a building that meets today's standards for safety, technology, and comfort.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

A new school is more than a building. For families in the Historic Westside, this is a signal that the neighborhood is getting real, long term investment. When a district spends $76 million on your local school, it is planning for the next 50 years of kids in that area. That kind of commitment tends to lift the whole community.

For parents, the day to day impact is simple and welcome. A modern campus means better heating and cooling, safer and cleaner spaces, updated technology, and classrooms designed for how kids learn today. It also means fewer of the maintenance headaches that come with a building from 1960. Those small things add up to a better school day for every child.

There is a housing angle here too, and it is one I watch closely. Schools shape where families choose to live. Buyers with children often start their home search by looking at the local elementary school. A fresh, well funded campus can make a neighborhood more attractive and help hold up home values on nearby streets. Renewal like this is a quiet driver of demand.

Walkability is part of the picture as well. Because the new school stays in the heart of the neighborhood, many families will still be within a short walk or a quick drive of the campus. That is a real convenience for busy parents, and it is one of the reasons a neighborhood elementary is so valuable. Kids who can get to school easily miss fewer days and stay more connected to their community.

Even residents without kids in the system benefit. Public school buildings often double as community anchors. They host meetings, voting sites, and neighborhood events. A safer and more modern building makes the whole area feel cared for, and that feeling matters when people decide whether to plant roots and stay.

There is also a jobs and business side to a project this size. Construction work brings crews, suppliers, and contractors into the area for the better part of two years. Local shops and eateries often see a bump from that activity. Once the school opens, it keeps staff jobs in the neighborhood, from teachers to office workers to support staff. A school is one of the steadiest employers a community can have.

Finally, keeping the school on its historic site matters. The district could have moved these students to another building. Instead, it chose to rebuild on the same land the community has known since 1960. That decision keeps kids close to home and keeps the school woven into the daily life of the Westside. For families who have lived in the area for generations, that continuity is worth a lot.

Background and History

The Historic Westside is one of the oldest neighborhoods in Las Vegas. For much of the 20th century, it was home to a large share of the city's Black community, and it grew its own churches, businesses, and schools during decades when many other parts of the valley were closed off to those families. That history gives the neighborhood deep roots and a strong sense of identity.

Matt Kelly Elementary has been part of that story since 1960. Generations of Westside families have sent their children through its doors. Because of that long history, the school carries meaning that goes beyond test scores and class sizes. It is a shared landmark, and a rebuild on the same site keeps that connection alive while giving the neighborhood a modern place to learn.

Over the past several decades, much of the newer growth in the Las Vegas valley pushed out toward the suburbs, from Summerlin in the west to Henderson in the southeast. Older core neighborhoods like the Historic Westside did not always see the same level of fresh investment. That is what makes a $76 million school rebuild stand out. It directs major public dollars back into an established neighborhood rather than a brand new subdivision on the edge of town.

The funding behind this project traces back to 2015. That year, Clark County backed a Capital Improvement Program built to tackle a huge backlog of aging schools. CCSD is one of the largest school districts in the country, and many of its buildings went up decades ago. The 2015 program set aside money to replace and repair the oldest and most worn campuses over many years.

Matt Kelly is one of those campuses. A 1960 building sits near the top of the list when a district ranks schools by age and condition. The groundbreaking this month is the payoff of a plan that has been years in the making, and it shows how long these public building projects really take from first vote to first shovel.

It also helps to understand how CCSD decides which schools to replace. The district weighs the age of the building, the cost of ongoing repairs, safety needs, and how well the layout fits modern teaching. Older schools reach a point where fixing them again and again costs more than building fresh. When a campus crosses that line, a full replacement becomes the smarter use of public money. Matt Kelly, at 66 years old, fits that pattern.

What Happens Next

With the groundbreaking done, the site now moves into full construction. Crews will spend the coming months on foundations, framing, and the two-story structure. Building a school is a long process with many steps, from pouring concrete to running wiring to final inspections, so residents nearby should expect construction activity to continue through 2026 and into 2027.

The finish line is the 2027-2028 school year. That is the target for when about 440 students will walk into the new building for the first time. Between now and then, the district will handle the usual work of a school move, including planning for how students and staff transition into the new campus once it is ready.

Timelines like this can shift, as any big construction project can run into weather, supply, or labor surprises. Still, the 2027-2028 target gives families a clear window to plan around. Parents of younger children can look ahead and know their kids may spend part of their elementary years in a brand new building. That is a nice thing to be able to tell a five or six year old who is just starting school.

Neighbors near the site can expect the normal effects of a large construction job. That can mean trucks, noise during work hours, and some temporary changes to traffic or parking around the school. These are short term trade offs for a building that will serve the neighborhood for decades. Anyone with concerns can reach out to the district to ask about the construction schedule.

It is also worth watching how the finished campus fits into the wider push to renew the Historic Westside. A new anchor school can pair well with other neighborhood improvements over time, from housing to small business growth. Projects like this rarely happen in a vacuum. When a district plants a fresh, modern school in an older neighborhood, it often marks the start of a longer wave of investment that residents will feel in the years ahead.
