New Study Questions How Much Buildable Land Clark County Really Has

A new report from Las Vegas research firm RCG Economics says Clark County has far less buildable land than a widely cited earlier study suggested. An older inventory found roughly 78,000 acres of "vacant or underutilized" land across the county, but the new review argues that much of that land is actually church, school, and store parking lots, or steep slopes and mountaintops that no one can realistically build homes on.

That matters because the size of our land supply is at the center of the whole Las Vegas housing debate. If the buildable number is much smaller than people thought, then the pressure on home prices, the fight over federal land, and the push for denser building all get more urgent. Here is what the study found and what it means for your home and your neighborhood.

What the New Study Found

The new analysis came from RCG Economics, a well known Las Vegas research firm led by economist John Restrepo. RCG prepared the report for the Southern Nevada chapter of the Commercial Real Estate Development Association, the group formerly known as NAIOP. The goal was to take a hard second look at an earlier land inventory that many people had been quoting.

That earlier inventory came out of a study tied to the Regional Transportation Commission of Southern Nevada. It used Clark County Assessor data and identified about 78,285 acres of vacant or underutilized land inside the county. Some coverage rounded that figure up toward 80,000 to 84,000 acres. On paper, that sounds like a lot of room to grow.

RCG says the real picture is very different. When the firm looked closer at those parcels, it found that a large share of the so called vacant land is not open ground at all. Much of it is private parking lots for churches, schools, and stores. Restrepo pointed out that the list even included a Costco parking lot. Other parcels sit on steep slopes and mountaintops where building a normal neighborhood would be difficult, costly, or simply impossible.

Even the original study seemed to hint at this. In that inventory, about 87 percent of the acreage was ranked low for development potential. Restrepo said the private sector was baffled when the 78,285 acre number first came out, because builders and developers on the ground kept saying they could not find land, especially larger parcels for bigger projects. The new report tries to close that gap between the headline number and what people building here actually see.

The RCG review walks through the many reasons a parcel might show up as vacant on paper but never turn into homes. Terrain is a big one, since a steep hillside costs far more to grade and build on than flat ground. Infrastructure access matters too, because land with no nearby roads, water lines, or sewer hookups can sit idle for years. Parcel shape, utility capacity, entitlement limits, and plain economic viability all play a role. When you filter out everything that fails these tests, the pool of truly build ready land shrinks fast.

This second look did not come out of nowhere. The report was released for the Southern Nevada development community earlier in 2026 and has been building momentum in local real estate circles since then. The mid July coverage in the Las Vegas Review-Journal put the debate back in the spotlight, right as several major housing projects around the valley move forward and the region keeps talking about how to add more homes.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

This is not just a numbers fight between two studies. It touches the price of nearly every home in the valley. When land is truly scarce, builders pay more for each buildable acre, and those higher costs get passed on to buyers. That is one reason new homes in places like Summerlin, Henderson, and the southwest keep climbing in price even when demand cools a little.

If the buildable supply is smaller than the old 78,000 acre figure implied, then the squeeze on first time buyers gets tighter. Renters trying to make the jump to ownership already face record prices. A shrinking pool of easy to build land means fewer entry level homes and more competition for the ones that do get built. That is a real problem for young families and workers who keep this city running.

There is also a fairness angle for existing homeowners. Tight land supply tends to protect and even lift the value of homes you already own. That sounds great if you are selling. It is harder if you are hoping your kids can buy nearby, or if you want the region to add enough housing to keep prices sane over the long run. Land scarcity cuts both ways for the people who live here.

The study also shapes how our leaders argue for more land. Local officials have been pushing the federal government to release more public land around the valley for housing. If critics can point to 78,000 acres of "empty" land already inside the county, that argument gets weaker. The RCG report gives officials and builders a way to answer that critique with a simple point. A parking lot is not a subdivision, and a mountaintop is not a home site.

For everyday buyers, the practical lesson is to be careful with big, scary land numbers you see online. A single acre figure can hide a lot of nuance. The same acre can be counted as vacant even when it is a school lot, a drainage channel, or a rocky slope. When you are house hunting, what really matters is the supply of homes in your price range and your target neighborhood, not the raw acreage on a countywide map. Those are two very different things, and this study is a good reminder of the difference.

Background and History

To understand why land is such a big deal here, you have to look at who owns it. The federal government controls the vast majority of land in Clark County. Agencies like the Bureau of Land Management, the National Park Service, and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service hold millions of acres. By many counts, roughly 85 to 88 percent of the land in the county is federal. That leaves only a thin slice in private hands for homes, stores, and offices.

Because of that, growth here does not just spread out freely the way it might in other states. New land for building usually has to be sold off by the federal government first. The main tool for that is the Southern Nevada Public Land Management Act of 1998. It lets the BLM sell public land inside a set boundary around Las Vegas, with the money going toward schools, water projects, and conservation.

Over the years, that boundary has slowly filled in. As the easy, flat, well located parcels get built, what is left is often harder to use. It might be far from roads and pipes, oddly shaped, or up against the mountains. So the story of Southern Nevada land has always been about more than raw acres. It is about which acres can actually become neighborhoods without huge added cost.

The way land sales are set up also shapes the debate. Under the rules that govern these federal sales, most of the money does not go straight into building homes. Roughly 85 percent funds conservation projects, another 10 percent goes to the Southern Nevada Water Authority, and about 5 percent supports Nevada education. That structure raises the effective cost of turning public land into housing, which is one more reason builders say the buildable supply feels tighter than the maps suggest.

There is also a track record on affordable housing land that tells its own story. Hundreds of acres were set aside years ago for affordable housing at a nominal cost, but only a small share has actually been developed into homes so far. Reports this summer noted that the federal land program meant to ease the housing crunch has been used far less often than the growing need would call for. That slow pace is part of why the RCG findings hit a nerve.

That history is exactly why the RCG report landed with a punch. It is easy to look at a map, see big blank spaces, and assume the valley has plenty of room. The reality on the ground is that terrain, infrastructure, parcel shape, utility capacity, and simple economics all decide whether a piece of land can hold homes. The new study puts those real world limits back into the conversation.

What Happens Next

Expect this report to become a talking point in nearly every land and housing meeting for the rest of the year. Builders and the commercial real estate group that commissioned it will use it to press for more federal land releases and for smarter zoning. The numbers give them fresh ammunition to argue that Southern Nevada faces a genuine land crunch, not a paperwork problem.

On the other side, planners and some elected officials will likely push back. They may argue that the answer is not just more raw land at the edges, but building up and building denser inside the areas we already have. Those parking lots and underused parcels could still hold housing someday if the county allows taller, mixed use projects on them. That debate over sprawl versus density is only going to get louder.

Watch for how this feeds into specific decisions. The region still needs somewhere around 80,000 to 90,000 more housing units to keep up with demand. Projects like the Desert Pines redevelopment in east Las Vegas, new villages at Tule Springs in North Las Vegas, and affordable housing land nominations all connect back to this same core question. How much room do we really have, and where. The RCG study will be cited in all of it.

Keep an eye on the entitlement process too. Even when a piece of land is technically buildable, it can take years to move through zoning changes, environmental review, utility planning, and permits before a single home goes up. If the true supply of ready land is as thin as RCG suggests, then anything that speeds up that pipeline, or that unlocks new parcels near the edge of the valley, becomes a much bigger deal for future home prices.

Ryan's Take

As someone who helps buyers and sellers across the valley every week, this report matches what I see on the ground. Land is tight, and it has been tight for a long time. When people ask me why Las Vegas home prices keep pushing higher even in a cooler market, the land story is a huge part of the answer. You cannot flood the market with new homes when there is nowhere easy left to build them.

My honest take is that this does not point to a crash. It points to steady, long term pressure on prices, especially for well located homes near jobs and good schools. If you already own here, that scarcity is quietly working in your favor. If you are trying to buy, it means waiting for a big flood of cheap new inventory is probably not a smart plan. The smarter move is to understand the real supply picture and act when the right home and the right rate line up for you.

I also think this report should push all of us to take density more seriously. We love our single story homes and big yards here, and there is nothing wrong with that. But if flat, easy land is nearly gone, then townhomes, condos, and mixed use projects on those underused parcels are going to be a bigger part of the future. Buyers who stay open to those options may find better value and shorter commutes than folks holding out for the last few traditional lots on the edge of town.

What You Can Do

If you care about how land decisions shape your neighborhood, get involved locally. Clark County Commission meetings, city council meetings in Las Vegas, Henderson, and North Las Vegas, and planning commission hearings are all open to the public. That is where land use, zoning, and density fights get decided. You can find agendas and public comment details on each government's website.

If you are thinking about buying or selling, focus on facts, not headlines. A study like this can sound scary or confusing, but the takeaway for most families is simple. Buildable land is limited, prices tend to hold up over time, and timing your move around your own life and budget usually beats trying to time the whole market. Talk to a local expert who can walk you through what is actually happening in your target area.

Finally, stay informed on the federal land question, because it drives so much of what happens here. Follow local coverage of the Bureau of Land Management, the Southern Nevada Public Land Management Act, and any new land sales near the edges of the valley. Those decisions will shape where the next wave of Las Vegas homes gets built, and how much they cost.
