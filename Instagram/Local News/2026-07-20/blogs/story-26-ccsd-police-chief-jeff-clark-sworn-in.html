New CCSD Police Chief Jeff Clark Sworn In After Unanimous Board Approval

The Clark County School District has a new top cop. Jeff Clark, a longtime Las Vegas Metro captain, was sworn in as the district's new chief of police on Monday, July 13, 2026, after the CCSD Board of School Trustees approved him in a unanimous vote. He now leads campus safety for one of the largest school districts in the country as families across the valley get ready for a new school year.

This matters to every parent, student, and homeowner near a Clark County campus. The person in this seat sets the tone for how schools respond to threats, fights, weapons, and everyday safety. Clark takes over a cabinet-level job that pays $225,000 a year, and he brings 21 years of Metro experience with him.

What Happened

The CCSD Board of School Trustees voted to approve Jeff Clark as the district's new chief of police. The vote was unanimous, and it passed without any discussion from the board. That approval was reported in early July 2026, and the swearing-in followed soon after.

Clark was sworn in on Monday, July 13, 2026, at Cimarron-Memorial High School in the northwest part of the Las Vegas Valley. The location was personal. Clark graduated from Cimarron-Memorial in 1996, so he took the oath at the same school he attended as a teenager. Superintendent Jhone Ebert administered the oath of office.

The role is a serious one. The chief of police for CCSD is a cabinet-level position, which means Clark sits near the top of the district's leadership chart and reports into the superintendent's team. The job pays $225,000 per year. That salary reflects the size of the task, because the CCSD Police Department covers hundreds of campuses spread across a huge slice of Southern Nevada.

Clark is not new to law enforcement in this valley. He spent 21 years with the Las Vegas Metropolitan Police Department, rising to the rank of captain. During his time at Metro, he helped build a threat-assessment framework designed for K-12 campuses. That background in school-focused safety planning is a big reason he was picked to lead the district's own police force.

The fact that the board vote was unanimous and came without discussion is worth noting. In public meetings, a quiet, no-debate vote usually means the trustees felt comfortable with the choice ahead of time. It suggests the district did its homework on Clark before the item ever reached the public agenda. For a job this important, that kind of agreement across the board is a signal of confidence in the pick.

The swearing-in itself was a public moment meant to mark the handoff of a big responsibility. Superintendent Jhone Ebert administering the oath tied the new chief directly to the district's top leader, showing the two will work side by side on safety. Choosing a real campus, rather than a district office, put the focus where the job actually lives, which is inside the schools themselves.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

School safety is one of the first questions families ask when they shop for a home. Parents want to know that the campus down the street is watched, that officers respond fast, and that someone competent is in charge. The person who now holds that responsibility for CCSD is Jeff Clark, and his approach will shape the daily experience of thousands of local kids.

The CCSD Police Department is not a small operation. It is a full law enforcement agency with sworn officers who patrol school grounds, respond to calls, and work with campus staff. These officers cover schools from the central valley to the far suburbs, including neighborhoods like Summerlin, Henderson, North Las Vegas, Spring Valley, and Centennial Hills. A new chief means a new voice guiding how those officers train, respond, and connect with students.

The relationship between officers and students matters as much as the response to emergencies. Kids who trust the officers on their campus are more likely to speak up when something feels wrong. That early tip can stop a fight, flag a weapon, or get a struggling student help before a small problem becomes a big one. A chief who understands schools knows this, and it shapes how officers are trained to talk with and listen to young people every day.

For renters and homeowners, this news also touches something bigger. Strong, calm campus safety helps schools keep good teachers and helps neighborhoods hold their value. Buyers pay close attention to school zones. When a district shows it is taking safety seriously by hiring an experienced leader, that steadiness can be a quiet plus for families weighing where to live.

There is also the money side. A $225,000 salary is public spending, and taxpayers have every right to ask what they get for it. In this case, the district chose someone with deep local roots and two decades of on-the-ground experience. That is the kind of hire that can pay off if it leads to fewer incidents, faster responses, and better trust between officers and the students they serve.

Think about the sheer scale of what this one office covers. Clark County is huge, and its schools sit in nearly every corner of it. A single chief oversees a police response that touches elementary schools, middle schools, and high schools all at once. When a parent in Skye Canyon and a parent in Green Valley both wonder whether their child's school is safe, the answer runs through the same department and the same leader. That is why the choice of chief is a valley-wide story, not a single-neighborhood one.

Everyday campus safety also shapes how students learn. When kids feel safe, they focus better, attend more often, and get more out of the school day. When they feel unsafe, learning suffers and families start looking elsewhere. The chief of police helps set that baseline of calm. A steady, experienced leader in the role is not just about big emergencies; it is about the ordinary school day going smoothly for hundreds of thousands of local children.

Background and History

The Clark County School District is one of the largest public school systems in the United States. It serves hundreds of thousands of students across the Las Vegas Valley and the wider county, which stretches far beyond the city into rural and suburban areas. A district that big needs its own police department, and CCSD has one.

The CCSD Police Department is a dedicated agency focused on schools. Its officers are trained to work inside the school setting, where the goals are different from regular street policing. The emphasis is on preventing problems before they grow, building relationships with students, and keeping campuses calm so learning can happen. The department has long leaned on the idea that stopping trouble early beats reacting to it later.

Jeff Clark's career fits that mission. Over 21 years at Metro, he worked his way up to captain and spent part of that time focused on school-related safety. He helped create a threat-assessment framework for K-12 campuses. Threat assessment is a structured way for schools and police to spot warning signs, weigh how serious a risk is, and step in before a situation turns dangerous. That work put him in close contact with the exact challenges CCSD faces.

His swearing-in at Cimarron-Memorial High School closed a loop that started when he was a student there in the 1990s. Clark graduated from the school in 1996. Taking the oath in that building sent a clear message that he understands these campuses from the inside, not just as a police officer but as someone who once walked the same halls.

School safety has climbed the priority list for districts everywhere over the past decade. Threat assessment has become a core tool in that work. Instead of waiting for a crisis, schools and police build teams that review concerning behavior, gather information, and decide on a response that protects students while still supporting the person of concern. Clark helped shape that kind of framework at Metro, so he arrives at CCSD already fluent in the model the district needs.

Moving from a captain's role at Metro to chief of a school district police department is a natural next step for a leader focused on campus safety. It trades a broad city-and-county policing mission for a sharper focus on students, staff, and school grounds. The experience carries over, but the day-to-day priorities shift toward prevention, relationships, and the unique rhythm of a school calendar. Clark's history suggests that is exactly the work he wants to do.

What Happens Next

The most immediate test comes with the new school year. Summer is winding down, and campuses across Clark County will soon fill back up with students. The start of the year is always a busy time for school police, with new routines, new drop-off patterns, and fresh questions about safety. Clark steps into the job right as that rush begins.

Watch for how the department handles training and staffing under new leadership. School police agencies across the country have worked to hire and keep officers, and CCSD is no different. A new chief often reviews how officers are placed, how they respond to calls, and how they team up with counselors and administrators. Any shifts in that approach will show up first at the campus level.

Parents and neighbors can also expect to hear more about threat assessment and prevention. Given Clark's background, it is reasonable to think that early-warning systems and campus safety planning will stay front and center. As the year unfolds, the district's own updates, board meetings, and news coverage will show how his plans take shape in real schools.

Keep an eye on how the department communicates with families, too. New leaders often refresh the way a district shares safety information, from alerts and newsletters to community meetings. Clear, calm communication is one of the simplest ways to build trust, and it costs very little. If Clark leans into that, parents should notice more updates and easier ways to ask questions about what is happening on their child's campus.

Finally, watch the budget conversations. School police staffing, training, and equipment all cost money, and those choices run through the same board that just approved the new chief. Nevada schools have faced tight funding debates, so how the district pays for campus safety will stay in the spotlight. The decisions made in the coming months will shape what the department can actually do on the ground.
