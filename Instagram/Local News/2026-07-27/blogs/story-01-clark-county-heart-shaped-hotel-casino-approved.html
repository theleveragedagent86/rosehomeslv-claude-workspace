Clark County Approves 752-Room Heart-Shaped Hotel-Casino on Former SkyVue Site

Clark County commissioners voted on July 22, 2026 to approve a brand new hotel-casino on the failed SkyVue site near the south end of the Las Vegas Strip. The building will stand 29 stories tall, hold 752 rooms, and wear a giant heart-shaped glass front wrapped in bright LED lights. This matters because it finally puts one of the Strip's most famous empty lots back to work after more than ten years of sitting still.

The land sits on about 12 acres across from Mandalay Bay. For years, drivers passing by have seen two lonely concrete columns rising from the dirt. Those columns were meant to hold a huge observation wheel called SkyVue. That project ran out of money and never opened. Now developer Eli Applebaum wants to turn that eyesore into a working resort. His plan keeps those two old columns and uses them to hold up a restaurant floating about 265 feet in the air.

The approval is a big deal for the whole valley. A new resort means construction jobs, then hotel and casino jobs once it opens. It also sends a message that money is still flowing into the south Strip. For people who live and own homes in Las Vegas, projects like this shape traffic, jobs, and home values for miles around. Here is a full look at what was approved, why it matters to you, and what comes next.

What Happened

On July 22, 2026, the Clark County Commission approved the hotel-casino plan from developer Eli Applebaum. He is building it through his company, Kulik River Capital. The group bought the SkyVue land this spring for $70 million. That price shows how much a good spot near the Strip is still worth, even when it comes with a decade of failed history attached.

The approved design is packed with features. Here is what the plan includes:

The tower will rise 29 stories and hold 752 hotel rooms. It will have a heart-shaped front made of glass, and that heart will be wrapped in an LED display. That means the whole face of the building can glow and change colors. On a Strip already famous for bright lights, a giant lit-up heart would stand out.

Inside and around the tower, the plan calls for a full casino floor. There will also be more than 42,000 square feet of convention and meeting space. That kind of space lets the resort chase business travelers and events, not just tourists. A six-story parking garage is part of the design too, which helps handle the cars that come with a busy resort.

The most talked-about piece is the mid-air restaurant. It will sit about 265 feet up in the air. The design uses the two leftover SkyVue concrete columns to help hold it in place. Instead of tearing down the old bones of the failed project, the new plan builds on top of them. That is a smart way to save money and to turn a sad symbol into something new.

The plan did change on its way to approval. Applebaum first proposed 874 rooms. The room count was cut down to 752. The reason was aviation height limits. Because the site sits near Harry Reid International Airport, buildings there cannot rise past certain heights that could get in the way of planes. So the design was trimmed to fit the rules. This is a normal step for tall projects near the south Strip, and it shows the plan went through real review before the vote.

Location is a big part of why this deal makes sense. The site sits across from Mandalay Bay, one of the anchor resorts at the south end of the Strip. It is also close to Allegiant Stadium, home of the Raiders, and near the airport. That mix of neighbors means heavy foot traffic and lots of visitors already flow through the area. A new resort there does not have to create its own crowd from scratch. It can tap into the millions of people who already pass by for games, flights, and stays at nearby hotels.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

You might wonder why a new casino should matter to you if you never plan to gamble there. The truth is that a project this size touches far more than the Strip. It reaches into jobs, home values, and daily life across the valley.

Start with jobs. Building a 29-story resort takes a small army of workers. That means work for people who pour concrete, hang steel, run wires, and finish rooms. These are solid paychecks that flow to local families. When the resort opens, it needs even more people. Think dealers, cooks, housekeepers, front desk staff, security, and managers. A resort with 752 rooms, a casino, and convention space can employ thousands over time. Those are steady jobs close to home.

Next, think about home values. When new resorts get approved on the Strip, it tells the market that Las Vegas is still growing. Growth like this helps support home prices in nearby areas. Neighborhoods near the south Strip, near the airport, and near the freeway often see steady demand from workers who want to live close to their jobs. If you own a home in those parts of the valley, more jobs nearby can mean more buyers and renters who want to be near work.

Renters feel it too. New jobs bring new workers who need places to live. That can push up demand for apartments and rental homes near the Strip. If you rent in that area, you may see more competition for units over time. If you own a rental, you may find it easier to keep it filled.

Then there is traffic and daily life. A busy new resort brings more cars, more rideshare pickups, and more foot traffic. People who drive Las Vegas Boulevard or the nearby freeway may notice changes during big events. On the good side, more visitors mean more money spent at local shops, gas stations, and restaurants. That spending helps the whole local economy, not just the resort itself.

The convention space matters more than it may seem. More than 42,000 square feet of meeting space lets the resort host events during the week, not just on weekends. Business travel fills hotel rooms on nights when tourists are scarce. That steady flow of guests supports steady jobs, and steady jobs support steady renters and buyers in the neighborhoods nearby. When a resort can stay busy year round, the people who work there have more secure paychecks.

Finally, this project helps clean up a long-standing eyesore. A half-built ruin on the Strip is not a good look for the city. Turning it into a working resort improves the view for everyone who drives by and can lift the feel of the whole south Strip area. A better looking corridor can help nearby property owners too, since curb appeal along the Strip affects how visitors and buyers see the whole south end of the valley.

Background and History

To understand why this approval is such big news, you have to know the story of the SkyVue site. The plan started years ago as a giant observation wheel. Think of a huge Ferris wheel meant to give riders sweeping views of the Strip. Builders put up two tall concrete columns to hold the wheel. Then the money dried up. The wheel was never finished, and the site was left with just those two bare columns standing in the dirt.

For more than a decade, that land sat unfinished. It became one of the best known empty lots on the Strip. Locals drove past it for years and watched nothing happen. The site sits in a prime spot, right across from Mandalay Bay near the south end of the Strip, which made the empty lot feel like an even bigger waste. A spot that valuable normally does not stay empty for long, but this one did.

Las Vegas has seen this pattern before. The Strip and its edges are full of projects that stalled when money ran out, only to come back to life years later under new owners. When times are good and lenders are willing, someone often steps in to buy a stalled site cheap and finish what others could not. The Strip rewards patience and deep pockets.

That is what makes this deal stand out. Applebaum's group did not tear everything down and start over. They paid $70 million for the land this spring and chose to build around the two old columns. By using those columns to hold the mid-air restaurant, the new plan turns a symbol of failure into part of something that works. It is a rare case where the leftovers of a dead project become a feature of the new one.

What Happens Next

Approval by the county is a major step, but it is not the last one. Now the project moves toward the real work of building. A tower this size takes years to design in full detail, line up financing, and construct. Big Strip resorts often take several years from approval to opening day. So this heart-shaped hotel-casino is not something that will pop up overnight.

Before crews can pour concrete, the project usually needs building permits and other sign-offs. Gaming plans will also need review from Nevada gaming regulators before a casino can open its doors. These steps are normal for any Strip resort and help make sure the building is safe and the operators are cleared to run a casino.

Financing is another key piece to watch. Buying land is one thing; paying to build a 29-story tower is a much larger bill. Big projects like this depend on loans and investors staying on board. If money markets stay steady, the project can move ahead on schedule. If lending gets tight, timelines can slip. That is worth keeping an eye on, since the Strip has seen projects slow down when money got harder to find.

There is also the branding side to watch. A heart-shaped tower wrapped in LED lights is built to grab attention. If the developer leans into that theme, the resort could become a new photo stop on the Strip, much like other landmark buildings that draw crowds just to look. A strong theme can help a new resort stand out in a crowded market where it must compete with much larger, older names right next door.

For now, the clearest sign of progress will be activity on the site itself. Watch for crews clearing the lot, fencing going up, and cranes arriving. Once you see steel rising near those old SkyVue columns, you will know the plan is truly moving from paper to reality. Local news outlets will likely report each milestone, from groundbreaking to topping out to opening.
