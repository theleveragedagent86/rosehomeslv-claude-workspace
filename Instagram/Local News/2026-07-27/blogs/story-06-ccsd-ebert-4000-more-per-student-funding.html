Superintendent Ebert Pushes for $4,000 More Per Student in Clark County Schools

Clark County School District Superintendent Jhone Ebert is asking for about $4,000 more in funding for every student in the district. She made the case in front of chamber of commerce audiences in mid-to-late July 2026, laying out district plans and pressing business leaders to back a bigger investment in local schools.

The request landed at a tricky moment. CCSD is one of the largest school districts in the country, and it is staring down a projected multi-year drop in both student enrollment and the money that comes with it. So the pitch was not just about wanting more. It was about warning that the district could fall further behind if the money does not grow. Ebert's office pointed to a survey of about 50,000 people that found strong demand for more real-life and career-connected learning in high school. That kind of program costs money, and that is a big part of why she is asking for more now.

For families across the Las Vegas Valley, this is bigger than a line in a budget. School funding touches property taxes, home values, and the everyday choice of where to raise a family. When people shop for a home in Clark County, the strength of the local schools is often near the top of the list. So a public fight over per-student dollars is really a conversation about the future of every neighborhood in the valley.

What Happened

In mid-to-late July 2026, Superintendent Jhone Ebert took her message directly to the business community. She spoke to chamber of commerce members, the local groups that represent employers and business owners across Southern Nevada. Instead of only talking to parents or school board members, she went to the people who hire graduates and pay a large share of local taxes. That choice tells you a lot about the strategy behind the request.

The heart of her message was simple. Ebert argued that CCSD needs roughly $4,000 more per student to do the job families expect. She tied that number to a plan for the district's future, not just to keep the lights on. According to her office, a survey of about 50,000 people showed a clear pattern. People want high schools to offer more hands-on, real-life learning and more career-connected experiences. That means things like job training, apprenticeships, work with local employers, and classes that connect directly to real careers.

Those kinds of programs are not cheap. Career and technical education needs equipment, shop space, certified teachers, and partnerships with businesses. If the district wants to build more of that, it needs a steady source of money to pay for it year after year. That is why the funding ask and the survey results were presented together. One explains the other. The survey shows what the public wants, and the funding request is the price tag Ebert put on delivering it.

The timing matters. Ebert made this appeal while CCSD faces a projected multi-year decline in enrollment and budget. In most public school systems, money follows students. When fewer children are enrolled, the district receives less funding from the state. So Ebert was making her case at the exact moment the district's built-in revenue is expected to shrink. That is a hard spot to ask for more, and it explains why she took the message to business leaders who might help build public and political support.

It is worth being clear about what has and has not happened. As of this writing, this was a public lobbying push, not a done deal. Ebert was making an argument and building support. The final decision on school funding in Nevada runs through the state, not through a single speech to a chamber crowd. Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

School funding is one of those topics that can feel far away until you realize how close it actually is to your daily life. If you own a home in Clark County, part of your property tax bill already helps pay for public schools. If you rent, the same costs show up in the rent your landlord charges. And if you have kids, the money the district gets per student shapes their class sizes, their teachers, and the programs their school can offer. So this is not just a school board issue. It is a household issue.

Start with class size and staffing. When money per student is tight, districts often have to make hard choices. That can mean larger classes, fewer aides, cuts to elective programs, or trouble keeping good teachers who could earn more elsewhere. When funding grows, districts have more room to keep classes smaller and hold on to strong staff. The $4,000 per student that Ebert is chasing is meant to give the district that room, especially for the career-focused programs families said they want.

Now think about the career-connected piece. A high schooler who can train for a real job, earn a certificate, or step into an apprenticeship has a clearer path after graduation. For a valley where hospitality, health care, construction, and skilled trades drive so much of the economy, that link between school and work is huge. Local employers need trained workers, and families want their kids ready for good-paying jobs. That is exactly why Ebert took her pitch to the chamber of commerce. Business owners have a direct stake in whether local schools produce ready workers.

There is also a plain money angle for homeowners. Strong, well-funded schools tend to support strong home values. Buyers pay attention to school ratings and program options when they choose a neighborhood. A home zoned to a school with solid programs and a good reputation often draws more interest and holds its value better over time. When school funding is shaky, some families start looking at other zip codes or even other cities. That kind of movement can shift demand from one part of the valley to another.

Finally, there is the tax question that always sits underneath these debates. More funding usually has to come from somewhere. It can come from the state, from local sources, or from a mix. Residents have a real interest in knowing where the extra $4,000 per student would come from, because part of the answer could touch their own wallet. That is why it is fair to watch this closely and ask questions before any final plan takes shape.

Background and History

To understand this request, it helps to know how big CCSD is and how Nevada funds its schools. Clark County School District covers Las Vegas, Henderson, North Las Vegas, and the surrounding communities. It is one of the largest school districts in the entire United States, serving hundreds of thousands of students across hundreds of campuses. A district that size has enormous needs, from bus routes to building repairs to teacher pay, and even small changes in per-student funding add up to very large dollar figures across the whole system.

Nevada has long ranked near the bottom nationally in per-pupil spending, which is money spent per student each year. For years, parents, teachers, and advocates have argued that the state does not put enough into its classrooms compared with other states. That history is the backdrop for Ebert's request. When a superintendent asks for $4,000 more per student, she is speaking into a decades-long debate about whether Nevada invests enough in the children it is educating.

In recent years, the state has changed how it hands out school money, moving toward a funding formula meant to send dollars where students need them most. Even so, the overall size of the pot remains a constant point of tension. More money for schools often means a bigger ask of taxpayers or a shift in state priorities, and that makes every funding fight a political one.

Layered on top is the enrollment problem. CCSD, like many big districts, has seen shifts in how many students walk through its doors. Housing costs, birth rates, family moves, and the rise of charter and private options all play a part. Because state money follows enrollment, a projected multi-year drop in students means a projected drop in funding. That is the squeeze Ebert is trying to get ahead of. She is essentially arguing that the district cannot afford to shrink its way into weaker schools, and that a bigger per-student investment is needed to protect quality even as the head count changes.

What Happens Next

The next chapter depends on how much support Ebert can build. A speech to a chamber of commerce is a starting point, not a finish line. To turn a $4,000 per-student goal into real dollars, the district generally needs backing from state leaders, since Nevada's school funding largely runs through the state. That means the request will likely become part of a larger conversation with lawmakers, the governor's office, and other officials about how much the state can and should spend on education.

Watch for a few signs in the coming months. First, look for whether other business groups, parent organizations, and community leaders line up behind the request. Broad support makes it harder to ignore. Second, watch for any specific plan that spells out where the money would come from and how it would be spent. A clear plan tied to career and technical programs would be a sign the district is serious about turning the survey results into action.

Third, keep an eye on the enrollment and budget numbers as the new school year begins. If enrollment continues to slide, the pressure to protect per-student funding grows. If the district can show strong demand for its career-focused programs, that strengthens the case for more money. Each of these pieces will shape whether the $4,000 figure gains traction or fades into just another proposal.
