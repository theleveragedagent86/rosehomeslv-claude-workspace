Crossing Guard Hiring Events Across the Valley Ahead of School Year

Clark County held a string of crossing guard hiring events at valley libraries in late July 2026, with a goal of hiring about 75 new guards before students head back to class. The part-time jobs start at $17 an hour, and applicants must be at least 18 years old and pass a background check.

The events ran across several days and several neighborhoods. They started at the West Las Vegas Library on July 20, moved to the Green Valley Library in Henderson on July 21, then to the Windmill Library on July 23, and wrapped up at the East Las Vegas Library on July 28. Spreading the events out this way made it easier for people all over the valley to show up in person, ask questions, and start an application without a long drive.

This might sound like a small story, but it touches something every parent in Las Vegas cares about. It is about who stands at the corner when your child walks to school. Crossing guards are one of the first lines of defense for kids near busy streets, and this year that job carries extra weight. Southern Nevada has seen a troubling rise in students being hit by cars near schools, which makes filling these positions more than a routine hiring push.

What Happened

Clark County scheduled a set of in-person hiring events at public libraries around the valley during the second half of July 2026. The plan was simple and smart. Instead of asking everyone to apply online alone or drive to one central office, the county brought the hiring process out into the community. People could walk into a nearby library, meet staff face to face, get their questions answered, and get moving on the paperwork the same day.

The schedule covered four library stops on four different dates. The first event took place at the West Las Vegas Library on July 20. The next day, July 21, the events moved south to the Green Valley Library in Henderson. Two days after that, on July 23, the Windmill Library hosted a session. The last stop on the list was the East Las Vegas Library on July 28. By picking libraries in the west, in Henderson, in the central-south area, and in the east, the county tried to reach families and job seekers across a wide slice of the metro area.

The goal behind all of this was clear. Officials aimed to hire roughly 75 crossing guards to help cover school zones for the coming year. That is a meaningful number of open spots to fill in a short window before school starts. Crossing guard shortages are common across the country, and Clark County is a large district with many campuses, so keeping enough guards on the corners takes steady recruiting.

The jobs come with plain, straightforward terms. These are part-time positions that start at $17 an hour. To apply, a person needs to be at least 18 years old and needs to pass a background check. That background check is standard for any role that puts an adult in a position of trust near children. The part-time hours also fit certain people well, such as retirees, parents with kids in school, and folks who want extra income without a full 40-hour week.

Crossing guard work follows the school calendar and the school day. Guards usually work short shifts in the morning and again in the afternoon, timed around when students walk to and from school. That rhythm is part of why the role appeals to people who want a predictable, part-time schedule tied to the neighborhood where they already live. Holding these events right before the school year gave the county a chance to line up staffing so more corners have a guard on day one. Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

For families, the person standing at the crosswalk is not a small detail. A trained adult in a bright vest, holding a stop sign, changes how safe a corner feels and how safe it actually is. When a guard is present, drivers slow down. Kids learn to wait for the signal to cross. Cars stop when they are supposed to stop. An empty corner, on the other hand, leaves young walkers to judge fast traffic on their own, and that is a hard thing to ask of a child.

This year the stakes feel higher because of a real safety problem on valley roads. There has been a documented spike in students being struck by cars near schools. That is exactly the kind of danger a crossing guard is trained to prevent. Every corner that has a guard is a corner where a driver is more likely to notice children and come to a full stop. So filling these 75 spots is not just about staffing a payroll. It is about putting real protection where kids cross the street twice a day.

There is also a money side that matters to households. A part-time job at $17 an hour is a solid option for people who want steady, local, school-schedule work. Retirees who want to stay active and connected to their neighborhood often make excellent guards. Parents who already drop kids at the same school can add a shift that fits their day. College students, caregivers, and anyone looking for a second income stream near home can find these hours easy to work around. The pay is not a fortune, but for a short daily commitment close to home, it adds up.

These jobs also help the whole community, not just the people who take them. When a school has enough crossing guards, the streets around it run more smoothly during the busy drop-off and pickup hours. Traffic backs up less. Drivers feel calmer. Neighbors who live near a school see fewer near misses at the corner. That kind of daily order makes a neighborhood feel safer and more pleasant to live in, which is something buyers notice when they look at a home near a campus.

It also helps to remember how many kids walk or bike to school in the valley. Not every family drives. Some parents work early and cannot drop off. Some kids live close enough that walking is the daily routine. For those students, the corner is the whole trip. A guard turns a scary crossing into a simple one, and that is true whether a child walks alone, walks with friends, or rolls up on a bike. The more corners that have coverage, the more of these young walkers are protected on both legs of the trip.

Finally, there is a trust factor. When the county puts effort into staffing crossing guards, it signals that student safety is a real priority. Parents pay attention to that. In a year with rising pedestrian crashes near schools, seeing officials work hard to put guards on corners can offer a bit of reassurance during an otherwise worrying stretch.

Background and History

School crossing guards have been part of American life for generations. The basic idea has not changed much. An adult stands at a key crossing near a school, wears high-visibility clothing, and helps children cross safely while managing the flow of cars. In many communities the role is handled through the local school district, the county, or the police department. In Clark County these positions are part of the wider effort to keep students safe on their way to and from campus.

Staffing these roles has long been a challenge. Because the jobs are part-time and follow the school calendar, they can be hard to keep filled year after year. Guards retire, move, or take other work, and each summer brings a fresh push to hire and train enough people before classes start again. That is a common pattern in large, fast-growing areas, and the Las Vegas Valley has grown quickly for decades. More rooftops and more schools mean more corners that need a guard.

Using public libraries as hiring sites is a practical move with a nice side benefit. Libraries are trusted, familiar, easy-to-reach spots in almost every part of the valley. People of all ages already know where their branch is. By holding events at the West Las Vegas, Green Valley, Windmill, and East Las Vegas libraries, the county met residents in the places they already visit. That lowers the barrier for someone who might be curious about the job but unsure how to start.

The timing also fits a long pattern. Late summer is hiring season for anything tied to schools, from teachers and aides to bus drivers and crossing guards. Districts across the country race in July and August to be ready for the first bell. This round of Clark County events fit right into that yearly rhythm, aimed at having as many corners covered as possible when students returned. What Happens Next

With the events wrapping up at the East Las Vegas Library on July 28, the next steps move behind the scenes. People who applied will go through the standard process, which includes the background check that every applicant must pass. Once cleared, new guards typically get training so they know where to stand, how to use their stop sign and vest, how to read traffic, and how to guide children across safely. That training matters because a guard has to make quick, calm decisions with cars and kids in motion.

The bigger question is whether the county reaches its goal of about 75 new guards. If the events drew strong turnout, more corners will have coverage when school begins. If some spots stay open, families may see certain crossings without a guard, at least for a while. Hiring for these roles is rarely one and done, so it is common for recruiting to continue into the school year as needed. Anyone who missed the July events can usually still apply through the normal channels.

Parents should also watch their own school zones as the year starts. It is worth knowing whether the corner your child uses has a guard, and whether the crossing has good signs, painted crosswalks, and clear lines of sight. If a corner near your home looks risky or lacks coverage, that is useful to flag to the school and the county. Community feedback often helps decide where the next guard is placed.

Word of mouth also plays a role in what happens next. When one neighbor takes a guard job and likes it, they often tell friends and family, and more people apply. The library events were built to spark exactly that kind of local buzz. A person who stopped in out of curiosity may end up telling a retired neighbor who has time in the mornings, and that is how corners get filled over the following weeks.

In the wider picture, the rise in students being struck by cars near schools is likely to keep student safety in the spotlight. That could mean more attention on crossing guard staffing, on speed limits in school zones, and on the design of crosswalks around campuses. For now, the immediate goal is simple. Get enough trained guards on enough corners before the buses roll and the sidewalks fill with kids.

What You Can Do

If you want one of these jobs, the path is clear. You must be at least 18 and able to pass a background check. Even though the July library events have passed, you can still look for crossing guard openings through Clark County's normal job channels and apply from there. The pay starts at $17 an hour, the hours are part-time, and the schedule follows the school day, which fits a lot of lifestyles.

If you are a parent, get to know your child's route to school. Walk it with them once before classes start. Point out the safe corner, the crosswalk, and the spot where the guard stands. Teach your child to wait for the signal and to make eye contact with drivers. If a crossing near your home has no guard or feels unsafe, tell the school office and share your concern with the county so they know where help is needed.

If you drive near a school, slow down and stay alert during the morning and afternoon rush. Watch for guards, stop when they raise the sign, and give kids plenty of room. Small choices behind the wheel protect the children in your own community.
