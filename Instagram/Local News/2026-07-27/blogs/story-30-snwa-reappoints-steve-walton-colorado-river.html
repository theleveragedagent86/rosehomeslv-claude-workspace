SNWA Board Reappoints Boulder City's Steve Walton to Colorado River Commission

The Southern Nevada Water Authority (SNWA) Board voted to reappoint Boulder City Council Member Steve Walton to the Colorado River Commission of Nevada for a second two-year term. That keeps a Boulder City voice on the small board that sets policy for how Nevada uses its share of Colorado River water and power, and it happens during one of the worst droughts the river has ever seen.

This may sound like a quiet government story. It is not. The Colorado River is the main reason more than two million people can live in the desert here in southern Nevada. Almost all of our drinking water comes from Lake Mead, which is filled by that river. The people who sit on the Colorado River Commission help decide how our region shares the water when there is not enough to go around. So when a local leader from Boulder City keeps his seat on that board, it matters to every family, business, and homeowner in the Las Vegas Valley.

In this article I will walk you through what happened, why it touches your home and your neighborhood, and what could come next. I will keep it plain and simple. My goal is to help you understand the story behind the headline so you can make smart choices about where you live and where you buy.

What Happened

The SNWA Board of Directors voted to reappoint Steve Walton to the Colorado River Commission of Nevada. This is his second two-year term. Walton is a member of the Boulder City Council, and he has served on the SNWA Board since 2022. His reappointment was announced in mid-July 2026.

Let me explain the two groups involved, because their names sound alike and it is easy to mix them up. The Southern Nevada Water Authority, or SNWA, is the agency that brings drinking water to the Las Vegas Valley. It is made up of the local water and wastewater agencies that serve our area. The SNWA Board is the group of elected officials who guide that agency.

The Colorado River Commission of Nevada is a different body. It is the state agency that manages Nevada's rights to Colorado River water and to the electric power made at dams like Hoover Dam. The commission has seven members, called commissioners. Here is the key part. The governor appoints four of those commissioners. The other three are appointed by the SNWA Board, and those three must also be members of the SNWA Board. Steve Walton is one of those three. So he wears two hats at once. He sits on the SNWA Board, and through that seat he also serves on the Colorado River Commission.

The commission sets policy for how Nevada uses its slice of the river. Nevada has the smallest share of the seven states that draw from the Colorado River, so every drop counts more here than almost anywhere else. The commission also handles contracts for the low cost hydropower that Hoover Dam produces. That power helps run pumps, homes, and businesses across the state.

By voting to keep Walton, the SNWA Board chose to keep steady, experienced leadership on the commission at a time when the river is under real stress. Walton already knows the players, the plans, and the problems. That continuity means Boulder City, the town that is home to Hoover Dam, still has a direct seat at the table where big water decisions get made.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Here is the simple truth. Water is the base under every home and every deal in southern Nevada. Roughly 90 percent of the drinking water in the Las Vegas Valley comes from the Colorado River through Lake Mead. If the river is healthy and the rules are fair, our region can keep growing. If the river shrinks and the rules turn against us, growth gets harder and life gets more expensive. So the people who help set river policy have a real effect on your daily life, even if you never hear their names.

Think about what depends on a steady water supply. New homes need water hookups before they can be built. Schools, parks, and hospitals need water to serve growing neighborhoods. Resorts, restaurants, and shops need water to run and to hire workers. When leaders make smart, steady choices about water, builders and businesses feel safe planning for the future. That confidence is part of what keeps our housing market strong.

Now think about the drought. Lake Mead has dropped to some of its lowest levels ever. National reports in 2026 have warned that the lake could fall even further in the next year or two. Lower water can also mean less power from Hoover Dam, which raises costs for everyone. When the lake is low, the states that share the river have to negotiate hard about who gives up water and how much. Nevada, with its small share, needs strong, calm voices in those talks. That is exactly the kind of role the Colorado River Commission plays.

For homeowners, this connects straight to your property value. Buyers pay a premium for a place they trust will still work in ten or twenty years. A region that manages water well is a region people want to move to. That demand supports home prices. A region that fumbles its water future scares buyers away and can soften values. So a stable, experienced commissioner is quietly good news for your equity.

There is also a piece of hidden engineering that shows how serious this is. Because Lake Mead has dropped so far, the SNWA built a deep intake, often called the third straw, that can pull water from near the very bottom of the lake. The agency also built a low lake level pumping station so it can keep sending water to our taps even if the lake falls to near dead pool. In plain words, our region spent hundreds of millions of dollars to make sure the water keeps flowing during the worst case. That kind of long term planning is exactly the mindset the commission brings to the bigger river talks. It is a big reason our supply has stayed reliable while headlines about the lake have grown scary.

Boulder City residents have an extra reason to care. Their town is the home of Hoover Dam and sits right next to Lake Mead. Having a local council member on the commission means Boulder City's needs are less likely to get lost when the state makes big calls. That local voice can help protect the small town feel and steady growth that make Boulder City special. Boulder City also has some of the strictest growth control rules in the state, which caps how many new homes can be built each year. That makes local water and land decisions even more important to home values there, since supply of new homes is already tight.

Background and History

The Colorado River has been shared under a set of rules that goes back more than one hundred years. In 1922, seven states signed the Colorado River Compact. That deal split the river between an upper group of states and a lower group of states. Nevada, Arizona, and California make up the lower group. Nevada was given the smallest share of all, just 300,000 acre feet a year, because at the time very few people lived here. No one back then guessed that Las Vegas would grow into a metro area of more than two million people.

The Colorado River Commission of Nevada was created to manage the state's share of that water and the power made at the river's dams. Over the years, the commission has helped Nevada stretch its small allocation through smart planning, recycling, and conservation. Southern Nevada now recycles almost all of the water used indoors and sends it back to Lake Mead, which earns the region extra credit toward its supply. Interestingly, Boulder City has been the one Lake Mead user that has lagged on recycling, though it has been working to catch up.

The last 25 years have been the hardest stretch in the river's recorded history. A long drought, tied to a hotter and drier climate, has drained Lake Mead and Lake Powell. In 2021 the federal government declared its first ever shortage on the river, which forced Nevada and Arizona to take cuts. Since then, the states have been in almost constant talks about how to keep the system from crashing. The current set of operating rules is due to expire in 2026, so new long term rules are being worked out right now. That is why steady leadership on the commission is so valuable this year.

What Happens Next

Walton's new term runs for two years. During that time, the biggest job in front of the commission and the whole river region is settling on the next set of operating rules for the Colorado River. The rules that have guided the system are set to end in 2026, and federal officials want new guidelines in place to cover the years ahead. Those talks decide how the seven states will share cuts when the lakes drop low. Nevada needs its representatives to protect our small but vital share.

Expect to see continued negotiations between the upper and lower basin states, with the federal Bureau of Reclamation in the middle. There may be new agreements on how much water each state gives up in dry years. There could also be more money and rules aimed at saving water, paying farmers to use less, and boosting recycling. Nevada leaders, including the commissioners, will keep pushing conservation and the reuse of water, which is our region's strongest card.

Closer to home, watch for the SNWA and local agencies to keep tightening water rules. In 2021 Nevada passed a law that requires the removal of most nonfunctional grass, the kind of decorative turf that no one walks on, along streets and in business areas across the Las Vegas Valley. More steps like these are likely as long as the drought continues. For buyers and homeowners, that means yards, pools, and landscaping rules will keep changing, so it pays to know the local rules before you buy or remodel.

There is also money on the table. State and federal programs have been paying water users to cut back and paying homeowners rebates to tear out grass. The SNWA offers one of the most generous grass rebate programs in the country, which pays you per square foot to replace lawn with desert friendly landscaping. Expect these programs to grow as leaders push to save every drop. If you own a home with a lot of old grass, this is a chance to lower your water bill and pocket a rebate at the same time.

You can also expect Boulder City to keep working on its own water recycling so it does its part for the shared supply. As the town that hosts Hoover Dam, Boulder City is in the spotlight, and Walton's seat helps make sure its interests are heard.

What You Can Do

You do not need to be a water expert to make smart moves. Start by learning the water rules in the neighborhood you like. Ask about grass limits, pool size rules, and any special fees. These rules affect what you can build and how much your yard will cost to keep up. Knowing them before you buy saves money and stress later.

Next, do your part to save water at home. Fix leaks, use water smart plants, and take advantage of the SNWA rebates that pay you to swap grass for desert friendly landscaping. Saving water is good for the region and good for your wallet, since lower use means lower bills.

You can also stay involved. SNWA Board and Colorado River Commission meetings are open to the public, and their agendas are posted online. If a water rule could change your plans, it is worth watching. When you understand how these decisions are made, you can plan your home purchase or remodel with more confidence.
