CCSD Board of Trustees Candidates Share Their Visions Ahead of the 2026 Election

Candidates running for three seats on the Clark County School District Board of Trustees have started laying out their plans, and the big themes are clear: how kids learn to read, how the district spends its money, and how the board itself is run. This matters to almost every family in the Las Vegas Valley because the people who win these seats will help steer one of the largest school districts in the country. The primary election is set for June 9, and the general election follows on November 3.

If you own a home here, rent here, or are raising kids here, this election reaches into your daily life. School board decisions shape the quality of nearby schools, and school quality is one of the biggest things that drives home values and where families choose to live. Las Vegas Weekly recently spoke with candidates about their platforms, and the picture that came out was a district at a crossroads. Some candidates want to protect current programs. Others want big changes to how the district is governed and how it reports results to the public. Below, I will walk through what was said, why it matters to you, how we got here, and what you can actually do about it before you vote.

What Happened

Candidates for three contested Clark County School District Board of Trustees seats have begun sharing their platforms with voters ahead of the 2026 election. According to Las Vegas Weekly, the candidates focused on three main topics: reading instruction, the district budget, and governance, which is just a fancy word for how the board makes decisions and holds the district accountable. The primary election is June 9, and the general election is November 3.

Reading instruction was one of the loudest topics. Candidates talked about how young students in Clark County are learning to read and whether the district is doing enough to make sure kids can read well by the time they finish third grade. This is a hot subject in the valley right now because the board has been debating changes to the state's "Read by 3" literacy rules. Some candidates want stronger reading support and clearer results. Others want to make sure struggling readers get help without being held back a grade. Reading came up again and again because it touches so many families with young children.

The budget was the second big theme. Clark County School District runs on a very large budget, and every dollar is fought over. Candidates talked about where money should go, whether it should reach classrooms and teachers more directly, and how to handle rising costs. Air conditioning repairs, teacher pay, building upkeep, and classroom supplies all compete for the same pool of money. Candidates offered different ideas about spending priorities, but nearly all of them agreed the district needs to show taxpayers exactly where the money goes.

The third theme was governance. This is about trust. Some candidates argued the board needs to work better as a team, communicate more openly, and rebuild public confidence after years of tension. Others said the board needs to be more independent from district staff and ask harder questions before approving big plans. Governance may sound boring, but it decides whether the other two topics, reading and budget, actually get fixed. A board that cannot agree or cannot follow through will struggle to deliver results no matter how good the ideas sound.

It is worth being clear about what we do not yet know. The specific candidate names and their exact positions are still coming into focus as the races develop, so I am sticking to the verifiable facts here: three seats are contested, the primary is June 9, the general is November 3, and the shared themes are reading instruction, budget, and governance. As the campaign moves forward, expect the field to narrow after the June primary, with the top finishers in each race moving on to November.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

School board races do not get the same attention as big national elections, but they may touch your daily life more directly. The Clark County School District serves hundreds of thousands of students across the valley. The trustees you elect will vote on policies that decide how your local school is run, what programs it offers, and how safe and well kept the buildings are. If you have kids in school, this is obvious. But even if you do not, these races still reach you.

Here is the connection to your home. Buyers care a lot about schools. When a neighborhood is served by strong, well run schools, homes there tend to hold their value better and sell faster. When schools struggle, buyers get nervous, and that can put pressure on prices. As a local real estate agent, I see this every week. Families relocating to Las Vegas almost always ask about school ratings before they ask about anything else in the house. The people on the school board help shape those ratings over time through the decisions they make on reading programs, budgets, and staffing.

The reading debate matters because early literacy is one of the strongest signs of how a child will do later in school. Kids who read well by third grade are far more likely to keep up in every other subject. Kids who fall behind early often stay behind. So when candidates argue about reading rules, they are really arguing about whether thousands of young students get the help they need at the exact moment it counts most. That has ripple effects for years, from graduation rates to the local workforce that employers depend on.

The budget matters because it is your money. Much of the school district's funding comes from taxes, including property taxes tied to homes across Clark County. When you pay your property tax bill, part of that supports local schools. So you have a direct stake in whether that money is spent wisely. Candidates who promise more classroom spending, better teacher pay, or tighter accountability are talking about how your dollars get used. Watching how they answer budget questions tells you a lot about how they will govern.

Governance matters because it is the glue. A board that fights and stalls cannot deliver on reading or budgets, no matter how good the promises sound. Voters across the valley have watched school board meetings grow tense in recent years. This election gives residents a chance to pick leaders who can actually work together and get things done. That is why paying attention now, before the June 9 primary, is worth your time.

Background and History

The Clark County School District is one of the biggest public school systems in the United States. It covers the entire valley, from Las Vegas and Henderson to North Las Vegas and the outlying communities. Running a district this large is a huge job, and the Board of Trustees sits at the top of it. Trustees are elected officials, chosen by voters in districts across the county, and they set policy, approve the budget, and provide oversight of district leadership.

Over the past few years, the district has faced a string of tough challenges. There have been debates over school start times, cellphone rules, campus safety, and how to keep air conditioning working in every building through the desert heat. There have also been fights over money, since the district has struggled to stretch its budget across hundreds of schools and tens of thousands of employees. These pressures have made the board's work harder and have put trustees under a bright public spotlight.

Reading has become one of the sharpest fault lines. Nevada's "Read by 3" approach was built around the idea that students should be reading at grade level by the end of third grade, with support and, in some cases, the possibility of repeating a grade for kids who are far behind. Recently, the board has moved to soften some of the retention parts of that policy. Supporters of the change say holding kids back can hurt them and that struggling readers need help, not a repeated grade. Critics say weakening the rules reduces accountability and lets the district off the hook for early reading results. That debate is now spilling directly into the 2026 campaign.

This election arrives at a moment when many residents are paying closer attention to local government than they used to. People want to know how their tax dollars are spent and whether the leaders they elect are delivering. The three contested trustee seats give voters a real chance to weigh in. The choices made in June and November will help set the district's direction for years to come.

What Happens Next

The next major date is the June 9 primary. In the primary, voters narrow the field of candidates in each contested race. Depending on how many people run and the local rules for each seat, the top finishers move forward. If a candidate wins by a large enough margin under the rules, a race can sometimes be settled early, but most contested seats with several candidates will carry forward to the general election.

The general election is November 3. That is when voters make the final choice for each of the three seats. Between June and November, expect the candidates who advance to campaign harder. You will likely see more public forums, mailers, interviews, and social media posts as they try to reach voters. This is the stretch where their positions on reading, budget, and governance should get clearer and more detailed, which gives you more to compare.

Watch for a few things as the campaign unfolds. First, look for specifics on reading. A candidate who can explain exactly how they would improve early literacy is easier to judge than one who speaks in slogans. Second, watch the budget answers. Ask yourself whether a candidate can name real priorities and tradeoffs, since every dollar spent one place is a dollar not spent somewhere else. Third, pay attention to how candidates talk about working with others. Governance is a team sport, and a trustee who cannot build agreement will struggle to deliver.

Also keep an eye on voter registration deadlines and your options for voting, including early voting and mail ballots, which Nevada offers. Making a plan to vote early can save you a trip on election day. As the dates get closer, the county will publish official information on where and how to cast your ballot. I will keep following these races and share updates as the field takes shape and the candidates lay out clearer plans.

What You Can Do

You have real power in this election, and it starts with a few simple steps. First, make sure you are registered to vote at your current address. If you have moved recently, update your registration so your ballot reaches you and you are voting in the right district. Nevada makes registration and updates fairly easy online, and doing it early avoids last minute stress.

Second, learn the candidates. As the races develop, look for candidate forums, interviews, and official statements. Focus on the three big themes: reading instruction, budget, and governance. Ask which trustee seat covers your area so you know exactly which race is yours to decide. Following local news outlets and the district's public meeting schedule is a good way to hear directly from the people running.

Third, make a voting plan. Mark June 9 for the primary and November 3 for the general election on your calendar. Decide whether you will vote early, by mail, or in person, and know your local polling options. If you have friends or neighbors with kids in Clark County schools, talk with them about what they are seeing on the ground. First hand experience is some of the best information you can get.
