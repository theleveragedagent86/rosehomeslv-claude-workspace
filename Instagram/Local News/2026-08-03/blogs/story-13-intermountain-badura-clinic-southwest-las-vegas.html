Intermountain Health Opens 90,000-Square-Foot Badura Clinic in Southwest Las Vegas

Intermountain Health cut the ribbon on its new Badura clinic on July 30, 2026, and it is now the largest clinic the health system runs in all of Nevada. The three-story building sits near Durango Drive and the 215 Beltway in fast-growing southwest Las Vegas. At 90,000 square feet, it is also the first building Intermountain has built from the ground up in the state. That matters because it puts a big, modern medical hub right in the middle of one of the valley's busiest and newest neighborhoods.

For years, families in southwest Las Vegas have watched homes, schools, and shopping centers go up fast. Doctors and clinics did not always keep pace. Many people had to drive across town or wait weeks for an appointment. The Badura clinic is meant to help close that gap. It brings many kinds of care together under one roof, including services from Shriners Children's. Instead of driving to three or four different offices, a family may be able to handle several needs in one visit to one address.

This is a real estate story as much as a health story. Where new medical buildings open, home values and buyer interest often follow. In this article I will break down what happened, why it matters for people who live here, the history behind the project, what comes next, and simple steps you can take if you live or want to live in the area.

What Happened

On July 30, 2026, Intermountain Health held a ribbon-cutting for its new Badura clinic in southwest Las Vegas. The event marked the official opening of a three-story, 90,000-square-foot building. Leaders, staff, and community members gathered to mark the moment. The clinic sits close to Durango Drive and the 215 Beltway, an area that has seen heavy home and business growth over the past several years.

The size is the headline number. At 90,000 square feet, this is the largest clinic Intermountain Health operates in Nevada. To picture that, think of a building with three full floors of exam rooms, offices, and care spaces stacked on top of each other. That is a lot of room for doctors, nurses, and support staff to work in at the same time.

There is another first here that is easy to miss. This is Intermountain's first ground-up build in Nevada. That means the company did not move into an old store or an empty office and fix it up. It planned and built the whole thing new, from the foundation to the roof. When a health system builds new instead of renting used space, it usually signals a long-term plan. You do not pour that much money into a brand-new building unless you expect to stay and grow.

The clinic is built to bring many specialties together in one place. The plan is to consolidate care, which is a simple idea with a big payoff. Instead of scattering different doctors across different buildings around the valley, the Badura site gathers them under one roof. A patient who needs to see more than one type of provider may be able to do it in a single trip.

The clinic also includes Shriners Children's services. Shriners is well known for helping kids with orthopedic conditions, burns, and other complex needs, often at low or no cost to families. Having those services inside a large, easy-to-reach clinic is a meaningful add for families in the southwest valley. Parents who once traveled far for pediatric specialty care may find help closer to home.

Put the pieces together and the picture is clear. Intermountain took its biggest Nevada clinic, built it new, placed it in a booming part of town, packed it with specialties, and added children's care. The July 30 opening was not just another office opening its doors. It was a major expansion of the health system's footprint in Las Vegas, aimed right at an area that has been growing faster than its medical services.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Health care access is one of the quiet things that shapes daily life. When a good clinic is close by, small problems get handled early. When care is far away or hard to book, people wait, and small problems turn into big ones. The Badura clinic lands in a part of the valley where the population has grown fast, so more nearby care is welcome news for a lot of households.

Think about the everyday value. A short drive to the doctor means less time off work and less time stuck in traffic. Parents with young kids know how hard it is to load everyone in the car for a long trip across town. A large clinic near Durango and the 215 cuts that trip down for many families in the southwest. That is time back in people's days.

Bringing specialties together also helps. In the old model, you might see your primary doctor in one building, then drive to another office for a specialist, then a third for tests. Each stop means a new front desk, new paperwork, and often a new wait. A clinic that consolidates care can smooth that out. It is easier to keep your records in one system and to move between providers who share a building.

The Shriners Children's piece deserves its own note. Families with kids who need orthopedic or specialty care sometimes travel long distances to find it. Adding those services in southwest Las Vegas means some of those families can stay closer to home. That saves money, time, and stress during what are often hard stretches for a family.

Homes with strong access to doctors, hospitals, and clinics tend to hold their appeal. When a major health system builds its largest state clinic in your area, it adds to the list of reasons a buyer picks your neighborhood over another. It signals that big, careful players believe in the area's future.

New medical buildings can also bring jobs. Clinics need doctors, nurses, techs, front-desk staff, cleaners, and more. Those workers often want to live near where they work. Steady local jobs and new residents can support home demand over time. A 90,000-square-foot clinic is not just a place to get care. It is an economic anchor that can help hold up a neighborhood's value for years.

Background and History

To understand why this clinic matters, look at how southwest Las Vegas grew. Over the past decade, this corner of the valley filled in with new homes, master-planned communities, and shopping. The area around Durango Drive and the 215 Beltway went from open desert and edge-of-town lots to a busy zone full of families. Roads widened, schools opened, and rooftops multiplied.

Health care often trails housing. Builders can put up a subdivision fast, but a hospital or large clinic takes years to plan, fund, and open. So new neighborhoods can grow for a while before big medical services catch up. That gap is exactly what many southwest residents have felt. Plenty of homes, not enough nearby care. The Badura clinic is a direct answer to that mismatch.

Intermountain Health itself is a large nonprofit health system with deep roots in the western United States. It has expanded its presence in Nevada in recent years, adding clinics and services across the valley. Until now, though, it had grown here mostly by moving into existing spaces. The Badura clinic changes that story. It is the system's first ground-up build in Nevada, which is a step up in commitment.

Building new from scratch lets a health system design a space around modern care. Older buildings often have to be squeezed to fit new needs. A new build can be shaped for the way medicine works today, with room for many specialties, better flow for patients, and space to add services later. That is why a first ground-up project is a bigger deal than just another office lease.

The choice of location tells its own story. Placing the largest Nevada clinic near Durango and the 215 is a bet on where the valley's growth is heading. Health systems study population trends closely before they build. Choosing the southwest signals that Intermountain sees this area as a long-term center of demand. For homeowners, that outside vote of confidence is worth paying attention to.
