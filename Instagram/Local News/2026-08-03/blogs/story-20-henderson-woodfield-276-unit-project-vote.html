Henderson Planning Commission to Weigh 276-Unit Woodfield Project at Volunteer and Gillespie

On August 13, the Henderson Planning Commission will decide whether to move forward a 276-unit apartment project called Woodfield on 9.1 acres at Volunteer and Gillespie. For anyone who lives, rents, or owns a home in this fast-growing corner of the valley, that vote matters because it will help set the tone for how much new housing gets built here, how dense it will be, and what your streets look like a few years from now.

This is not a groundbreaking. It is an early but important step in the approval path. The Planning Commission is the body that reviews land-use and zoning requests before they move on to the City Council or, in some cases, become final. The Woodfield item asks the city to allow a multifamily community under RH-36 zoning, which is a higher-density residential category. On the same night, the commission is also expected to weigh the cancellation of the Henderson West development agreement, a separate deal covering roughly 103 acres near St. Rose Parkway.

Below, I break down what is on the table, why it affects your home value and your daily commute, the history behind Henderson's building boom, and exactly how you can weigh in before the meeting. My goal is to give you the facts in plain language so you can form your own opinion, whether you cheer for more housing or worry about traffic and density.

What Happened

The core item is a proposal for a 276-unit residential project known as Woodfield on about 9.1 acres near the intersection of Volunteer Street and Gillespie Street in Henderson. The request centers on RH-36 zoning. In simple terms, RH stands for high-density residential, and the number points to how many homes per acre the category is built around. A category in the mid-30s per acre is meant for apartments, townhomes, or similar attached housing rather than single detached homes on large lots.

Do the math on the site itself. Roughly 276 units spread across about 9.1 acres works out to around 30 homes per acre. That is squarely in apartment territory. It tells you this is a rental-style or attached community, not a subdivision of standalone houses. Projects at this density usually include shared parking, a clubhouse or leasing office, a pool, and landscaped common areas. The exact building heights, unit mix, and amenities are spelled out in the plans the commission will review, and those details can change as staff and commissioners ask for adjustments. The Planning Commission's job at this stage is to check the request against the city's rules and long-range plans. Commissioners look at whether the zoning fits the surrounding area, how traffic will flow in and out, how the project handles drainage and utilities, and whether the design meets Henderson's standards. They can approve it, approve it with conditions, send it back for changes, or recommend denial. Some items the commission decides outright, while others it forwards with a recommendation to the City Council for a final vote.

The second big item on the agenda is the proposed cancellation of the Henderson West development agreement. A development agreement is a long-term contract between the city and a landowner or builder that locks in the rules for a large piece of land, sometimes for many years. This one covers roughly 103 acres near St. Rose Parkway. Canceling it does not by itself decide what gets built there. Instead, it clears away an older set of terms, which usually means a property will be governed by current city zoning and code, or that a new plan will come forward later under fresh rules. Both items share a theme. They shape how residential growth unfolds in the southern and western parts of Henderson, an area that has drawn steady interest from builders because of its access to freeways, shopping, and newer schools. Together they signal that the city is actively managing where density lands and how much flexibility past agreements still carry.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Here is the honest picture. Henderson has been one of the most in-demand places to live in the whole valley, and demand pushes prices up. Local market data has pointed to a Henderson median home price near $540,000, with more affordable entry points showing up in communities like Whitney Ranch and Cadence. When a city keeps growing but does not add enough homes, prices and rents climb faster than paychecks. New multifamily projects like Woodfield are one of the main tools a city has to add supply and take pressure off the market.

If you rent, a 276-unit community is a meaningful addition. More apartments in the pipeline can help slow rent increases over time, because renters gain more choices and landlords compete harder for tenants. That matters in a valley where studio rents recently spiked to a 13-month high even as some larger units cooled. Every new building will not lower your rent overnight, but supply is the long game that keeps housing within reach for teachers, nurses, service workers, and young families.

If you own a home nearby, your interests are more mixed, and that is fair. On one hand, nearby apartments can bring more foot traffic to local shops and can support better services. On the other hand, higher density can mean more cars on your street, fuller schools, and a different feel to the neighborhood. These are real trade-offs, and they are exactly why the public hearing exists. Your voice on traffic studies, buffer landscaping, building height, and entry points can shape the conditions the city attaches to an approval.

There is also a bigger money angle. Housing supply affects the whole regional economy. When homes are scarce, employers struggle to attract workers, and families spend more of their income on rent instead of local businesses. Adding attainable housing near jobs and freeways helps keep the valley competitive. So even if you live across town, the pattern set by projects like Woodfield ripples outward. The choices Henderson makes about density today shape affordability across Clark County tomorrow.

Finally, the Henderson West cancellation matters because it shows how the city handles older land deals. Buyers and investors watch these moves closely. When a development agreement goes away, it can reset expectations for a whole area, sometimes opening the door to new plans and sometimes slowing things down while fresh proposals take shape. If you own land or a home near St. Rose Parkway, this is worth tracking.

Background and History

Henderson has grown from a wartime industrial town into the second-largest city in Nevada, and it did not happen by accident. For decades, master-planned communities like Green Valley, Anthem, Inspirada, and Cadence have drawn families with newer homes, parks, and schools. That success created a steady appetite for more housing of every type, from luxury villas in MacDonald Highlands to entry-level condos and apartments closer to the freeways.

The Volunteer and Gillespie area sits in the southern part of the city, near corridors that connect to the 215 Beltway and Interstate 15. Land near these routes is attractive to apartment builders because residents can reach jobs on the Strip, in the southwest valley, and in Henderson's own business parks without long drives. Over the past several years, the city has approved a mix of single-family subdivisions and higher-density housing across this zone as it tries to balance growth with quality of life.

RH-36 style zoning is a familiar tool in this effort. Henderson uses higher-density residential categories to concentrate apartments in spots that can handle the traffic and are close to services, rather than scattering density into quiet single-family pockets. This is how many cities try to add homes without changing the character of established neighborhoods. Woodfield fits that pattern, placing a larger community on a compact site near existing roads and commercial areas.

Development agreements like the Henderson West deal have also played a big role in the city's growth. During boom years and the recovery that followed, cities often signed long-term agreements to give builders certainty on large tracts. Years later, some of those agreements no longer match current market conditions or city goals, which is why cancellations and updates come before the Planning Commission from time to time. Seeing one on the agenda is a normal part of a maturing city managing land it approved long ago.

What Happens Next

The key date is August 13, when the Henderson Planning Commission is set to take up both items. At that meeting, city staff will present their analysis, the applicant may speak, and members of the public can comment before commissioners vote. The commission can approve, approve with conditions, continue the item to a later date, or recommend denial. Continuances are common when commissioners want more information or when an applicant agrees to tweak the plans.

For the Woodfield project, watch for conditions. Even when a project moves forward, the city often attaches requirements covering traffic improvements, landscaping buffers, wall heights, lighting, and the timing of construction. These conditions are where neighborhood concerns get addressed. If you attend and speak, specific and constructive comments about a particular intersection or a particular buffer tend to carry more weight than general opposition.

Depending on how the request is structured, the item may need a final decision from the Henderson City Council after the Planning Commission acts. Zoning changes and certain approvals often require that second step. The cancellation of the Henderson West development agreement may follow a similar path. If you care about either item, the smart move is to confirm whether a City Council date follows, so you do not miss the final vote. [NOT VERIFIED: whether each item is final at the Planning Commission or advances to the City Council is not stated in the public summary.]

After approvals, real construction still takes time. Builders must pull permits, finish engineering, secure financing, and schedule grading before anything rises out of the ground. For a 276-unit community, it is realistic to expect many months of site work and construction before the first residents move in. So even a yes vote on August 13 is the start of a longer process, not the finish line.

Ryan's Take

I look at projects like Woodfield through a simple lens. Our valley does not have enough homes for the people who want to live here, and that shortage is the single biggest driver of rising prices and rents. Well-placed apartment communities near freeways and jobs are exactly the kind of supply that helps working families stay in Henderson instead of getting priced out. On balance, I see this project as a healthy sign that the city is still building.

That said, I never dismiss neighborhood concerns, because they are usually about real things like traffic, parking, and how a project fits its surroundings. The good news is that the public hearing is built to handle those worries. When residents show up with specific, practical input, the city can attach conditions that make a project a better neighbor. Density done thoughtfully, with good design and smart traffic planning, adds far more than it takes away.

If you are a homeowner nearby, do not panic about your value. Across the valley, new housing has gone hand in hand with strong long-term appreciation, because growth brings jobs, shops, and services. If you are a renter or a first-time buyer, more supply is your friend. Either way, stay informed and get involved. The families who show up shape the outcome, and I would rather you help write the conditions than watch from the sidelines.

What You Can Do

First, mark August 13 on your calendar and plan to follow the Henderson Planning Commission meeting. You can find the agenda, staff reports, and meeting details on the City of Henderson website under the Planning Commission page. Agendas usually post a few days before the meeting, so check back close to the date for the exact time, location, and the specific case numbers for the Woodfield project and the Henderson West cancellation.

Second, decide how you want to participate. You can attend in person to give public comment, or in many cases submit written comments ahead of time. If you speak, keep it short and specific. Talk about the exact intersection, the buffer, the building height, or the entry point that concerns you, and suggest a fix. That kind of input is what commissioners can act on through conditions of approval.

Third, do your homework on the area before you form a final view. Drive Volunteer and Gillespie at rush hour, look at nearby schools and parks, and picture how 276 new homes would fit. If you own property nearby, this is also a smart time to understand how added housing supply could affect your value and your options. Knowledge helps you speak with confidence, whether you support the project or push for changes.
