The New Construction Buyer's Defense Checklist

The questions builders hope you don't ask, and the costs they don't put on the sign.

Before you fall in love with a floor plan

A new build feels safe. Clean walls, a warranty, a friendly face at the model home. But that friendly face works for the builder, not for you. This checklist puts you back on even footing. Print it, bring it, and check every box before you sign anything.

One thing to know up front: in almost every Las Vegas new build, the builder pays your agent's commission. Bringing your own representation usually costs you nothing.

01Before you walk into the model home
Bring your own agent on your first visit. Most builders pay your agent, so it costs you nothing. But many require your agent to be registered on visit one. Walk in alone and you can lose the right to bring one later, with no discount for going without.
Know who the sales rep represents. They are friendly, and they work for the builder's interests, not yours.
Don't sign the registration card first. Hold off on any form until you have decided on representation.
02The contract was written to protect the builder
Read every page, or have it reviewed. Builder contracts are drafted by the builder's lawyers. Have your agent or an attorney read it.
Find the delay clause. If the home is finished late, what happens? Can you walk, and do you get anything? Many builders owe you nothing for delays.
Find the cancellation terms. Can the builder cancel on you, and what part of your deposit is at risk?
Confirm your price is firm. Ask in writing whether material or tariff cost increases can be passed to you. Tariffs have added roughly $7,500 to $17,500 per home in 2026.
Get every promise in writing. Incentives, upgrades, and credits mean nothing at closing unless they are in the contract.

03The money behind the pricethe costs not on the sign
The big Las Vegas one
SID or LID bonds. Ask if the community has a Special Improvement District (common in Summerlin and Skye Canyon) or a Local Improvement District (common in Henderson). These are bond assessments billed on top of your property taxes. Get in writing:
  • The annual amount (commonly $500 to $3,000 or more)
  • How many years remain (often 10 to 20)
  • That it is billed twice a year, transfers to you as owner, and carries about a 3% penalty for early payoff
  • That an unpaid SID or LID can lead to foreclosure
HOA. Get the monthly amount, exactly what it covers, and any history of special assessments. Ask for the financial disclosures that Nevada's transparency rules require.
Property taxes. Good news for Nevada: homes are assessed at 35% of taxable value, the effective rate is roughly half a percent, your primary residence increase is capped at 3% a year, and there is no surprise supplemental tax bill like California has. Still, confirm the estimate for this specific home.
Lot premium. If you are paying extra for the lot, ask exactly what for, and whether that premium tends to hold value at resale.
04The lender trapincentives with strings
Know the incentives have strings. Rate buydowns, closing-cost credits, and design-center dollars are usually tied to using the builder's preferred lender.
Compare the full Loan Estimate. Get the builder lender's Loan Estimate, then at least one outside quote. Compare the rate and the fees, not just the headline rate.
Understand buydowns. A 2/1 or 3/2/1 buydown lowers your payment only temporarily. Ask what the payment becomes once it ends, and make sure you can afford the permanent rate.
Make sure the incentive is real money. If the price quietly rose to fund a "free" credit, it is not free.

05Inspections, yes, even on a brand-new home
New does not mean flawless. About 9 out of 10 new homes have at least one significant issue at inspection.
Get a pre-drywall inspection (roughly $200 to $400) before the walls close up. This is your one chance to see the wiring, plumbing, and framing.
Get a final independent inspection (roughly $310 to $730) before closing, separate from the builder's own walkthrough.
Do a blue-tape walkthrough and put every item in writing before you sign off.
06The warranty, know what is actually covered
Understand Nevada's 1-2-10 warranty. Year 1 covers workmanship, Year 2 covers systems (plumbing, electrical, HVAC), and Years 3 through 10 cover the structure.
Know your legal rights. Nevada law (NRS Chapter 40) gives the builder a 90-day window to respond to a defect claim and requires mediation first. The structural protection runs 10 years.
Get the warranty in writing and know exactly who to call, the builder directly or a third-party warranty company.
Take it seriously. The average structural warranty claim tops $40,000. This is real protection, not paperwork.
07Built for the desert?Vegas-specific build items
No basements here. The caliche soil layer makes them impractical, so plan storage and square footage accordingly.
Plan for the heat. Attics can hit 140 to 155F in summer and your AC runs 10 to 11 months a year. Ask about insulation, ductwork, and the AC efficiency rating.
The water is very hard (around 17 grains per gallon). Budget for a water softener loop or system.
Grass is banned in new yards (since April 2022). Plan for desert landscaping. The water authority offers turf rebates.
Check the 2026 rules. An EV-ready garage circuit is now required, the federal solar tax credit ended January 1, so price any solar carefully, and updated building codes took effect January 11.

08Help paying for itprograms to ask about
Home Is Possible. Down payment assistance that is forgiven after three years, with a dedicated version for teachers.
Worker Advantage. Assistance for essential workers, a newer program. A strong fit if you are a teacher, nurse, or first responder.
VA loan. Zero down and no PMI if you are eligible.
Ask what stacks. Home At Last, Rural Rocks, and the Clark County Welcome Home program may layer on top of builder incentives.
