RTC Proposes 50 Percent Bus Fare Increases and Up to 40 Percent Service Cuts to Fill $118.5 Million Shortfall

The Regional Transportation Commission of Southern Nevada announced on May 4, 2026 that it may raise standard bus fares by 50 percent and cut nearly 40 percent of its service unless the agency can close a $118.5 million annual budget gap. This is one of the largest transit funding crises Clark County has ever faced, and it could affect tens of thousands of residents who depend on the bus system every single day.

What Happened

On May 4, 2026, the RTC of Southern Nevada went public with a stark warning: the agency is facing a $118.5 million annual budget shortfall, and it has proposed two ways to deal with it. The first option is to raise fares. The second is to cut service. In reality, the final plan will likely include a mix of both.

Here is what the proposed changes look like in plain terms. For standard local bus service, a single ride would jump from $2.00 to $3.00. That is a 50 percent increase. For rides along the Las Vegas Strip, the single-ride fare would go from $4.00 to $5.00. Monthly pass prices and reduced-fare options for seniors and people with disabilities have not yet been fully detailed, but those categories would also see increases.

On the service side, the RTC warned that nearly 40 percent of current routes and schedules could be eliminated. That would mean fewer buses, longer waits, and in some cases, no service at all on certain routes. Routes that run during off-peak hours or in lower-density neighborhoods are the most likely to be cut first.

The agency opened a public comment period that runs through June 23, 2026. Riders, community members, and advocates can submit comments at rtcsnv.com/transitupdates. The RTC board will review all feedback before making a final decision.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

For many people in Clark County, the RTC bus system is not a convenience. It is a lifeline. Tens of thousands of residents use the bus every day to get to work, reach medical appointments, attend school, and run basic errands. Many of them do not own a car or cannot afford one. For this population, a 50 percent fare hike combined with major route cuts is not just an inconvenience. It is a serious threat to their daily stability.

Think about a healthcare worker who takes two buses to reach a hospital on the north side of town. Or a student at a community college who relies on an afternoon route to get home safely. Or a senior citizen in Henderson who takes the bus every two weeks to a pharmacy because they cannot drive. These are the people who will feel this the most, and they are often the people with the least ability to absorb added costs or adapt to service gaps.

Beyond the direct impact on riders, there is a broader economic effect to consider. Public transit reduces traffic congestion on major corridors. It supports businesses by bringing customers and workers to commercial areas. When bus service shrinks, more cars end up on the road, parking becomes harder, and businesses that depend on foot traffic can suffer. The hospitality and gaming industries along the Strip in particular depend on workers who commute by bus from across the valley.

For homeowners and property investors, transit access is also a real estate factor. Neighborhoods with reliable bus service tend to attract renters who do not own vehicles, including younger adults, seniors, and lower-income households. If routes near certain communities are cut, that could reduce the pool of renters, affect walkability scores, and shift housing demand in subtle but real ways over time.

Background and History

The root cause of this crisis is straightforward: the RTC has not raised its base fare in more than 15 years. During that same period, operating costs have climbed steadily. Fuel, labor, vehicle maintenance, and administrative expenses have all gone up. Ridership patterns have shifted since the pandemic. And federal pandemic relief funding that propped up transit agencies across the country has now run out.

At $2.00 for a standard local ride, the RTC's base fare is one of the lowest among large transit agencies in the Western United States. By comparison, the Los Angeles Metro charges $1.75 but recently completed a fare-free pilot program. The San Francisco Bay Area's BART charges between $2.20 and $6.85 depending on distance. Phoenix's Valley Metro charges $2.00 for a single local ride. On paper, the RTC's current price looks competitive. But the agency argues that the combination of flat fares and rising costs has created an unsustainable gap that can no longer be papered over.

The $118.5 million shortfall does not represent a one-time emergency. It is a structural, recurring gap that will grow each year if not addressed. Raising fares generates some additional revenue, but fare increases alone cannot close a gap this large. The math simply does not work. Fare revenue covers only a portion of what it costs to run a transit system. The bulk of funding comes from sales tax receipts, federal grants, and government appropriations, all of which have their own limits and uncertainties.

Clark County and the broader Las Vegas metro area have also struggled with transit funding compared to peer cities. Nevada has historically been more car-centric than states like California or New York, and political appetite for large public transit investments has been mixed. The RTC has pushed for expanded light rail and bus rapid transit over the years, but funding those projects while maintaining existing bus service has proven difficult.

What Happens Next

The public comment period is the first and most important step. The RTC has set a deadline of June 23, 2026 for residents to weigh in. Comments can be submitted online at rtcsnv.com/transitupdates. The agency is also expected to hold public meetings across the valley so that residents in different areas can speak directly with RTC staff and board members. Watch the RTC website for scheduled meeting dates and locations.

After the comment period closes, the RTC board will review the feedback and likely hold additional hearings before voting on a final plan. It is unlikely that either proposed option, a pure fare increase or a pure service cut, will be adopted in full. More realistically, the final outcome will blend modest fare increases with targeted service reductions on the lowest-ridership routes.

There is also the question of whether additional funding could come from state or federal sources. Nevada lawmakers could choose to direct more state revenue toward the RTC. The federal government periodically offers grants for transit capital and operating costs, though those processes are slow and competitive. Local governments in Clark County, including the City of Las Vegas, Henderson, North Las Vegas, and the county itself, could theoretically provide supplemental funding, but none have announced plans to do so as of this writing.

Advocacy groups and labor unions are already mobilizing. Transit worker unions have a direct stake because service cuts often lead to layoffs or reduced hours. Community organizations that serve low-income residents are gearing up to attend public meetings and submit formal comments. The Nevada legislature is also in session, which creates at least a narrow window for emergency transit funding to move through state government before the RTC is forced to act.

If no additional funding materializes, the RTC will have to act. The most likely scenario is a phased approach: smaller fare increases and selective route changes take effect first, with more significant cuts delayed or avoided if ridership and revenue respond positively. But the $118.5 million gap is large enough that some level of pain for riders seems unavoidable.

Ryan's Take

As someone who works in Las Vegas real estate every day, I pay close attention to anything that affects how people get around this city and where they choose to live. The RTC situation is one worth watching closely, even if you own a car and never ride the bus.

Here is why I say that. Public transit access is one of the factors that shapes neighborhood value over time. Areas near reliable bus routes tend to support a wider range of residents, including renters, seniors, and younger workers who have not yet bought their first home. When that transit infrastructure weakens, it can shift demand in ways that are hard to predict but real in their effect.

I am also thinking about the workforce that makes Las Vegas run. The hospitality industry, healthcare, retail, and construction all depend heavily on workers who commute by bus. A 40 percent reduction in service would create real strain for employers trying to staff their operations, especially for early morning and late night shifts that rely on transit workers who cannot afford to drive to work every day.

That said, I want to be clear that this is still a proposal. Nothing is final. The public comment period exists for a reason, and the RTC board will be listening. If you want to have a voice in how this plays out, the next six weeks are your window. Go to rtcsnv.com/transitupdates, read the full proposal, and submit your feedback. That is the most direct way to influence the outcome.

What You Can Do

If this issue affects you or someone you know, here are the concrete steps you can take right now.

Submit a public comment. Go to rtcsnv.com/transitupdates before June 23, 2026. You can submit comments online. Be specific about which routes you or your family use and what a fare increase or service cut would mean for your daily life. Personal stories carry weight in public hearings.

Attend a public meeting. The RTC will hold community meetings across the valley before the comment period closes. Check rtcsnv.com for the schedule. Showing up in person is one of the most powerful ways to make your voice heard. Bring neighbors, coworkers, or family members who also rely on transit.

Contact your elected representatives. Clark County commissioners, Las Vegas city council members, Henderson city council members, and Nevada state legislators all have a role to play in whether additional transit funding comes through. A short email or phone call to your representative's office stating that you want them to support RTC funding goes on the record and matters.

Spread the word. Many residents do not follow local government news closely. Share this story with neighbors, community groups, and social networks. The more people who engage with the public comment process, the more the RTC board will understand the depth of community concern.

Think about how this affects your housing situation. If you rent in a neighborhood with heavy transit access, a service reduction could affect your commute options and the long-term desirability of your area. If you own rental property, consider how your tenant pool might shift if nearby routes are cut. These are conversations worth having with a real estate professional who knows the local market.
