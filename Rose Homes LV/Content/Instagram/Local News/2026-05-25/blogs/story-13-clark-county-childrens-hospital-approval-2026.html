Clark County Unanimously Approves Nevada's First Standalone Children's Hospital

Nevada is finally getting its own children's hospital. On May 20, 2026, the Clark County Zoning Commission voted unanimously to approve the permits needed for Intermountain Health to build a dedicated pediatric hospital in the southwest Las Vegas Valley. It will be the first facility of its kind in the entire state.

For decades, Nevada families with seriously ill children had two choices: rely on a hospital not built specifically for kids, or pack up and fly to another state. That changes with this vote. The approval clears the last major regulatory hurdle before construction can begin.

This is a big deal not just for healthcare, but for the Las Vegas housing market and for every family with young children considering a move here. A world-class children's hospital changes the conversation about what it means to raise a family in Southern Nevada.

What Happened

The Clark County Zoning Commission approved both a development permit and a use permit for the new hospital on May 20, 2026. The vote was unanimous. No commissioners voted against it, and there was no extended public opposition that slowed the process.

The project is led by Intermountain Health, a nonprofit health system headquartered in Salt Lake City, Utah. They operate dozens of hospitals and clinics across the western United States and are known for their work in pediatric care. Nevada has been on their radar for years, and this project represents their largest investment in the state to date.

The hospital will be located at UNLV's Harry Reid Research and Technology Park, near the corner of Durango Drive and Patrick Lane in the Spring Valley area of unincorporated Clark County. That puts it in the southwest Las Vegas Valley, close to established neighborhoods like Summerlin South, Enterprise, and Rhodes Ranch. It is also near the 215 Beltway, which makes it accessible from nearly every part of the valley within 20 to 30 minutes.

The numbers are impressive. The building will be eight stories tall and cover 830,350 square feet. It is designed to hold approximately 200 inpatient beds. The total cost of the project is expected to exceed one billion dollars, making it one of the largest single healthcare investments in Nevada history.

Construction is expected to begin sometime in 2026, following this approval. Intermountain Health has said the hospital is projected to open in 2030. When fully operational, it will employ between 2,000 and 3,000 caregivers, which includes doctors, nurses, therapists, technicians, and support staff.

The facility will sit on land that is part of UNLV's research and technology campus. That location is significant. The partnership between a major research university and a world-class health system creates opportunities for medical research, physician training, and innovation that would not exist if the hospital were built on a standalone commercial site. Faculty and students from UNLV's Kirk Kerkorian School of Medicine are expected to work alongside Intermountain clinical staff.

The Spring Valley area where this hospital will be built is one of the most densely populated parts of unincorporated Clark County. Tens of thousands of families live within a short drive. Access from the 215 also means families in Henderson, North Las Vegas, and even Boulder City can reach it reasonably quickly.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

The lack of a dedicated children's hospital has been one of the quiet but serious quality-of-life problems in the Las Vegas Valley for a long time. Nevada consistently ranks near the bottom in healthcare access and outcomes, and pediatric care has been a particular weak spot.

Right now, children with complex medical needs are often treated at adult hospitals with dedicated pediatric floors or units. That is not the same as a hospital built from the ground up for children. Pediatric-specific hospitals are designed differently. The equipment is sized for children. The staff are trained specifically in child medicine. The environments are designed to reduce stress and fear. The outcomes data on dedicated pediatric facilities versus general hospitals with pediatric units consistently shows better results for kids in standalone facilities.

For parents in Las Vegas, that has meant a difficult choice when something serious happens. Do you accept care that is good but not specialized? Or do you arrange a transfer to a children's hospital in Phoenix, Los Angeles, or Salt Lake City? Those transfers are stressful, expensive, and sometimes dangerous. Families have had to take emergency flights out of state with critically ill children. That will no longer be the only option.

The jobs impact is also meaningful. Between 2,000 and 3,000 caregivers is a significant number. Many of those will be high-income professionals, including physicians and specialists who currently choose not to live in Nevada because the medical infrastructure is not here. When the hospital opens, it will attract talent that the valley has historically struggled to keep.

For families thinking about buying a home in Las Vegas, this is a real factor. Healthcare access is consistently in the top five concerns for homebuyers with children. The spring 2026 real estate market in Las Vegas has shown some softening in demand, but the announcement of a dedicated children's hospital is exactly the kind of long-term infrastructure signal that supports home values in the surrounding neighborhoods. Areas near Durango and Patrick Lane, and the broader southwest valley corridor, stand to benefit most directly.

Parents who have been on the fence about moving to Las Vegas often cite schools and healthcare as their top hesitations. This hospital addresses one of those two in a major way. It does not solve everything, but it removes a legitimate concern that has cost the valley residents and economic activity.

Background and History

Nevada's lack of a standalone children's hospital is not a new issue. Healthcare advocates and state leaders have discussed the problem for at least two decades. The state has one of the fastest-growing populations in the country, and Clark County adds tens of thousands of new residents every year. Yet the healthcare infrastructure has never kept pace.

Nevada ranks poorly on almost every healthcare access metric compared to other states. The ratio of pediatric specialists to children is well below the national average. Pediatric intensive care capacity is limited. Families in rural Nevada counties face even more severe shortages, and they often have no choice but to drive hours to reach even basic pediatric care in Clark County.

Intermountain Health entered Nevada's healthcare market carefully. They operate Primary Children's Hospital in Salt Lake City, which is one of the top-ranked children's hospitals in the United States. Their entry into Nevada signals confidence in the long-term growth of the market and a genuine commitment to filling the gap.

The Harry Reid Research and Technology Park is a deliberate location choice. UNLV opened the Kerkorian School of Medicine in 2017 and has been building out its research capacity since. The park is designed to attract healthcare, technology, and biotech companies. Placing a billion-dollar children's hospital there anchors the entire development and creates a medical district identity for the southwest valley.

The unanimous approval from the Clark County Zoning Commission reflects how much political and community support this project has. Normally, a billion-dollar hospital near residential neighborhoods would generate at least some opposition around traffic, noise, or scale. That did not happen here. The community need is recognized broadly, and local leaders have been eager to see the project move forward.

The project also benefits from Intermountain's nonprofit status. Unlike for-profit hospital chains, Intermountain reinvests surplus back into operations, services, and community programs. Their track record in Utah and other states suggests they will bring resources and programs that go beyond basic clinical care, including mental health services, community outreach, and preventive care programs for children.

What Happens Next

The zoning approvals granted on May 20 are the most critical regulatory milestone. With those in hand, Intermountain Health can now move into the full construction planning and permitting phase. The next steps include finalizing construction permits with Clark County Building Department, completing site preparation at the Harry Reid Research and Technology Park, and selecting and contracting with construction firms capable of handling a project of this scale and complexity.

Construction is expected to begin in 2026. Given the size of the project, an eight-story, 830,000-plus square foot structure, the build timeline is expected to take approximately four years. That lines up with the projected 2030 opening.

Between now and opening day, Intermountain will begin recruiting medical staff. Attracting pediatric specialists, subspecialists, and surgeons to Nevada requires significant lead time. Many of the physicians who will staff this hospital will need to be recruited from out of state, and that process typically begins years before a facility opens.

The UNLV partnership will also develop further during the construction period. Academic affiliations, residency programs, and research agreements will be negotiated. The presence of the Kirk Kerkorian School of Medicine means there is a natural pipeline for training physicians who may ultimately choose to stay in Nevada and practice at the new hospital.

Community members who want to follow the project can monitor updates through Clark County's official channels and through Intermountain Health's Nevada-focused communications. There will likely be a formal groundbreaking ceremony once construction begins, which will be another opportunity for the community to engage with the project and understand its scope.

Between now and 2030, existing pediatric care in Nevada continues as it has. Families should still maintain relationships with their current pediatric providers. The new hospital does not replace any existing care options, it adds to them.

Ryan's Take

As a Las Vegas real estate professional, I pay close attention to what drives families to move here, and what sometimes holds them back. Healthcare access for kids has been a real sticking point for years. I have had conversations with buyers who love everything about the Las Vegas Valley, the neighborhoods, the schools improving, the cost of living relative to California, and they still hesitate because of what would happen if their child got seriously ill.

This hospital changes that conversation. Not immediately, because it will not open until 2030. But the approval is real, the funding is committed, and Intermountain Health is a serious organization with a track record of delivering. Four years from now, Las Vegas will have something it has never had: a world-class pediatric facility built specifically for children.

For anyone buying a home near the southwest valley corridor today, in Spring Valley, Summerlin South, Enterprise, or along the 215, this is worth understanding. That area is already strong. Proximity to this medical campus adds another long-term value driver. Healthcare infrastructure of this scale does not move, and the neighborhoods around it tend to grow stronger over time.

The 2,000 to 3,000 jobs this hospital will create are also worth noting. High-earning medical professionals are excellent neighbors. They buy homes, support local businesses, and contribute to stable, desirable communities. This project is a genuine win for the valley, and I am glad to see it finally moving forward.

What You Can Do

If you have children and are currently comparing hospitals as part of a relocation decision, note that the existing pediatric resources at hospitals like Summerlin Hospital Medical Center and Valley Hospital continue to serve the community. The Intermountain children's hospital will be an addition to those options, not a replacement. Ask your current or prospective pediatrician what changes they expect when the new hospital opens.

If you are a homebuyer looking at the southwest Las Vegas Valley, factor this project into your long-term thinking. The area near Durango and the 215 was already growing before this approval. A billion-dollar medical campus and 2,000-plus high-earning jobs will accelerate that growth.

If you work in healthcare or know someone who does, Intermountain Health Nevada is already a known brand and will be actively recruiting over the next several years. Pediatric specialists, nurses, and healthcare administrators who want to be part of building something from the ground up in a high-growth market should watch Intermountain's Nevada job postings.

Stay informed by following Clark County's official news channels and the Intermountain Health Nevada website. When the groundbreaking is announced, attending is a worthwhile way to see firsthand what is coming to the valley.
