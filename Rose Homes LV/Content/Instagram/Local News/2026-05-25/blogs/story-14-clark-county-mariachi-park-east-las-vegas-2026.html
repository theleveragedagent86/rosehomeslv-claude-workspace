Clark County Renames Cesar Chavez Park to Mariachi Park in East Las Vegas

Clark County officially renamed Cesar Chavez Park to Mariachi Park on May 19, 2026, following a unanimous vote by the Board of Commissioners. The park at 1580 Radwick Drive in East Las Vegas now carries a name meant to celebrate the mariachi music tradition that has deep roots in the community and in Clark County's school system.

The decision came after allegations of sexual abuse surfaced against labor leader Cesar Chavez. Commissioner Tick Segerblom put forward the new name, pointing to the roughly 8,000 Clark County School District students who participate in mariachi programs each year. A public naming ceremony on May 23, 2026 marked the occasion with live mariachi performances, drawing neighbors and families to the park for what became a community celebration. This is more than a name change. It is a statement about who this community is and what it values.

What Happened

On May 19, 2026, the Clark County Board of Commissioners voted unanimously to rename the park located at 1580 Radwick Drive in East Las Vegas. The park had carried the name of Cesar Chavez, the well-known labor organizer and co-founder of the United Farm Workers union, for years. His name appears on streets, schools, and public spaces across the country. But that legacy has become complicated.

In recent years, accounts from women who worked closely with Chavez came forward describing a pattern of sexual abuse. These allegations, reported and examined by journalists and historians, prompted communities across the United States to reconsider whether Chavez's name should remain on public spaces. Clark County did not move quickly on this. The board took time to consider what the right course of action would be, and ultimately decided that renaming the park was appropriate.

Commissioner Tick Segerblom brought the new name to the table. He wanted to replace one cultural symbol with another, something that resonated specifically with the East Las Vegas community. Mariachi music was the natural choice. It is a living, breathing part of life in this part of the valley. It is played at quinceañeras, weddings, funerals, and community gatherings. It passes from parents to children. And in Clark County schools, it has become one of the most vibrant music programs in the entire state.

Segerblom noted that approximately 8,000 CCSD students participate in mariachi programs. That number puts Clark County among the largest mariachi education hubs in the country. Naming a park after the music tradition honors not just history but present-day reality. It puts a spotlight on what thousands of young people in East Las Vegas are learning and doing right now.

The county wasted no time after the vote. A naming ceremony took place just four days later, on May 23, 2026. Mariachi groups performed at the park. Families came out. Local leaders spoke. The event felt more like a celebration than a bureaucratic formality, and that was by design. The goal was to give the community something to gather around from day one under the new name.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

East Las Vegas is one of the most culturally rich parts of the entire valley. It is home to a large Hispanic and Latino population, many of whom have deep roots in Nevada and in Mexican and Central American traditions. Mariachi music is not background noise here. It is part of everyday life in ways that people outside the community do not always fully appreciate.

When a public space takes on a name that reflects a living tradition rather than a historical figure, it does something powerful. It tells the people who live nearby that their culture is worth honoring right now, not just in museums or retrospectives. A child who walks past Mariachi Park on the way to school gets a message that what they are learning in band class matters enough to have a park named after it.

That is not a small thing. East Las Vegas has historically been an underserved part of the valley when it comes to public investment and recognition. Parts of the community lack the same level of resources and attention that more affluent neighborhoods receive. A decision like this, while symbolic, carries real weight for residents who want to see their neighborhood treated with the same dignity as other parts of Clark County.

The mariachi programs in CCSD are also a point of genuine pride. Students compete regionally and nationally. Teachers who run these programs often describe them as life-changing for kids who might otherwise struggle to find a place in traditional school settings. Mariachi gives students discipline, teamwork, performance experience, and a direct connection to their cultural identity. Having a park named after that tradition reinforces the message that these programs deserve support and celebration.

There is also the matter of the Chavez allegations and how the community responded. Some residents had mixed feelings about removing his name. Chavez's work organizing farm workers in the 1960s and 1970s had direct benefits for many Hispanic families, including those in Nevada. His legacy is genuinely complicated, and the decision to rename the park did not come without thoughtful debate. But the board's unanimous vote suggests that the commissioners found consensus that honoring the music tradition was a forward-looking way to handle a difficult situation.

For longtime East Las Vegas families, the ceremony on May 23 offered a chance to come together around something joyful. Mariachi music at a park on a Saturday afternoon is exactly the kind of community moment that neighborhoods remember. It set a tone for what the park is meant to represent going forward.

Background and History

Mariachi music has roots in the western Mexican state of Jalisco, though its traditions spread across Mexico and into the United States over generations. The style, which typically features violins, trumpets, guitars, vihuelas, and guitarrons played by musicians in charro suits, became internationally recognized in the 20th century. It was declared an Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity by UNESCO in 2011.

In the United States, mariachi education programs grew significantly in the latter half of the 20th century, particularly in areas with large Mexican-American communities. San Antonio, Texas is often cited as one of the early leaders in school-based mariachi programs. Las Vegas followed a similar path as its Hispanic population grew.

Clark County School District has developed one of the most robust mariachi programs in the western United States. The programs run at multiple schools throughout the valley, with particularly strong participation in East Las Vegas, North Las Vegas, and other areas with dense Latino communities. Students begin learning as early as middle school in some programs, and many continue through high school. Regional competitions draw schools from across Nevada and neighboring states.

The park at 1580 Radwick Drive has served as a neighborhood green space for many years. It sits in the heart of a residential area in unincorporated Clark County, the portion of the Las Vegas valley governed directly by the county rather than by the cities of Las Vegas, Henderson, or North Las Vegas. East Las Vegas falls largely within unincorporated county territory, which means decisions about local parks and infrastructure go through the county commission rather than a city council.

The Cesar Chavez name had been attached to the park for years. Chavez parks and streets are common in Western cities with large Latino populations, reflecting the significant role the UFW played in advocating for better wages and conditions for agricultural workers, many of whom were Mexican immigrants or Mexican-Americans. The surfacing of abuse allegations against Chavez has led to a broader national conversation about how communities handle the legacies of complicated historical figures.

What Happens Next

The name Mariachi Park is now official. Clark County will update signage at the park and in county records. The change is permanent barring any future commission vote to alter it again, which is not expected.

Commissioner Segerblom and other supporters of the renaming have indicated interest in doing more to celebrate and support mariachi culture in East Las Vegas. Whether that takes the form of permanent performance infrastructure at the park, regular community events, or closer partnerships with CCSD's mariachi programs remains to be seen. No specific plans have been announced beyond the naming ceremony itself as of the time of this writing [NOT VERIFIED for any post-May 23 announcements].

The Clark County Parks and Recreation Department will likely look at whether additional amenities or programming at the park can reflect the new name. A stage, covered performance area, or acoustic improvements would make the park a natural gathering spot for mariachi events year-round. East Las Vegas summers are hot, so any outdoor performance infrastructure would need to account for shade and heat management.

For CCSD mariachi students, the park may become a meaningful destination. Schools could use it for outdoor rehearsals, community performances, or end-of-year celebrations. That kind of community-school connection is exactly what strong neighborhood parks are designed to support.

Neighboring residents can also expect the park to gain visibility. Local media coverage of the ceremony brought attention to the Radwick Drive location that it may not have had before. New visitors who come to see the park may discover the surrounding neighborhood and the small businesses, restaurants, and community organizations that make East Las Vegas what it is.

In the broader context of Clark County development, the renaming signals that the commission is paying attention to community identity in East Las Vegas. That matters for residents who want to see the area continue to grow and receive investment. A neighborhood whose culture is publicly honored tends to attract more interest from businesses, organizations, and even homebuyers who are drawn to authentic, rooted communities.
