Nevada Grass Ban Lawsuit Now Covers 10,000 Homes After Supreme Court Ruling

A class action lawsuit fighting Nevada's nonfunctional grass ban has grown to more than 10,000 homes after the Nevada Supreme Court refused to shut it down in May 2026. The court's decision clears the way for even more HOAs, homeowners, and other property owners to join the fight against the Southern Nevada Water Authority's enforcement of the 2021 turf removal law.

This is one of the biggest legal challenges to a state water conservation law in Nevada's history. The case directly affects hundreds of thousands of Southern Nevada residents who face a December 31, 2026 deadline to remove decorative grass from their properties. If you own a home in a homeowners association, or if your yard has any ornamental turf, this lawsuit could affect your legal obligations and your landscaping costs.

The stakes are enormous. Together, the plaintiffs now represent more than 25 million square feet of grass across the Las Vegas Valley. That's roughly the equivalent of 430 football fields worth of turf that the state says must go. Homeowners and HOAs say the law is unconstitutional and unfair. The water authority says the desert cannot afford to keep watering it.

What Happened

The Nevada Supreme Court issued its ruling in May 2026, denying the Southern Nevada Water Authority's motion to dismiss the class action lawsuit. The authority had argued that certain plaintiffs did not have legal standing to sue and that the case should be thrown out before ever going to trial. The court disagreed.

The court's decision is significant for two reasons. First, it keeps the lawsuit alive and on track for a full hearing. Second, it opens the door for additional parties to join the case as co-plaintiffs. In the weeks following the ruling, HOAs, individual homeowners, and at least one Catholic church signed on to the lawsuit, pushing the total number of represented homes past 10,000.

A key hearing in the case was scheduled for May 21, 2026. That hearing was expected to address how enforcement of Assembly Bill 356 should proceed while the lawsuit is pending. AB 356 is the 2021 law at the center of the dispute. It requires the removal of all nonfunctional, or decorative, grass in Southern Nevada by December 31, 2026. The law applies to commercial properties, government buildings, homeowners associations, and the common areas of multifamily developments.

The plaintiffs argue the law is unconstitutional for several reasons. They say it takes private property without fair compensation. They also say the law was applied unevenly, targeting HOA common areas and decorative turf while exempting golf courses and other large-scale water users. The Southern Nevada Water Authority disputes those arguments. The authority says the law is a necessary response to Nevada's long-term water shortage, especially given the declining water levels in Lake Mead.

The case has attracted national attention because it pits individual property rights against state-mandated environmental conservation. Legal experts say the outcome could set a precedent for other water-scarce western states that are considering similar laws. Arizona, Colorado, and California have all discussed turf removal requirements, and they are watching Nevada closely.

The 25 million square feet of grass represented by the plaintiffs is not a small number. For context, the Southern Nevada Water Authority estimated in 2021 that the region had approximately 40 million square feet of nonfunctional grass when the law passed. That means the plaintiffs in this lawsuit represent a very large share of the total turf the law was designed to eliminate.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

If you live in an HOA community in Las Vegas, Henderson, Summerlin, Green Valley, or anywhere else in the Southern Nevada Water Authority's service area, this lawsuit affects you directly. Most HOA common areas include grass, and those areas are specifically targeted by AB 356. If the law stands, your HOA will need to remove that grass by December 31, 2026. That work is expensive, and the cost typically gets passed to homeowners through special assessments or higher monthly dues.

Landscaping contractors in the Las Vegas Valley have reported that demand for turf removal and xeriscape installation has surged dramatically in 2025 and 2026. Many are booked months out. The Southern Nevada Water Authority offers rebates of up to $3 per square foot for grass removal, but homeowners and HOAs say that amount often does not cover the full cost of replacing the turf with desert-friendly landscaping. Estimates for full xeriscape conversions range widely, but large HOA common areas can cost tens of thousands of dollars or more to redo properly.

This is where the lawsuit becomes very practical for ordinary homeowners. If the plaintiffs win, enforcement of the December 2026 deadline could be delayed, paused, or restructured. HOAs that have been rushing to get bids and complete removal projects might be able to slow down and plan more carefully. On the other hand, if the law is upheld and the deadline stays in place, homeowners who have not started planning yet are running out of time.

The Catholic church that joined the lawsuit represents another angle worth watching. Religious institutions, schools, and other nonprofit organizations were not initially at the center of the debate, but they own significant amounts of property in the Las Vegas Valley. Many have lawns and grass areas used for community gatherings, religious ceremonies, and recreational activities. Their inclusion in the lawsuit signals that the challenge to AB 356 is broader than just private homeowners and HOAs.

Real estate agents, buyers, and sellers also need to pay attention. If a home or HOA has nonfunctional grass that has not yet been removed, that could become a disclosure issue in a sale transaction. Buyers may factor the cost of required removal into their offers. Sellers may need to document what steps their HOA has taken to comply. This is a live legal question right now, and anyone involved in a real estate transaction in the Las Vegas Valley should be aware of it.

The broader question for residents is what the Las Vegas Valley will look like if the law does take full effect. Water conservation advocates say removing nonfunctional grass could save billions of gallons of water annually, water that the region desperately needs as the Colorado River basin faces its worst drought in recorded history. Critics say the visual change will be jarring, and many homeowners feel attached to the green lawns that give their neighborhoods a traditional residential feel.

Background and History

Nevada passed Assembly Bill 356 in 2021 during a special session focused on water conservation. Governor Steve Sisolak signed the bill into law, making Nevada the first state in the country to ban nonfunctional grass on a statewide basis. The law defined nonfunctional grass as turf that is not regularly used for recreation or does not serve an essential function. Grass in HOA common areas along streets, medians, and around signs was the primary target.

The law did not happen in a vacuum. It came as Lake Mead, the reservoir that supplies the majority of Southern Nevada's drinking water, dropped to historically low levels. In 2021, the Bureau of Reclamation declared the first-ever Tier 1 water shortage on the Colorado River. That triggered mandatory cuts to Nevada's water allocation. Nevada, which has worked for decades to reduce per-capita water use, needed to find more savings.

The Southern Nevada Water Authority had already been paying homeowners to remove grass voluntarily for years through its turf removal rebate program. That program helped reduce grass coverage significantly, but officials said voluntary programs were not moving fast enough. The 2021 law was designed to eliminate the remaining decorative turf that served no functional purpose other than aesthetics.

When the law passed, reactions were mixed. Environmental groups and water conservation advocates applauded it as a necessary and overdue step. Many homeowners and HOA boards worried about costs and community character. Some HOA governing documents actually required green lawns as part of their architectural standards, creating a conflict between state law and private contractual obligations.

The lawsuit was first filed in 2023 by a group of HOAs and homeowners who argued the law violated their property rights and due process. The case worked its way through the courts slowly. The Southern Nevada Water Authority fought to have it dismissed at multiple stages. The Nevada Supreme Court's May 2026 ruling is the latest chapter in a legal battle that shows no signs of ending quickly.

What Happens Next

The May 21, 2026 hearing was a critical moment in the case. The outcome of that hearing will shape how enforcement proceeds for the remainder of the year. If the court granted any kind of stay or temporary relief, it could pause enforcement while the case continues to be litigated. If the court declined to intervene, the December 31, 2026 deadline remains fully in effect.

With more than 10,000 homes now represented in the class action, the case has the weight and resources needed to go the distance. Class action lawsuits of this size are expensive to fight, and the plaintiffs' legal team will have more ability to pursue expert witnesses, depositions, and appeals as needed.

The Southern Nevada Water Authority is unlikely to back down. The authority has been one of the most aggressive water management agencies in the country for decades. Its leadership views the grass ban as essential to the long-term sustainability of the region. Conceding the legal fight would undermine years of conservation policy and could create pressure to roll back other water-saving measures.

Watch for several developments in the coming months. First, the court's ruling from the May 21 hearing will provide the clearest signal yet about whether the December 2026 deadline is truly firm. Second, additional plaintiffs may continue to join the class, which could further increase pressure on the water authority. Third, Nevada's state legislature, which meets regularly in Carson City, may weigh in if the legal situation creates enough political pressure. Several legislators have expressed sympathy for homeowners caught in the middle of this dispute.

For homeowners and HOAs, the safest posture right now is to keep planning for compliance while monitoring the legal situation closely. Do not assume the lawsuit will result in a delay. But also be aware that the legal landscape could shift before the year ends.

Ryan's Take

As a real estate agent who works across the Las Vegas Valley every day, I have been watching this situation closely since 2021. The grass ban affects nearly every HOA community in Southern Nevada, and that means it touches nearly every home sale I handle.

Here's what I tell buyers right now: ask about the HOA's compliance status. Has the board gotten bids for turf removal? Have they assessed members to cover costs? Is the grass in the common areas already removed, or is it still there? These are legitimate questions that affect your ownership experience and potentially your wallet after closing.

For sellers, I recommend getting ahead of this conversation before you list. If your HOA is behind on compliance planning, it may show up as a flag for informed buyers. If your HOA has already completed the transition or has a clear plan, that's actually a selling point. Show buyers that the community is well-managed and ahead of the deadline.

The legal outcome is genuinely uncertain. The Nevada Supreme Court's decision to let the lawsuit proceed is significant, but it does not mean the plaintiffs will win. Water law in the American West is complex, and courts have generally given states and water authorities broad power to regulate water use. The constitutional property rights arguments are interesting, but they are an uphill climb against that history.

What I do know is that water scarcity in Southern Nevada is real. Lake Mead's levels affect our water supply in a very direct way. Whatever happens with this lawsuit, the long-term trend toward desert-adapted landscaping is not going away. Homes with drought-tolerant yards are increasingly appealing to buyers who want lower utility bills and less maintenance. That's a trend worth leaning into regardless of how the courts rule.

What You Can Do

If you are an HOA homeowner, the most important first step is to contact your board and find out where your community stands. Ask directly: has the board scheduled or completed the removal of nonfunctional grass? What is the estimated cost, and how will it be funded? Is a special assessment coming? These questions will help you plan financially and stay ahead of any surprises.

If you own your own home without an HOA, check whether any grass on your property qualifies as nonfunctional under AB 356. The Southern Nevada Water Authority has a detailed guide on what qualifies and what is exempt. Functional grass used for play areas, sports fields, or other active recreation is generally exempt. Decorative grass around mailboxes, along streets, or in unused areas is typically not.

If you want to take advantage of the rebate program, contact the Southern Nevada Water Authority directly. The agency offers up to $3 per square foot for qualifying turf removal. That rebate can offset a meaningful portion of the cost if you move quickly. Contractors are very busy right now, so the sooner you get bids, the better position you will be in to meet the deadline.

Follow the lawsuit closely. The Las Vegas Review-Journal and other local outlets are covering every major development. The May 21 hearing result will likely be reported quickly, and that result will tell you a lot about where things stand for the rest of the year.
