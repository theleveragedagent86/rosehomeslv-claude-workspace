Las Vegas Athletics Ballpark Hits $400 Million Spent and Stays on Track for 2028 Opening

The new Las Vegas Athletics ballpark has crossed the $400 million spending mark and is on time and on budget as of May 2026. Construction crews are moving fast, with concrete work done on the first two levels, steel rising on the upper seating decks, and the first roof trusses being assembled on the ground ahead of a June installation.

The $2 billion stadium sits at Las Vegas Boulevard and Tropicana Avenue, right on the Strip. It is set to open February 29, 2028, making Las Vegas the permanent home of Major League Baseball for the first time in the city's history. For residents, homeowners, and anyone watching Las Vegas grow, this is one of the biggest construction stories unfolding right now.

What Is Happening at the Construction Site

As of May 23, 2026, the Athletics ballpark project has spent approximately $400 million of its $2 billion budget. That works out to about 20 cents of every dollar, and crews have a lot of work left to do before the first pitch in 2028. But the pace so far has project managers calling it on schedule.

Here is what has been completed or is currently underway according to Ballpark Digest:

Concrete work on the first and second seating levels is finished. Those lower levels form the base of the bowl, where fans closest to the field will sit. Getting that concrete done early is critical because everything else, the upper seating, the concourses, the suites, builds on top of it.

Steel work is now advancing on the third and fourth seating levels. Steel framing gives the stadium its shape. Watching those upper-deck steel sections go up is when a ballpark starts to look like a ballpark, and crews are in the middle of that phase right now.

Four of the five 200-foot shoring towers are installed. These towers are temporary support structures that hold the massive roof in place while it is being assembled and until it can stand on its own. A retractable roof is one of the project's biggest engineering challenges, and the shoring towers are a key part of making it work safely.

The first roof trusses are being assembled on the ground at the site. Those trusses are the giant steel arches that span the roof and carry its weight. Assembling them at ground level before lifting them into position is standard practice for large-span structures. The schedule calls for the first roof trusses to be lifted and installed in June 2026.

The roof itself is one of the most talked-about features of the stadium. Las Vegas summers regularly hit 110 degrees or hotter, so a retractable roof is not a luxury. It is a necessity for player safety, fan comfort, and the ability to hold games year-round. The Athletics have said the roof will be able to open and close and that the stadium will be fully air-conditioned.

The stadium is designed to hold around 33,000 fans, making it a mid-sized modern ballpark. That is intentional. Smaller, newer ballparks tend to sell out more often and create better atmospheres than the oversized concrete bowls of the 1970s and 1980s. The design also includes premium club spaces, open concourses with views of the field, and direct connection to the Strip's pedestrian environment.

Why This Matters to Las Vegas Residents

It is easy to think of a sports stadium as something that only matters to fans. But what is being built at Las Vegas Boulevard and Tropicana Avenue will affect nearly everyone who lives in the valley, whether they ever buy a ticket or not.

The most direct impact is economic. Major League Baseball brings 81 regular-season home games per year, plus any playoff games the Athletics qualify for. Each game draws thousands of visitors who spend money on hotels, restaurants, bars, rideshares, and souvenirs. The Las Vegas Convention and Visitors Authority has estimated that a successful professional baseball franchise adds hundreds of millions of dollars annually to the local economy. That money circulates through neighborhoods well beyond the Strip.

The second major impact is on real estate. Sports venues change the character of the land around them. Neighborhoods near NFL and MLB stadiums in other cities have seen mixed results, but when a venue is located in a dense, walkable area with good transit connections, which this one is, the surrounding land tends to increase in value over time. New development follows. Hotels, restaurants, residential towers, and retail all tend to cluster near major sports venues.

The area around this ballpark is already seeing that effect before the stadium opens. The Tropicana site itself is being redeveloped into a larger entertainment district. Brightline West is building a high-speed rail station connecting Las Vegas to Southern California a short distance away. The MSG Sphere opened just north on the Strip in 2023. The area is turning into one of the most active development corridors in the country.

For homeowners in surrounding zip codes, including zip codes that cover the Las Vegas Strip, Summerlin, Henderson, and the southwest valley, the ripple effects of that concentration of development tend to support property values over the medium and long term. The city becomes more attractive to corporate relocations, to tourism, and to in-migration from other states. All of those things support demand for housing.

There is also the identity question. Las Vegas already has the Golden Knights in the NHL and the Raiders in the NFL. Adding MLB completes a rare trifecta that only a handful of cities in the country can claim. That status matters for how the city is perceived nationally and for the caliber of professionals and businesses considering a move here.

Construction jobs also matter. A $2 billion project of this scale employs thousands of tradespeople, engineers, and support workers throughout its multi-year build. Many of those workers live in Clark County and spend their paychecks here.

Background and History of the Athletics Move to Las Vegas

The Oakland Athletics played in Oakland, California for more than five decades. For much of that time, they shared the Coliseum with the NFL's Raiders, a venue that was widely considered one of the worst in professional sports. By the late 2010s, the team was actively searching for a new home.

The Athletics explored options in several cities before landing on Las Vegas. The key moment came when Nevada lawmakers passed legislation in 2023 approving up to $380 million in public funding toward the stadium construction, using tax increment financing tied to the development around the ballpark site. That public contribution was the financial bridge that made the project viable.

The team played its final season in Oakland in 2024 under considerable controversy. A significant portion of Oakland's longtime fan base was opposed to the move and made their feelings known throughout that season. The Athletics played temporarily in Sacramento during 2025 while the Las Vegas site was being prepared.

Ground was broken on the Las Vegas ballpark in 2025. The site at Las Vegas Boulevard and Tropicana Avenue was chosen for multiple reasons: its visibility on the Strip, its proximity to existing hotel rooms and entertainment infrastructure, and its potential to anchor a broader sports and entertainment district on the south end of the Strip.

The stadium is being developed through a partnership between the Athletics organization and the development firm Bjarke Ingels Group provided the architectural design. The retractable roof and the open-air connection to the Strip are signature design elements intended to make the building feel like a Las Vegas venue, not just a transplant from another city.

The February 2028 target opening date was set to allow the team to play its first full home season in Las Vegas starting with the 2028 MLB season. February 29 is a leap day, and the organization has indicated it plans to use that date for a ceremonial first event at the stadium.

What Happens Next

The next major construction milestone is the roof truss installation, which is scheduled for June 2026. Lifting those large steel arches into place is a complex operation that requires careful coordination and the right weather conditions. Once the trusses are in place, crews can begin assembling the retractable roof mechanism itself.

After the roof structure is in place, interior work will accelerate. That includes seating installation, concourse finishing, suite construction, clubhouses and locker rooms, and all of the mechanical systems: plumbing, electrical, HVAC, broadcast infrastructure, and the scoreboard and video board systems that modern fans expect.

Exterior work including facade panels, plaza paving, and the pedestrian connections to the surrounding streets will also ramp up in the second half of 2026 and into 2027.

The project team has maintained that the February 2028 opening date remains firm. With $400 million spent and major structural work either complete or in progress, that claim appears credible. Large construction projects of this type can still encounter delays from supply chain issues, weather, or labor challenges, but as of May 2026, no such problems have been reported publicly.

The team is also working in parallel on front-office operations, ticket sales infrastructure, and the marketing work needed to build a Las Vegas fan base from scratch. The first season ticket sales campaigns have already begun. Capacity events in 2027, including open-house tours of the completed structure, are expected before the 2028 opening.

Clark County and city of Las Vegas planners are also continuing work on the street improvements, transit connections, and public space enhancements planned for the broader stadium district. Those projects are intended to be complete, or close to it, when the ballpark opens.

Ryan's Take: What This Means for Las Vegas Real Estate

When I look at the Las Vegas real estate market over the next five to ten years, the concentration of major developments on the south Strip is one of the biggest factors I watch. The Athletics ballpark is not just a sports story. It is a signal about where investment is going, and investment tends to pull population and housing demand with it.

Las Vegas is already in a moment where inventory is rising and prices have softened from their 2022 peaks. That makes 2026 a good time for buyers to get into the market before the next wave of development activity lifts demand again. Projects like this ballpark, the Brightline station, the MSG Sphere, and ongoing Strip development create a longer-term demand floor under the local housing market that is hard to ignore.

Buyers looking at neighborhoods on the west side of the valley, in Summerlin and the southwest, are not necessarily buying close to the stadium. But they are buying into a city that is becoming more valuable to live in because of projects like this one. More amenities, more jobs, more visitors, and more national attention all translate into a stronger housing market over time.

If you are a homeowner in Las Vegas, this is good news for your long-term equity. If you are a buyer on the fence, the development story here is one of the strongest arguments for not waiting.
