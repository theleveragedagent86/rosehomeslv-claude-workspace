Las Vegas Small Businesses Are Struggling. A Senate Roundtable Just Put It on the Record.

Las Vegas small business owners told U.S. Senator Catherine Cortez Masto exactly what they are facing: fewer customers, higher costs, and no clear end in sight. The roundtable happened in May 2026, and it came as Las Vegas visitation dropped 7.5% in 2025 to roughly 38.5 million visitors, the lowest total since 2021.

That is not a small dip. That is nearly 3 million fewer people walking through the doors of restaurants, tattoo shops, bars, and storefronts across Clark County. For businesses that already run on thin margins, the difference between 41 million visitors and 38.5 million can be the difference between staying open and locking up for good.

The roundtable gave local owners a rare platform. Business owners do not often get a seat at a Senate table. This time, they did, and what they said painted a picture of an economy under real pressure from multiple directions at once.

What Happened at the Senate Roundtable

Senator Catherine Cortez Masto hosted the roundtable to hear directly from Nevada small business owners about the pressures hitting them in 2026. The event brought together owners from multiple industries across the Las Vegas area, and the stories they shared were specific and detailed.

Studio 21 Tattoo Gallery was one of the businesses represented. The owners told the Senator that Canadian and European guests had dropped to nearly zero. That is a striking statement. Las Vegas has long attracted international visitors, and those visitors tend to spend more per trip than domestic travelers. Losing that segment entirely is not just a revenue problem. It is a customer base that may not come back quickly even if broader conditions improve.

The reasons for the drop in international visitors are layered. Travel patterns changed sharply in 2025. A stronger U.S. dollar made American destinations more expensive for foreign visitors. Concerns about border policies and travel friction discouraged some international tourists from planning trips to the United States at all. Las Vegas, which depends heavily on discretionary travel spending, felt that shift more than most cities.

Beyond the visitor count, business owners described rising costs on everyday supplies that most people do not think about. Napkins cost more. Limes cost more. Avocados cost more. Each item seems small on its own, but for a restaurant or bar buying those items by the case every week, the increases add up fast. When ingredient and supply costs climb at the same time that foot traffic falls, the business math gets very difficult very quickly.

One thing that came through clearly at the roundtable is that these businesses are not asking for handouts. They are asking for policy attention, for someone to understand that decisions made in Washington have real effects on a tattoo shop or a restaurant in Las Vegas. Tariffs on imported goods, visa processing delays, exchange rate swings, all of those things filter down to the local level in ways that are easy to miss from a distance.

Senator Cortez Masto has been a vocal advocate for Nevada's tourism-dependent economy, and the roundtable was framed as a listening session. Whether it leads to specific action or legislation is a separate question, but at minimum, the concerns of Las Vegas small business owners are now on the official record.

Why This Matters to Las Vegas Residents

When visitors stop coming, or slow down, the effects spread far beyond the Strip. Las Vegas is built on a service economy. Hotels, restaurants, entertainment venues, retail shops, and hundreds of small businesses in neighborhoods all across Clark County depend on a steady flow of spending from people who are visiting or locals who are doing well because the visitor economy is doing well.

A 7.5% drop in visitation in a single year is significant. For context, 38.5 million visitors in 2025 is the lowest figure since 2021. The city was recovering from the pandemic in 2021. A return to those numbers in 2025 without a pandemic forcing closures is a different kind of problem entirely. It suggests that underlying demand has softened.

That has direct effects on employment. The service and hospitality sector is the largest employer in Clark County. When restaurants cut hours or reduce staff because business is slow, those are real jobs affecting real families. When a small tattoo shop or boutique sees revenue fall 20% or 30%, the owner often absorbs the loss personally before they reduce staff, but that can only go on for so long.

There is also a neighborhood effect to consider. Small businesses are part of what makes communities feel alive and worth living in. A neighborhood with thriving local restaurants and shops is more appealing than one where storefronts sit empty. When a wave of closures hits, it changes the character of an area and can affect how people feel about living there.

For homeowners and prospective buyers in Las Vegas, the business climate is not a separate issue from the housing market. They are connected. A strong local economy supports home values. A struggling one creates headwinds. If Las Vegas sees continued declines in visitor spending and small business health, that affects the overall economic outlook for the region, and that matters to anyone who owns a home here or is thinking about buying one.

The people who came to that roundtable are not abstractions. They are your neighbors. They own the spot where you get lunch, or the shop you visit for a birthday gift, or the bar where you watch the Golden Knights game. When they struggle, the community feels it, even if it does not always show up right away in the headlines.

Background and History: How Las Vegas Got Here

Las Vegas visitation peaked at around 42 million visitors in the years before the pandemic. The city was on a consistent upward trajectory for most of the 2010s, and the visitor economy felt nearly invincible. Then 2020 hit, and the Strip went dark for months. Visitation collapsed to around 19 million in 2020.

The recovery was fast by most measures. Visitors came back in 2021 and 2022 as restrictions lifted and people were eager to travel. By 2023 and 2024, Las Vegas was back in the low-to-mid 40 million range for some reporting periods. The mood was positive. The city had bounced back.

But 2025 told a different story. The 7.5% decline to 38.5 million visitors happened in an environment where no major external shock like a pandemic was forcing people to stay home. That makes the drop harder to explain away and harder to reverse with a single policy fix.

Several factors converged. International travel softened, partly due to economic conditions in source markets like Canada and Europe, and partly due to concerns about the American travel experience itself. Canadian visitors, who have traditionally been one of Las Vegas's largest international tourism groups, pulled back sharply. Reports of Canadian tourists boycotting American destinations circulated widely in early 2025, and that sentiment appears to have carried through the year.

Domestic travel also faced headwinds. Inflation has eroded discretionary spending for many American households. A trip to Las Vegas is a luxury item for most families. When budgets tighten, that is one of the first things that gets postponed or canceled. Hotel rates on the Strip have also risen substantially over the past few years, which shifts some visitors toward shorter stays or fewer visits per year.

Small businesses off the Strip have always been more vulnerable than the big casino resorts. The resorts can absorb lean periods through their diversified revenue streams and casino floors. A tattoo shop or a family-owned restaurant does not have that cushion. When visitor numbers drop and costs rise, those businesses feel it first and feel it hardest.

What Happens Next

The roundtable with Senator Cortez Masto is unlikely to produce an immediate fix. Policy changes take time. But putting these issues on the record at the Senate level does matter. It signals that Nevada's delegation is aware of the problem and creates a foundation for legislative action.

Several things are being watched closely in the coming months. The Las Vegas Convention and Visitors Authority tracks visitor numbers monthly, and the data for the first half of 2026 will be closely scrutinized. If the decline is continuing into 2026, that will add urgency to calls for support.

Federal tariff policy is one area that business owners are focused on. Many of the cost increases hitting restaurants and shops can be traced back to tariffs on imported goods. Changes in tariff policy could provide some relief on the cost side, even if visitor numbers take longer to recover.

International tourism recovery is another variable. If visa processing becomes smoother and if the messaging around U.S. travel improves in key markets like Canada and Europe, Las Vegas could see a rebound in international visitors. That would help businesses like Studio 21 Tattoo Gallery that depend on that clientele.

Locally, the Las Vegas Metro Chamber of Commerce and other business groups have been advocating for support programs for small businesses. Low-interest loan programs, technical assistance, and workforce training resources are all part of the conversation. Whether those programs can scale to meet the need is an open question.

For individual businesses, the near-term outlook is uncertain. Owners are watching their cash flow carefully, making decisions about staffing and inventory week by week. Some will make it through this period. Others will not. The roundtable was important because it acknowledged that reality without sugarcoating it.

What You Can Do

If you are a Las Vegas resident, the most direct thing you can do is support local small businesses. Spend money at the independent restaurant, the neighborhood shop, the local service provider. It sounds simple, but it is real. Every dollar you spend locally stays in the community longer and supports your neighbors directly.

If you are a small business owner who is struggling, you are not alone, and there are resources available. The Nevada Small Business Development Center offers free consulting and low-cost training. The Las Vegas Metro Chamber of Commerce connects businesses to resources and advocacy. The U.S. Small Business Administration has loan programs designed for exactly these kinds of economic conditions.

You can also contact your elected representatives. Senator Cortez Masto held this roundtable because she is hearing from constituents. If you have a story to share, share it. Elected officials respond to specific, personal accounts from real business owners far more than to general statistics. Your story matters.
