Clark County Commission Approves Pair of 600-Foot Towers on North Strip

The Clark County Commission has approved permits for a big new project that will bring two 600-foot towers to the north end of the Las Vegas Strip. This is one of the largest projects to come to that part of the Strip in years, and it does not depend on gaming. The plan covers 10 acres and includes a hotel, condos, shops, a place for live shows, and even a tall amusement ride. The project is being led by a local developer named Brett Torino through his company, BPS Partners LLC. If everything moves forward, this corner of Las Vegas Boulevard could look very different in a few years.

For folks who live here, news like this matters. The Strip is more than a tourist spot. It is the engine that drives a lot of our jobs, our home values, and the way our city grows. When a project this large gets the green light, it sends a signal about where Las Vegas is heading. In this article, I will walk you through what was approved, why it matters to you and your neighborhood, the history behind this part of the Strip, and what you can do if you want your voice heard. Let us dig in.

What Happened

The Clark County Commission gave its approval to a non-gaming development on the north end of the Las Vegas Strip. The site sits along Las Vegas Boulevard South, near Elvis Presley Boulevard. This area is in unincorporated Clark County, which means it is not inside the City of Las Vegas. It is governed directly by the county commission. That is an important detail, because the rules and the people who make zoning choices are different from the ones in the city proper.

The project itself is large and packed with features. According to reports from 8 News Now and the Las Vegas Review-Journal, the plan calls for two towers that each rise 600 feet into the sky. One of those towers would be a hotel with 750 rooms. The plan also includes 425 condominium units, which would give people a chance to actually own a home right on the Strip. On top of that, there is mixed-use retail space, which usually means shops, restaurants, and other businesses mixed together in one area.

The fun does not stop there. The developers want to build an entertainment arena, plus a 439-foot amusement ride. A ride that tall would be one of the standout attractions on the Strip. There are also plans for a domed performance venue with 3,310 seats, which would give the project a place to host concerts and live shows. Put all of these pieces together, and you have a true mixed-use destination. It is meant to be a place where people can stay, live, shop, and be entertained without leaving the property.

The developer behind the project is Brett Torino, working through BPS Partners LLC. Torino is a local name, not an out-of-state corporation parachuting in. That local connection is worth noting. The land sits on the underdeveloped north end of the Strip, an area that has long had empty lots and older, smaller buildings. The exact date the commission cast its vote was not confirmed in the reporting I reviewed, so I will not put a number on it [NOT VERIFIED]. What we do know for sure is that the approval happened in June 2026 and that the project cleared the permit step it needed to move ahead.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

You might be thinking, "I do not gamble, and I rarely go to the Strip, so why should I care?" That is a fair question. The truth is that big Strip projects ripple out across the whole valley. They touch jobs, housing, traffic, and the value of homes far from Las Vegas Boulevard. Let me break that down.

First, jobs. A project this size needs construction workers to build it and then staff to run it once it opens. We are talking about electricians, plumbers, crane operators, and laborers during the building phase. After that come hotel workers, restaurant staff, retail clerks, performers, ride operators, and managers. Each of those jobs supports a family, and many of those families will be looking for a place to live. That demand touches the housing market, which touches home values across the region.

Second, this is a non-gaming project, and that is a meaningful shift. For most of our history, the Strip ran on casinos. A development that leans on hotels, condos, shops, and live entertainment instead of slot machines shows that Las Vegas is broadening what it offers. That matters because a more diverse economy is a steadier economy. When the city has more than one way to make money, it can weather tough times better. Local groups like the LVGEA have been pushing this exact idea, and a project like this fits right into that goal.

Third, the 425 condos deserve special attention. The Strip is famous for hotel rooms, but it has very few places where regular people can actually own a home. Adding hundreds of condo units creates a new kind of housing option in one of the most recognized addresses in the world. Some buyers will be investors. Others will be people who want a second home or who simply love the energy of the Strip. Either way, new supply near the center of the action can shape demand and pricing in nearby neighborhoods too.

Finally, there is the simple matter of momentum. When a major non-gaming project gets approved on a stretch of land that has sat quiet for years, it tends to attract more interest. Other developers watch closely. If this one succeeds, you may see more cranes and more plans for that part of the boulevard. For homeowners and buyers, momentum on the Strip often means rising interest in the whole region.

Background and History

To understand why this project is a big deal, it helps to know about the north end of the Strip. For decades, the south and central parts of Las Vegas Boulevard got most of the attention. That is where the giant resorts, the dancing fountains, and the biggest crowds are. The north end, closer to downtown, has lagged behind. It has held a mix of older motels, empty lots, and smaller buildings that never grew into the mega-resorts found farther south.

Over the years, several plans were floated to bring the north Strip back to life. Some got started and then stalled. Others never made it past the drawing board. The 2008 recession hit Las Vegas hard, and many big plans across the valley were frozen or canceled. The north Strip carried more than its share of those unfinished stories. So when a project of this scale gets approved here, longtime residents understand it is not a sure thing just because it cleared the permit stage. They have seen plans come and go.

That said, the past few years have brought real change to this area. New attractions, new hotels, and big sports investments have shifted attention back toward the north and central parts of the boulevard. The arrival of major league sports has been a huge part of that story. Each new project makes the next one a little more likely, because investors start to believe the area can support it.

Brett Torino is a familiar name in local development circles. Having a local developer at the helm gives this project a different feel than a deal led by a faraway company. Local developers tend to know the land, the neighbors, and the way the county works. The fact that the site sits in unincorporated Clark County, rather than the City of Las Vegas, also shapes the story. The county commission, not the city council, holds the keys to approvals here. That is why this news is filed under county action, not city action.

What Happens Next

Getting permits approved is a major step, but it is not the finish line. A project like this still has a long road ahead before anyone checks into that 750-room hotel or rides that 439-foot amusement ride. The next stages usually involve detailed design work, financing, and more rounds of review for things like building safety, traffic, and utilities.

Financing is often the biggest hurdle. A development with two 600-foot towers, hundreds of condos, an arena, and a domed venue carries a very large price tag. The developer will need to line up the money to build it, and the cost of borrowing plays a big role in whether projects like this move forward on schedule. If interest rates and the broader economy cooperate, the timeline can move faster. If they do not, even approved projects can sit and wait.

Then comes construction itself. Building towers of this height takes years, not months. Expect to see early site work first, then foundations, then the slow climb of steel and concrete. The condos, in particular, often get marketed and sold before the building is even finished, so you may hear about sales activity well before the towers are done.

I will be watching for the next set of milestones. Those include any official groundbreaking, the start of condo pre-sales, and updates on the timeline for the arena and the domed venue. I will also keep an eye on whether the developer sticks with all the features in the current plan or trims any of them as the project moves along. Big plans sometimes shrink between approval and opening, so it pays to follow the details. As new and confirmed information comes out, I will share it here so you are not left guessing.
