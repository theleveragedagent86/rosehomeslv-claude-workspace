Athletics' Las Vegas Ballpark Steel Bowl Completed, Roof Trusses Rising

The new home of the Athletics is taking real shape on the Las Vegas Strip. At a media tour held on June 12, 2026, team officials announced that all of the steel for the ballpark's seating bowl is now finished. Crews completed that huge job in only about three months. On top of that, the first of five giant roof trusses has been lifted and set into place. This is the structure that will one day hold up the dome over the field. The $2 billion ballpark will seat 33,000 fans and is still on track to open before the 2028 Major League Baseball season. If you live in Southern Nevada, this is one of the biggest projects you will ever see rise in your own backyard.

What Happened

On Friday, June 12, 2026, the Athletics opened the gates to reporters for a tour of their construction site. The site sits on the land where the Tropicana hotel used to stand, right on the south end of the famous Las Vegas Strip. The team wanted to show off how far the work has come, and the progress is hard to miss.

The biggest news from the tour was that the entire steel skeleton of the seating bowl is now done. The bowl is the part of the stadium that wraps around the field and holds all the seats. Building the full steel frame for a bowl this size is a massive task, yet the crews pulled it off in roughly three months. That is a very fast pace for a job this large.

The team also reached another big milestone. Workers lifted the first of five enormous roof trusses and locked it into position. A truss is a strong beam made of many smaller steel pieces joined together. These five trusses will form the main support system for the dome that covers the entire ballpark. Setting the first one in place is a sign that the roof phase is now truly underway.

Officials said the next step starts very soon. The week of June 15, crews will begin installing the concrete seating. This is when the rows where fans will sit start to appear inside the bowl. Watching the seats go in will make the building feel much more like a real ballpark and less like a construction site.

The workforce is also about to grow in a big way. Right now, around 650 skilled craft workers are on the job each day. Once the roof panels start going up, the team expects that number to roughly double. That means even more local jobs tied directly to this one project.

Through it all, the team repeated the same message they have shared before. The ballpark remains on schedule to open before the 2028 MLB season. When it does, it will bring the first Major League Baseball team to Southern Nevada, a moment many local fans have waited years to see.

It helps to understand why the bowl steel mattered so much. The seating bowl is the heart of any ballpark. It has to be strong enough to hold thousands of people, all of their movement, and the weight of the seats and walkways. Getting that frame finished means the building now has its basic shape. Crews can use the completed bowl as a base for everything that comes next, from the seats to the dome above. Finishing it in about three months tells you the crews and planners have kept a tight, well-run schedule.

The roof trusses are just as important in their own way. A domed ballpark needs a roof that can cover the whole field and the stands without blocking the action. Each of the five trusses is huge and heavy, and lifting one into place takes careful planning and the right equipment. Setting the first truss safely is proof that the team and its builders have the system worked out. The other four should follow the same path, one careful lift at a time.

Background and History

The road to this point has been a long one. The Athletics spent decades playing in Oakland, California. After years of trouble finding a new home there, the team set its sights on Las Vegas. Leaders in Nevada worked out a deal to help bring the team to the valley, and the plan slowly moved forward through votes, approvals, and design work.

The chosen spot carries real history of its own. The ballpark is rising on the former Tropicana site. The Tropicana was a classic Las Vegas hotel and casino that stood on the Strip for many years. It hosted shows, guests, and gamblers across several generations. To make room for the new stadium, the old resort was closed and cleared. For longtime locals, watching that change has been bittersweet, since the Tropicana was a piece of old Vegas.

The location was picked on purpose. The south end of the Strip is already home to Allegiant Stadium, where the Raiders play football, plus T-Mobile Arena and other big venues. Adding a baseball park to this zone helps turn the area into a true sports and entertainment hub. The idea is that visitors can find something to do in this corner of the city no matter the season.

The project is also a sign of how Las Vegas is changing. For a long time, the city leaned almost entirely on gaming and shows. In recent years, leaders have pushed to bring in pro sports, large events, and new kinds of business. The A's ballpark fits right into that bigger goal of building a more rounded, year-round economy that does not depend on just one thing.

It is worth remembering how quickly all of this has come together. Just a few years ago, the idea of a major league team in Las Vegas felt like a long shot to many people. Now the steel bowl is finished and the roof is going up. That fast pace shows how serious the team and local leaders are about getting it right. For residents who have watched the south Strip shift from an old resort site to a modern stadium district, the change has been striking to see in such a short time.

What Happens Next

The next several months will be busy at the site. The most immediate step is the concrete seating, which begins the week of June 15. Crews will install the rows that fans will one day fill. As the seats take shape, the inside of the bowl will start to look like the ballpark fans have pictured.

After that, attention turns to the roof. Only the first of five trusses is in place, so the other four still need to be lifted and set. Once the truss frame is complete, workers will begin adding the roof panels that form the dome. This is the stage when the team expects the workforce to roughly double from about 650 craft workers. More hands on site should help keep the project moving toward its goal.

Beyond the structure itself, there is still a great deal of inside work to come. A modern ballpark needs plumbing, wiring, seating finishes, concession stands, restrooms, and all the technology that runs a major venue. None of that is small, and it all has to come together before opening day.

The target remains the same. The A's plan to open the ballpark before the 2028 MLB season. As that date gets closer, expect to hear more about the area around the stadium too. New restaurants, parking plans, and transportation ideas often follow a project like this. For residents and home shoppers, those details will matter just as much as the building itself.

Keep an eye on the worker count too, since it is a handy sign of where things stand. When the crew roughly doubles from about 650 workers, that tells you the roof phase is in full swing. A bigger crew usually means more visible progress from week to week. For neighbors who drive past the site, the building will start to look more finished as the dome takes shape and the seats fill in the bowl.
