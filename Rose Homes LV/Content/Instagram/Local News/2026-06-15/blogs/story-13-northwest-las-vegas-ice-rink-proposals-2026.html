New Two-Sheet Northwest Ice Rink Proposals Due June 15

The City of Las Vegas wants to build a new ice rink in the northwest part of town, and the deadline for builders to send in their plans is today, June 15, 2026. The plan calls for a two-sheet rink, which means two full ice surfaces under one roof. The site sits on five acres of city-owned land in the northwest corridor near North Hualapai Way and West Deer Springs Way. At least one builder has already sent in a proposal. The city pushed the deadline back by eight weeks so more builders could join in, especially as about 3,500 new homes go up nearby. If this rink gets built, it could change how families in Centennial Hills, Skye Canyon, and the rest of northwest Las Vegas play, train, and spend their weekends.

What Happened

The City of Las Vegas opened a window for builders to send in proposals for a brand-new ice arena in the northwest. The plan is for a dual-sheet rink, which is a fancy way of saying two ice surfaces inside the same building. Two sheets matter a lot more than one. With two sheets, a youth hockey team can practice on one side while a learn-to-skate class runs on the other. Families do not have to fight over the same few hours of ice time. A figure skater and a hockey player can train at the same time without bumping into each other.

The land for the project is already owned by the city. It covers five acres near North Hualapai Way and West Deer Springs Way. That spot puts the rink right in the heart of the growing northwest, close to neighborhoods like Centennial Hills and Skye Canyon where thousands of families already live and thousands more are moving in.

The original deadline for proposals came and went, but the city decided to extend it by eight weeks. Why? Because city leaders wanted to give more builders a fair shot at the job. A bigger pool of proposals usually means better ideas, lower costs, and a stronger final product. The timing also lines up with a wave of new housing. Roughly 3,500 new homes are coming online near the site. That is a lot of new neighbors, and many of them will have kids who want something to do.

Even with the extension, at least one builder has already turned in a proposal. That tells you there is real interest in getting this rink done. The reporting on this story comes from FOX5 Vegas and LVSportsBiz, who have been following the city's effort to bring more ice to the valley.

One of the loudest voices on the need for ice belongs to the Vegas Golden Knights. John Penhollow, the team's President of Business Operations, has said the market needs four to eight more sheets of ice just to keep up with demand for youth hockey. That is a stunning number. It means the valley is short on ice by a wide margin, and a new two-sheet rink in the northwest would be a strong first step toward closing that gap.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

For families in northwest Las Vegas, this is about more than hockey. It is about having a place to go. Right now, if you live in Skye Canyon or Centennial Hills and your child wants to skate or play hockey, you may have to drive a long way to reach an open sheet of ice. Practices land at strange hours because there is just not enough ice to go around. Some kids skate at 10 p.m. on a school night. Some families give up the sport because the drive and the schedule are too much.

A new rink near North Hualapai Way and West Deer Springs Way would cut those long drives way down. Parents could get their kids to practice in minutes instead of an hour. That saved time adds up across a season. It means less gas, less stress, and more time at home together. For the 3,500 households moving into the new homes nearby, the rink would be a real perk built right into the neighborhood.

There is also a money side to this story, and it touches every homeowner in the area. Amenities drive home demand. When a neighborhood gets a new park, a new school, or a new recreation center, buyers notice. They are willing to pay a little more to live near something their family will use. A two-sheet ice rink is exactly that kind of draw. It gives the northwest a feature that few other parts of the valley can match.

Think about how a buyer shops. A young family with kids who skate will search for homes close to ice. If the only big rink sits in the northwest, that family will look hard at Centennial Hills and Skye Canyon first. More buyers chasing the same homes tends to support prices and keep demand steady. That is good news for anyone who already owns in the area and good news for anyone thinking of selling down the road.

The rink would also serve people who never lace up a skate. Public rinks host birthday parties, open skate nights, and holiday events. They become gathering spots. In a part of town that is growing fast, having a true community anchor matters. It helps a brand-new subdivision feel like a real neighborhood instead of just rows of houses.

Background and History

Las Vegas has loved hockey since the Golden Knights arrived in 2017 and made a deep playoff run in their very first season. The team won the Stanley Cup in 2023, and the city has not looked back. But there is a problem behind all that excitement. When kids and adults caught the hockey bug, the valley did not have enough ice for everyone who wanted to play.

Ice is hard to come by in the desert. Building and running a rink is expensive. You need huge cooling systems to keep the ice frozen when it is 110 degrees outside. For years, the valley got by with a small number of sheets, and those sheets stayed packed from early morning until late at night. Hockey leagues, figure skating clubs, and public skate sessions all competed for the same limited hours.

As the sport grew, the shortage got worse. More kids signed up every year. The Golden Knights themselves have pushed youth hockey through clinics, learn-to-play programs, and street hockey events. All of that work created demand, but the ice did not keep up. That is why John Penhollow's comment about needing four to eight more sheets carries so much weight. It is not a guess. It reflects years of growing wait lists and crowded schedules.

At the same time, the northwest valley has been one of the fastest-growing parts of Las Vegas. Communities like Skye Canyon and Centennial Hills have added thousands of homes over the past decade. Skye Canyon was planned around an active, outdoor lifestyle, with parks and trails built in. Centennial Hills grew into a major hub with shopping, schools, and medical centers. Families chose these areas because they offered space, newer homes, and a strong sense of community.

Putting a new ice rink in this corridor connects two big trends. You have a sport that is starving for ice, and you have a part of town full of young families with money to spend on activities. The city owns the land already, which removes one of the biggest hurdles in any project. All that is left is to find the right builder with the right plan. That is what the proposal period, ending today, is meant to do.

This is also part of a larger pattern of sports growth across the valley. Henderson has been adding fields and complexes. The PWHL, a pro women's hockey league, has tied itself to Las Vegas. The region keeps proving that it can support more sports at every level, from little kids to professionals.

What Happens Next

With the deadline landing today, the next step is for the city to review every proposal it received. City staff will look at the plans, the costs, the timelines, and the experience of each builder. They will weigh which proposal gives the public the best deal and the best facility. Because at least one proposal already came in before the extension, and the extra eight weeks were meant to bring in more, the city may have several plans to compare.

After the review, city leaders will likely pick a partner or a short list of finalists. From there, the chosen builder and the city would work out the details. That includes the design of the two sheets, how the rink will be paid for, who will run the day-to-day operations, and when the doors might open. Big projects like this take time. Permitting, design, and construction can stretch across a couple of years even when everything goes smoothly.

Residents should watch for public updates from the City of Las Vegas as the review moves forward. There may be chances to weigh in at city meetings. The 3,500 homes coming online nearby will keep the pressure on to get this done, since those new families will want amenities close by. Keep an eye on local news outlets like FOX5 Vegas and LVSportsBiz, which have tracked this story from the start and will likely report the winning proposal once the city makes its choice.
