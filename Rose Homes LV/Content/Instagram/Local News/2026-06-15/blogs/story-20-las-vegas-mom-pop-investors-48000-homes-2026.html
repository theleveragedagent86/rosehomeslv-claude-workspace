Mom-and-Pop Investors Bought About 48,000 Las Vegas Homes in a Decade

Small investors have been busy buyers in the Las Vegas Valley. From 2015 to 2025, mom-and-pop investors purchased about 48,058 single-family homes here, according to UNLV's Lied Center for Real Estate. These are the small players, the people who own 10 homes or fewer. Together they made up nearly 66 percent of all the homes bought by business entities in Clark County over that 10-year stretch. The numbers come from an early June 2026 report by the Las Vegas Review-Journal. That is a huge slice of our local housing market, and it helps explain why so many regular families have a hard time finding a home to buy.

When people hear the word "investor," they often picture giant Wall Street companies. But this story is about something different. Most of the buying was done by smaller folks, not the big firms. That changes how we think about who is competing for homes in Southern Nevada. Let's break down what the report found, why it matters to people who just want a place to live, and what you can do about it if you are trying to buy a home in 2026.

What Happened

The Las Vegas Review-Journal looked at data from UNLV's Lied Center for Real Estate. The Lied Center studies our local housing market and tracks who is buying homes. The big finding was clear. Small mom-and-pop investors bought roughly 48,058 single-family homes in the Las Vegas Valley between 2015 and 2025. That is about 4,800 homes a year on average, every year, for a decade.

So what counts as a mom-and-pop investor? The report defines them as buyers who own 10 or fewer homes. These could be a couple who bought a second house to rent out. They could be a small local business that owns a handful of rental properties. They could be a retiree who put savings into a few homes instead of the stock market. The point is they are small. They are not the massive companies that own thousands of houses across the country.

Here is the part that surprised a lot of people. Those small investors made up nearly 66 percent of all the homes bought by business entities in Clark County. A business entity means the home was bought under a company name, like an LLC, instead of by a person. So when you add up every company that bought a home in our county over those 10 years, two out of every three of those purchases came from these small investors. Only about a third came from the bigger players.

That does not mean the big companies are missing from the picture. Two separate Review-Journal investigations in 2025 found that the valley's two largest home owners are likely big institutional players. The reports pointed to Pretium and Invitation Homes as the largest residential owners in the area. These are the kind of national firms that own homes in many cities and rent them out. They are real, and they own a lot of houses. But the new Lied Center data shows the small investors, all added together, have been doing far more of the actual buying.

It also helps to picture the scale. Imagine roughly 48,000 homes. That is the size of several large master-planned communities put together. It is a number big enough to fill many full neighborhoods across the valley. When you spread that buying over 10 years and across small investors, you can see how it slowly reshaped who owns the homes around us. Each purchase looked small on its own. Added together, they moved tens of thousands of houses out of the hands of would-be owner-occupants and into the rental pool.

The takeaway is that investor demand in Las Vegas comes from many sizes of buyers. Big firms grab the headlines. Small investors do most of the volume. And all of them are shopping in the same market as families who just want a roof over their heads.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

If you are trying to buy your first home in Las Vegas, this report explains a lot. The valley does not have enough single-family homes for everyone who wants one. When you have limited supply and lots of buyers, prices go up and competition gets fierce. Investors of every size are part of that crowd of buyers, and they are competing directly with you.

Think about what happens when a nice starter home hits the market in Henderson or the northwest valley. A young family might want it. But so might a mom-and-pop investor who plans to turn it into a rental. The investor often has advantages. They may pay cash, which is faster and cleaner for sellers. They may not need to ask for repairs. They may waive things that a regular buyer cannot skip, like an appraisal or certain inspections. That makes their offer look stronger, even if a family is offering the same price or more.

When investors win those battles again and again, owner-occupant buyers get pushed to the side. An owner-occupant is simply a person who plans to live in the home they buy. These are the buyers who put down roots, send kids to local schools, and become long-term neighbors. When they lose out to investors, two things happen. First, fewer local families get to own a home. Second, more of our homes become rentals instead of owner-occupied houses.

That affects affordability too. Every home an investor buys and rents out is a home that is no longer for sale. That keeps the for-sale supply tight. Tight supply keeps prices high. High prices keep first-time buyers stuck renting longer than they would like. It can become a cycle that is hard to break, especially for working families in Clark County who are already stretched by the cost of living.

There is also a knock-on effect on the kinds of homes families can find. Small investors tend to target the same affordable, move-in-ready houses that first-time buyers want most. That is the bread-and-butter price range for the valley. When investor demand crowds into that exact slice of the market, entry-level buyers feel it the hardest. The very homes meant to be the first rung on the ownership ladder become the homes investors compete for the most. That makes it tougher for a young family or a first-time buyer to get started, even when they have done everything right.

It is worth saying that investors are not villains here. Rentals are needed. Not everyone wants to buy, and people moving to town often rent first. Small local investors also fix up older homes and provide places to live. But when nearly two-thirds of business-entity purchases come from small investors, regular buyers feel the squeeze. Knowing that helps you plan and compete smarter.

Background and History

To understand today's numbers, it helps to look back. After the 2008 housing crash, Las Vegas had a flood of cheap homes. Prices fell hard, and many houses sold for far less than they once did. That drew investors from all over. Some were huge companies buying in bulk. Many were small buyers who saw a chance to own a rental at a low price. That period set the stage for the investor activity we still see today.

Over the years that followed, Las Vegas kept growing fast. People moved here from California and other states looking for lower costs, no state income tax, and warm weather. Builders put up new neighborhoods, but home building could not always keep up with how many people wanted to live here. Land near the city is limited because so much of the area is federal land. That natural limit on supply has been a long-running theme in our valley.

As demand stayed strong, investors kept buying. The data from UNLV's Lied Center now lets us see the full decade clearly. From 2015 to 2025, the steady drumbeat of small-investor purchases added up to that big 48,058 number. It did not happen in one wild year. It built up slowly, home by home, year after year.

It is also worth remembering how the valley earned its reputation as an investor town. During the recovery years, Las Vegas was often near the top of national lists for investor home buying. National media covered the trend, and word spread that Southern Nevada was a place where rentals could pay off. That reputation pulled in even more buyers, both big and small. Some came and went with the market. Many small investors stayed, kept their homes, and kept buying when they could. The 48,058 figure is the result of all those years stacking up.

The 2025 Review-Journal investigations added another layer. By digging through ownership records, reporters traced which companies owned the most homes. They found that Pretium and Invitation Homes likely sit at the top. This showed that big institutional money is real in Las Vegas. But the newer Lied Center data balances that picture by showing the small investors do most of the buying by count. Both things are true at once, and that is the full story.

What Happens Next

So where does the market go from here? A few things are worth watching in the months ahead. First, keep an eye on whether investor buying speeds up or slows down. When interest rates are high, borrowing costs more, and some investors pull back. When rates ease, they often jump back in. The pace of investor activity can shift with the cost of money.

Second, watch for new rules. Across the country, some lawmakers have talked about limits on large investors buying single-family homes. Some places have floated taxes or caps aimed at big firms. Nevada has not passed sweeping rules like that, but the conversation is alive. If lawmakers act, it could change how many homes investors are able to buy here. Any new rule would likely target the big institutional owners more than the small mom-and-pop buyers, but the details would matter a lot.

Third, watch new home construction. More building is the most direct way to ease the supply crunch. If builders deliver more single-family homes, there is more to go around for everyone, including owner-occupant families. That can take pressure off prices over time. The challenge is that building takes years and is limited by available land.

For everyday buyers, the near future probably looks a lot like the recent past. You will likely still be competing with investors for homes, especially in the more affordable price ranges where small investors shop most. The good news is that being prepared and working with the right help can put you in a strong spot to win. Knowing the competition is real is the first step to beating it.
