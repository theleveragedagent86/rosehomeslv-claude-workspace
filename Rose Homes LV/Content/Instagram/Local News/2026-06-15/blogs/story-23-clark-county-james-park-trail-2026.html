Clark County Opens 3.1-Mile Multi-Use Trail at James Regional Sports Park

Clark County just gave the southwest Las Vegas Valley a brand new place to walk, run, and ride. On June 10, 2026, Commissioner Michael Naft cut the ribbon on a new 3.1-mile multi-use trail loop at James Regional Sports Park, located at 8400 W. Robindale Road. The project cost about $9.8 million, and it is now open to the public every single day. If you live near Mountains Edge, Spring Valley, or anywhere in the fast-growing southwest valley, this is a big deal for your daily life and your home value.

The new loop is built for almost everyone. It has a 10-foot paved asphalt path on one side and a 5-foot decomposed granite running surface on the other. That means walkers, joggers, cyclists, and stroller-pushing parents can all share the space without getting in each other's way. The loop measures the same distance as a 5K race, so runners training for an event finally have a measured route close to home. The trail is open from 6 a.m. to 11 p.m. daily, which gives early-morning exercisers and evening dog-walkers plenty of room in their schedule. Let's look at what this means for you and your neighborhood.

What Happened

On June 10, 2026, Clark County held a ribbon-cutting ceremony at James Regional Sports Park to officially open the new 3.1-mile multi-use trail. Commissioner Michael Naft, who represents much of the southwest valley, led the event and welcomed residents to come try out the path. The trail had been in the works for some time, and the roughly $9.8 million price tag covered the design, the dual-surface construction, lighting, and connections to the rest of the park.

The most interesting part of this trail is the dual-surface design. Most trails in town are just one big strip of concrete or asphalt. This one is different. The 10-foot-wide paved asphalt path is smooth and firm, which is perfect for bikes, scooters, skateboards, wheelchairs, and strollers. Right next to it runs a 5-foot-wide strip of decomposed granite. Decomposed granite is a packed, gravel-like surface that is softer than concrete. Runners love it because it is easier on the knees and joints over long distances. By building both surfaces side by side, the county made sure the trail works for serious athletes and casual families at the same time.

The loop itself was designed to mirror a 5K distance. A 5K is 3.1 miles, which is one of the most popular race lengths in the country. Plenty of people set a personal goal to walk or run a 5K. Now those folks can train on a loop that matches the exact distance, so they always know how far they have gone. No more guessing or driving across town to find a measured course. You just park at James Regional Sports Park, do one full lap, and you have finished a 5K.

The daily hours of 6 a.m. to 11 p.m. are also worth noting. In the desert, the time of day matters a lot. During the hot summer months, many people only want to exercise in the early morning or after the sun goes down. Opening at 6 a.m. lets people get a workout in before the heat builds. Staying open until 11 p.m. gives evening walkers a safe, lit place to move after dinner. The long hours make the trail useful nearly year-round, which is exactly what a growing community needs.

James Regional Sports Park is already a busy spot. It has ball fields, courts, and open space that draw youth sports leagues and weekend crowds. Adding a 3.1-mile trail turns it into an even bigger recreation hub. Families can now drop one kid at a soccer game and take a walk or run while they wait. That kind of flexibility is hard to find, and it makes the whole park more valuable to the people who use it.

A brand new 3.1-mile trail at James Regional Sports Park gives the entire surrounding area a fresh selling point.

Neighborhoods like Mountains Edge and Spring Valley have grown quickly over the past decade. Thousands of new homes went up, and many of those homes filled with young families. Those families want safe, close places to get exercise and let their kids burn off energy. A measured 5K loop with both paved and softer surfaces checks a lot of boxes. Parents can push a stroller, a teenager can ride a bike, and a grandparent can take a slow walk, all on the same loop at the same time.

There is real research behind the idea that trails lift home values. Across the country, homes near well-kept trails and parks tend to sell for more than similar homes that are farther away. The reason is simple. People are willing to pay a little extra to live near a place where they can be active and meet neighbors. A trail also signals that the area is being cared for and invested in, which gives buyers confidence about the future.

For the southwest valley specifically, this trail helps close a gap. As more homes have been built, the demand for public recreation has climbed. Roads and rooftops came first, and now the public spaces are catching up. The county spending nearly $9.8 million on one trail loop is a sign that leaders see the southwest valley as a place worth investing in. That matters to anyone who has money tied up in a home here, because public investment tends to support private property values over time.

The trail also helps with something many desert residents struggle with, which is staying active in a tough climate. When people use the trail, the neighborhood feels more alive and more connected. Friendly faces on a morning walk make a community feel safer and more welcoming, and that feeling shows up when it is time to sell a home.

Background and History

To understand why this trail matters, it helps to look at how the southwest valley grew. For a long time, this part of Clark County was open desert on the edge of town. Then, starting in the early 2000s, builders began turning the area into master-planned communities. Mountains Edge became one of the largest, with thousands of homes, schools, and shopping centers spread across the southern part of the valley. Spring Valley filled in to the north of it. Together, these areas turned into a thriving region full of families.

Fast growth is exciting, but it brings challenges. When homes go up quickly, the public spaces that make a neighborhood feel complete can lag behind. Parks, trails, libraries, and community centers take time and money to plan and build. For years, residents of the southwest valley asked for more recreation options. They had plenty of houses and stores, but the open public spaces that bring people together were still catching up to the population.

James Regional Sports Park was part of the answer. The park gave the area organized sports fields and courts, which served youth leagues and weekend players. It quickly became a gathering point. Still, a sports park focused on games does not always serve people who just want to walk, run, or bike on their own schedule. That is the gap the new trail fills. It turns a sports park into a true all-around recreation destination that works for athletes and everyday families alike.

Clark County has been building out its trail network across the valley for years. The region has flood-control channels, washes, and open desert that can be turned into trail corridors. Over time, the county and nearby cities have added miles of paths so residents can walk and bike more safely. The James Regional Sports Park loop is one more piece of that bigger picture. It does not connect to every other trail yet, but it adds an important, high-quality loop in a part of town that needed it.

The dual-surface design also reflects lessons learned over the years. Early trails were often plain concrete, which works fine for bikes but is hard on runners over long distances. Newer trail projects often mix surfaces so different users get what they need. By choosing both asphalt and decomposed granite, the county built something modern that should serve the community well for a long time. The roughly $9.8 million investment shows that this was a serious, well-planned project, not just a quick add-on.

What Happens Next

Now that the trail is open, the next step is simple. Residents will start using it, and the county will watch how busy it gets. Popular trails often lead to more improvements, like added shade, extra benches, water stations, and better lighting. If the James Regional Sports Park loop draws big crowds, county leaders may look for ways to make it even better and to connect it to other paths in the area.

Connection is the key word to watch. A single 3.1-mile loop is great on its own, but trails become far more powerful when they link together. Over the coming years, expect Clark County to keep looking for ways to tie this loop into the larger valley trail network. The more these paths connect, the easier it becomes to walk or bike between neighborhoods, parks, and shopping without using a car. That kind of connected network is a long-term goal for growing communities.

For homeowners, the next few years could bring more public investment in the southwest valley. A successful trail often encourages more spending on nearby parks and amenities. As the area keeps growing, leaders will likely keep adding recreation options to match the rising population. Each new park and trail adds another reason for buyers to choose the southwest valley, which supports home values across the whole region.

It is also worth keeping an eye on how the trail is maintained. Well-kept trails stay valuable. Trails that fall into disrepair lose their appeal fast. The early signs here are good, given the size of the investment and the modern design. If you live nearby, using the trail and reporting any issues helps keep it in top shape for everyone.
