LVGEA Launches Three-Year Plan to Diversify the Las Vegas Economy

The group in charge of bringing new jobs to Southern Nevada just rolled out a big new plan. On June 15, 2026, the Las Vegas Global Economic Alliance, known as the LVGEA, launched a three-year strategy. The goal is simple to say but hard to do. They want to make the Las Vegas economy less dependent on tourism. To get there, they picked five key industries to grow. These are advanced manufacturing, creative industries, business and financial services, health and life sciences, and technology. If the plan works, it could change the kinds of jobs that come to the valley and the kinds of paychecks families bring home.

What Happened

The LVGEA is the main economic development group for the Las Vegas region. Its job is to bring new companies to the area and help local companies grow. On June 15, 2026, the group shared a fresh three-year strategic plan. The plan sets the path for how the region will try to attract employers and high-wage jobs over the next few years.

The heart of the plan is the choice of five target industries. The first is advanced manufacturing. This means making products with high-tech tools and skilled workers. The second is creative industries, which covers things like film, design, media, and the arts. The third is business and financial services, which includes offices that handle money, banking, and company support work. The fourth is health and life sciences, which covers hospitals, medical research, and companies that make health products. The fifth is technology, which covers software, data centers, and other tech-driven firms.

Why these five? Each one tends to pay solid wages. Each one also helps spread the economy across more than one base. Right now, Las Vegas leans heavily on tourism. When visitors stop coming, the whole region feels the pain fast. The LVGEA wants to build a wider set of industries so the valley has more legs to stand on.

The plan also sets a clear number to chase. The LVGEA wants to raise the region's Hachman Index score. The Hachman Index is a tool that measures how diverse a local economy is. A higher score means the jobs are spread across many industries instead of bunched into just a few. The region's score sits at 74 today. The plan aims to push it toward 76 or 77. That may sound like a small jump, but moving the index for a whole region takes real work over many years.

To fund all this, the LVGEA wants to grow its yearly budget. The budget now sits at about $3.8 million. The group wants to lift it to about $4.5 million. The extra money would pay for more staff, more marketing, and more outreach to companies that might move here. The group also plans to change its legal setup. It wants to become a 501(c)(3), which is a type of nonprofit. That status can make it easier to raise money from donors and grants. It can also help the group work more closely with other community partners.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

For people who live in the valley, this plan is about more than business talk. It is about the steadiness of your paycheck and the value of your home. When an economy depends on one industry, it rides a roller coaster. Tourism is great when times are good. But it can drop hard during a recession, a health scare, or a national pullback in travel. We saw this play out in past downturns when the valley lost jobs faster than almost any other big metro area.

A more diverse economy spreads out that risk. If one industry slows down, others can keep humming. That means fewer big layoffs all at once. It also means more steady demand for housing. When people have stable, well-paying jobs, they can afford to rent or buy with confidence. They are less likely to fall behind on payments during a slow stretch. That steadiness helps protect home values across the whole valley, not just in one neighborhood.

The five target industries also tend to pay more than many tourism and service jobs. A worker in advanced manufacturing or health sciences often earns a higher wage than a worker in a lower-paying service role. Higher wages give families more buying power. They can save for a down payment faster. They can move up from a starter home to a larger one. They can handle the cost of living in a growing region. All of that supports a healthier housing market over time.

There is also a ripple effect. When a new company moves to town with a few hundred high-wage jobs, those workers spend money at local shops, restaurants, and services. They need places to live near their jobs. That demand spreads across many parts of the valley, from the southwest to the north to Henderson and beyond. New employers can even spark new home building in areas that have room to grow. For homeowners, more demand and more jobs usually mean a stronger and steadier market.

Finally, a diverse economy can attract more young families and skilled workers who want long-term careers. People put down roots when they see a future here. They buy homes, raise kids, and stay for years. That kind of steady, rooted growth is exactly what helps a housing market stay healthy through good times and bad.

Background and History

To understand why this plan matters, it helps to look back. Las Vegas grew into a world-famous city on the back of tourism and gaming. Casinos, resorts, shows, and conventions drew millions of visitors each year. That brought jobs, money, and fast growth. For a long time, the formula worked well. The region grew faster than almost anywhere in the country.

But leaning on one main industry came with a cost. During hard times, the valley felt the pain more than most places. When travel slowed, hotels emptied, casinos cut shifts, and workers lost hours. Home values fell sharply during the worst downturns. Many families lost homes. The region learned a tough lesson. A single-industry economy can swing hard in both directions.

Because of that, local leaders have talked about diversifying the economy for many years. The idea is not brand new. What is new is the focused, measured approach in this LVGEA plan. By picking five specific industries and setting a clear target on the Hachman Index, the group is trying to turn a broad goal into real, trackable steps.

The Hachman Index itself is a useful tool here. It compares a region's mix of industries to the mix of the whole nation. The closer a region matches the national spread, the higher its score. A score of 100 would mean a perfectly balanced economy. Las Vegas sits at 74 today. That tells us the region is still tilted toward a few industries. Moving toward 76 or 77 would mean the economy is becoming more balanced and more like the country as a whole.

The valley has already made progress in recent years. Pro sports teams arrived. New stadiums and arenas went up. Tech and logistics companies opened operations near the region. Health systems expanded. These wins showed that companies outside of gaming will choose Las Vegas. The new LVGEA plan tries to build on that momentum and speed it up.

The push to become a 501(c)(3) nonprofit also fits this history. As the group takes on a bigger role, it needs more tools to raise money and partner with others. The budget bump from $3.8 million to $4.5 million reflects the bigger job ahead. Recruiting major employers takes staff, travel, research, and time. The plan recognizes that you have to invest in the effort to see results.

What Happens Next

Now that the plan is public, the real work begins. Over the next three years, the LVGEA will focus on the five target industries. That means reaching out to companies in those fields, pitching the region, and helping firms that already operate here grow. Success will not show up overnight. Recruiting employers and building new industry clusters takes patience.

One thing to watch is the budget. The group wants to grow its funding from about $3.8 million to $4.5 million. That money has to come from somewhere, often from public and private partners. The shift to a 501(c)(3) nonprofit will also take steps to complete. Both moves will shape how much the group can actually do.

Another thing to watch is the Hachman Index score. The plan aims to move it from 74 toward 76 or 77. That number will act as a report card. If the score climbs over the next few years, it means the economy is getting more balanced. If it stays flat, leaders may need to adjust the approach.

Residents can also watch for company announcements. When a manufacturer, tech firm, or health company says it is opening or expanding in the valley, that is a sign the plan is bearing fruit. Each new high-wage employer adds jobs and demand that ripple through the local economy and the housing market. Over the next three years, those announcements will be the clearest proof of whether the strategy is working.

What You Can Do

You do not have to be a business owner to make use of this news. If you live in the valley or plan to move here, the direction of the local economy matters to you. Here are a few simple steps to stay ahead of it.

First, keep an eye on local job news. Watch for announcements about new employers in manufacturing, tech, health sciences, finance, and creative fields. When a big employer moves into an area, nearby housing demand often rises. That can be useful to know whether you are buying, selling, or just tracking your home's value.

Second, think about location when you buy. Homes near growing job centers tend to hold value well. If you are house hunting, ask about what is planned nearby. New offices, campuses, and business parks can lift demand for homes in the area over time.

Third, plan your own finances with steadiness in mind. A more diverse economy helps the whole region, but your own budget still matters most. Keep an emergency fund, watch your debt, and buy a home you can comfortably afford. That way you are ready for both the good times and the slow stretches.

Fourth, stay informed about the bigger picture. Plans like this one shape the valley for years. Knowing where the region is headed helps you make smart, calm decisions instead of rushed ones.
