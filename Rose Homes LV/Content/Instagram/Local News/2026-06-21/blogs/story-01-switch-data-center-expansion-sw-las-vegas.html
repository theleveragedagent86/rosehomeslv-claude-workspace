Clark County Approves Switch Data Center Expansion in Southwest Las Vegas Despite Resident Pushback

Clark County commissioners approved a new Switch data center in southwest Las Vegas on June 17, 2026, even after residents spoke out against it. The project, called LAS 19, will add roughly 56,000 square feet of space on the north side of Warm Springs Road. This matters because it is a clear sign that Clark County plans to keep saying yes to data centers, even as more and more neighbors worry about water, power, and what these giant buildings do to the area around their homes.

What Happened

On June 17, 2026, the Clark County Commission voted to approve Switch's LAS 19 data center project in the southwest part of the Las Vegas Valley, in the Enterprise area. The building will sit on the north side of Warm Springs Road and cover about 56,000 square feet. Switch is a Las Vegas based company that has built large data center campuses in the valley for years, so this is an expansion of work the company already does here.

The vote did not come easily. Residents showed up to speak against the project. People who live near the site raised concerns about traffic, safety, and the strain that big tech buildings can put on local resources. Data centers are warehouses full of computer servers, and even a modest one like this becomes part of a much larger conversation about how much power and water these facilities use across the valley.

During the hearing, Switch made some changes to win approval. The company withdrew its requests for landscaping waivers. A landscaping waiver is when a builder asks the county to skip or reduce normal rules about plants, trees, and green space around a property. By pulling those requests, Switch agreed to follow the standard landscaping rules instead of getting special treatment.

Switch also agreed to pay for a sidewalk safety barrier along Warm Springs Road. This is meant to protect people who walk near the busy road. Adding a safety barrier was one way the company answered some of the concerns raised by neighbors and commissioners during the meeting.

The company also pointed to the way this data center is designed. Switch said LAS 19 uses a closed-loop cooling system with near-zero daily water use. A closed-loop system reuses the same water again and again instead of pulling fresh water every day, which is a big deal in a desert city. Switch also said the facility runs on 100 percent renewable power, meaning energy from sources like solar instead of fuels that pollute the air.

On the county's own scoring system for how sustainable a project is, LAS 19 earned 6.5 out of 7 points. That high score was part of why the commission felt comfortable approving it, even with residents pushing back. In the end, the changes Switch made and the building's design helped the project get the green light.

It is worth slowing down on what those numbers really mean for the people nearby. A near-zero daily water design does not mean the building uses no water at all. It means the cooling system is built to recycle water rather than pour fresh supply down the drain each day. In a normal data center, cooling can be one of the thirstiest parts of the operation, so a closed-loop approach is one of the strongest ways a company can shrink its water footprint in the desert. For commissioners weighing the vote, that design choice carried real weight.

The promise of 100 percent renewable power works in a similar way. Data centers run nonstop, every hour of every day, which means they pull a steady and heavy load from the power grid. When a company commits to renewable energy, it is trying to answer the worry that its building will strain the system or add to pollution. Switch has leaned on this kind of commitment before, and it was central to the case the company made for LAS 19. None of this erased the concerns residents raised, but it gave the commission a record to point to when it voted yes.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

This approval is bigger than one building on Warm Springs Road. It tells you a lot about where Clark County is heading. The county is signaling that it will keep approving data centers even while residents ask for limits on water and power use. For anyone who lives, works, or owns a home in the valley, that is important to understand.

Las Vegas sits in a desert, and water is always on people's minds here. Lake Mead, the main water source for the region, has been dropping for years. So when a new project comes in that could use water and power, neighbors pay close attention. The fact that Switch chose a near-zero daily water design and renewable power shows that companies now know they have to answer these questions head on. Residents who speak up are clearly shaping how these projects get built, even when they do not stop them.

There is also the question of land. The valley has only so much room to grow because much of the surrounding land is owned by the federal government. Every large piece of land that goes to a data center is land that does not become homes, parks, or stores. In the southwest valley, where neighborhoods are still filling in, that trade-off feels very real to families looking for a place to live.

For homeowners, the type of neighbor matters too. A data center is quieter than a factory, but it is still a large industrial building rather than a shop or a park. People care about what sits near their street because it can affect traffic, the look of the area, and how the neighborhood feels day to day. The sidewalk safety barrier that Switch agreed to fund shows that pedestrian safety along Warm Springs Road was a real worry, not just a small detail.

Finally, this vote is part of a much larger fight happening across Southern Nevada. Henderson is looking at a pause on new data centers. Boulder City residents are fighting a project there. Clark County, on the other hand, keeps approving them. Where you live in the valley may soon decide whether your local leaders welcome these projects or slow them down. That makes this a story every valley resident should follow closely.

There is one more reason this matters to your wallet and your future. Data centers bring investment and tax dollars into the county, and they create some construction and tech jobs. That can be good for the local economy that supports home values. The flip side is that they compete for the same water and power that homes need, and they take up land that could become housing. Las Vegas already faces a tight housing supply, so every choice about how land gets used ripples out to buyers and renters. Watching how the county balances these trade-offs will tell you a lot about where prices and growth are headed in the years ahead.

Background and History

Switch is not new to Las Vegas. The company was founded here and has built some of the largest data center campuses in the region. Data centers are buildings packed with computer servers that store and move the information behind the apps, websites, and online services people use every day. As more of life moves online, demand for these buildings keeps growing fast.

Over the past few years, data centers have spread across Clark County and the wider desert Southwest. Companies like them here for a few reasons. Land has been available, the area is far from many natural disasters, and Nevada has worked to attract tech business. The southwest valley and the Enterprise area have seen steady growth, mixing new homes with commercial and industrial projects.

But the growth has come with a backlash. Across the country, neighbors and local governments have started to push back on data centers because of how much electricity and water they can use. A single large campus can draw as much power as a small town. NV Energy, the local utility, has said that the many data centers being proposed could require roughly double the power the utility serves at its busiest times today. That is a huge jump.

That national debate has now reached Southern Nevada in a serious way. Henderson is weighing a 180-day moratorium, which is a temporary pause, on new data center permits. Boulder City residents gathered thousands of signatures to fight a proposed facility there. Against that backdrop, the Switch LAS 19 approval stands out. Clark County chose to keep approving while its neighbors considered slowing down. The closed-loop water design and renewable power on this project show how companies are trying to ease the exact concerns that fuel the pushback elsewhere.

What Happens Next

With the approval in hand, Switch can move forward on the LAS 19 data center on Warm Springs Road. The company will follow the standard landscaping rules it agreed to and pay for the sidewalk safety barrier along the road. Building a data center takes time, so neighbors can expect construction activity in the area before the facility opens. [NOT VERIFIED: an exact construction timeline was not given in the source.]

The bigger story to watch is what other local governments do. Henderson's proposed 180-day pause was referred to a July 21 meeting, so a decision there could come soon. If Henderson approves the pause, it would be the first city in Southern Nevada to hit the brakes on new data centers. That could push more companies to look at Clark County, which has shown it is still open for business.

Boulder City is another place to keep an eye on. Residents there gathered far more signatures than they needed for a ballot petition, and the city council could take up the issue in July. If Boulder City voters end up deciding the matter directly, it could set an example that other valley communities follow.

For the southwest valley, the question is how many more projects will come. NV Energy's warning about doubling peak power demand means the state will likely keep talking about limits on water and power. Future projects may face even more questions about how they cool their servers and where their energy comes from. The Switch approval may turn out to be a model, where companies promise near-zero water use and renewable power to win a yes from county leaders.

State leaders may also step in. As more cities and counties handle data centers in their own way, there could be pressure for clearer statewide rules so that every community is not left to fight the same battle alone. Lake Mead's continued decline only sharpens the focus on water, and any project that touches the valley's supply will draw attention. For now, the safest thing residents can do is stay informed, because the rules around these projects are changing quickly and the next big vote could land close to home.
