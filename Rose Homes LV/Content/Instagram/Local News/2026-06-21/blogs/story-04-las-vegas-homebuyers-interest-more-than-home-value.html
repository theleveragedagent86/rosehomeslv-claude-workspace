Las Vegas Homebuyers Could Pay More in Interest Than the Home Is Worth

Here is the short answer. A new study found that a Las Vegas buyer who purchases a median home today, puts 20 percent down, and keeps the loan for the full 30 years can pay about $456,589 in interest over the life of the mortgage. That is more money in interest than the home itself costs. The median home in the study was priced near $445,000. So the bank can earn more than the price of the house just from the cost of borrowing.

That number is not a typo, and it is not a trick. It is simple math when mortgage rates sit near 6.5 percent. Below I will break down where that figure comes from, why it matters for people living and buying in the Las Vegas Valley, and what you can actually do about it. The goal is to help you understand the true cost of a home loan so you can make a smart, calm decision instead of a scared one.

What Happened

A study from Clever Real Estate looked at what it really costs to buy a home in big metro areas across the country. The study was reported by the Las Vegas Review-Journal on June 18, 2026. Instead of only looking at the sticker price of a home, the study added up all the interest a buyer pays over a full 30-year loan. That is the part many people forget about when they shop for a house.

Here is what the study found for Las Vegas. Start with a median home priced near $445,000. Put 20 percent down, which is about $89,000. That leaves a loan of roughly $356,000. At a mortgage rate of 6.53 percent on a 30-year fixed loan, the buyer pays about $456,589 in interest by the time the loan is paid off. That interest total is larger than the price of the home.

The study also compared today's rates to the rates we saw a few years ago. Back in 2021, the average rate dropped to about 2.96 percent. If a buyer could lock that same loan at that old rate, they would pay around $275,022 less in interest over 30 years. In plain words, the jump from a 3 percent rate to a 6.5 percent rate adds more than a quarter of a million dollars to the lifetime cost of the same house.

One more detail worth knowing. Las Vegas ranked 26th in the country on this list. We are not the most expensive place to borrow, and we are not the cheapest. We sit right in the middle of the pack, which tells you this is a national problem, not just a Las Vegas problem. The same study showed that buyers in pricier coastal metros face even larger interest totals, simply because their home prices and loan amounts are higher to begin with.

It helps to picture how a 30-year loan actually works. When you make your very first mortgage payment, only a small slice goes toward paying off what you borrowed. The rest is interest. As the years pass, the balance slowly shifts, and more of each payment chips away at the principal. By the final years of the loan, almost all of your payment goes to principal and very little to interest. That slow shift is why the total interest piles up so high over three full decades.

A quick note on numbers so we keep things honest. The $445,000 figure in this study is a broad median that covers all housing types, drawn from a Redfin and Clever basket of data. That is a different number than the record many local buyers have seen in the news. The Las Vegas Realtors group reported a record median price of $490,000 for existing single-family homes. Both numbers are real. They just measure slightly different things. Throughout this article, the interest math is tied to the $445,000 study figure.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

For a lot of valley families, the home is the single biggest purchase they will ever make. When you hear that interest alone can cost more than the house, it can feel like the deck is stacked against you. So let me put it in real terms that matter to your monthly budget.

On a loan near $356,000 at today's rates, the principal and interest payment lands close to $2,257 a month. That is before you add property taxes, homeowners insurance, and any HOA fees. In the early years of a 30-year loan, most of that payment goes straight to interest, not to paying down what you owe. That is just how loans are built. The bank front-loads the interest.

This matters because it changes how you should think about buying. A higher rate does not just raise your monthly payment. It quietly raises the total price of the home over decades. Two families could buy the exact same house for the exact same price, but if one buys at 3 percent and one buys at 6.5 percent, they are really paying two very different prices once you count the interest.

It also matters for renters who are trying to decide whether now is the time to buy. With rates this high, the gap between renting and buying has grown in many cases. That does not mean renting always wins. It means the choice deserves a careful look at your own numbers, not a one-size-fits-all answer. Las Vegas rents have actually been falling lately, which adds another piece to the puzzle for anyone weighing the two paths.

The good news is that the interest number scares people more than it should. Very few buyers actually keep the same loan for the full 30 years. People sell, move, or refinance. So while the headline number is true, it is also a worst-case ceiling, not your guaranteed cost. I will come back to that in the section on what you can do.

Background and History

To understand why this is happening, you have to look back a few years. During the pandemic, the Federal Reserve pushed interest rates very low to help the economy. Mortgage rates followed and dropped to historic lows. In early 2021, the average 30-year rate fell below 3 percent. That was the cheapest borrowing many Americans had ever seen.

Those low rates set off a buying rush. People who locked a loan in 2020 or 2021 got a rate that may never come back in their lifetime. Many of those homeowners are now what people call rate-locked. They do not want to sell because doing so would mean giving up a 3 percent loan and taking on a 6.5 percent loan. That keeps homes off the market, which keeps the supply of homes for sale tight here in the valley.

Then came inflation. Prices for everyday goods shot up across the country. To fight that, the Federal Reserve raised its benchmark rate again and again. Mortgage rates climbed right along with it. By 2022 and 2023, rates had more than doubled from their pandemic lows. They have hovered near 6.5 percent ever since.

At the same time, Las Vegas home prices kept climbing. Our valley has been one of the fastest-growing regions in the country for years. People keep moving here from California and other states, drawn by no state income tax, plenty of jobs, and a lower cost of living than many big coastal cities. That steady stream of new residents keeps demand strong. Strong demand plus tight supply equals higher prices.

So buyers today face a double squeeze. Home prices are near record highs, and the cost to borrow is more than double what it was a few short years ago. That combination is exactly why a study like this can show interest costs that beat the price of the home. It is the math of high prices meeting high rates at the same time.

What Happens Next

The big question on every buyer's mind is simple. Will rates come down? The honest answer is that no one knows for sure. What we do know is what the forecasters are saying right now.

At its June 17, 2026 meeting, the Federal Reserve voted to hold its rate steady. As of June 18, 2026, Freddie Mac put the average 30-year fixed mortgage at about 6.47 percent. Most forecasters expect rates to stay near 6.5 percent through the rest of 2026. So for now, buyers should plan their budget around a rate in that range rather than betting on a big drop.

If rates do ease over the next year or two, that would open the door to refinancing. A buyer who purchases today at 6.5 percent and refinances later to, say, 5 percent would cut both their monthly payment and the total interest they pay over time. That is one of the main reasons the worst-case interest number rarely becomes a person's real cost.

On the price side, Las Vegas inventory has been creeping up a little. More homes on the market gives buyers a bit more room to negotiate than they had during the frenzy of 2021. That does not mean prices are crashing. It means the market has calmed down from its peak heat, and patient buyers can find better deals than they could a few years back.

Population growth is the wild card that keeps a floor under prices. As long as people keep moving to the valley, demand stays strong. That makes a big price drop unlikely even if sales slow. The most likely path is a market that moves sideways or rises slowly, with rates that stay high until inflation cools further.

Ryan's Take

I have helped a lot of valley families buy and sell homes, and I want to be straight with you about this study. The headline is shocking on purpose. Yes, the lifetime interest math is real. But that number assumes you never sell, never refinance, and never pay a dollar extra on your loan for 30 straight years. Almost nobody does that.

In my experience, most homeowners move or refinance within about seven to ten years. When they do, they never reach that full interest total. So please do not let the scary number talk you out of a smart purchase. A home is still one of the best ways regular families build long-term wealth, even at today's rates.

Here is how I coach my clients to think about it. Marry the house, date the rate. That means buy a home you love at a price that fits your budget today, and treat the rate as a temporary thing you can change later through refinancing. You cannot control where rates go, but you can control the home you choose and the payment you sign up for.

I also tell people not to wait for the perfect moment, because it almost never comes. Many buyers in 2021 waited for prices to drop and missed out on those once-in-a-lifetime low rates. Many buyers today are waiting for rates to drop and may be missing out on the negotiating room and slower competition we have right now. The best time to buy is when you are financially ready and you find the right home, not when a headline tells you it is safe.

One last thought. This study is a great reminder to shop your loan carefully. The difference between a 6.3 percent rate and a 6.8 percent rate adds up to tens of thousands of dollars over the life of the loan. A little homework on the financing side can save you more than haggling over the price of the house.

What You Can Do

If this study has you thinking hard about your next move, here are practical steps that can lower your true cost as a Las Vegas buyer.

First, shop more than one lender. Get rate quotes from at least three lenders on the same day. Rates and fees vary more than people expect, and a small difference in your rate can save you a large amount over time. Ask each lender for a written loan estimate so you can compare them side by side.

Second, put more down if you can. A bigger down payment means a smaller loan, and a smaller loan means less interest over the life of the mortgage. If you cannot reach 20 percent, that is fine, just know that a larger down payment lowers both your monthly payment and your total interest.

Third, think about extra principal payments. Paying even a little more than required each month, applied to the principal, can shave years off your loan and cut your total interest by a meaningful amount. You do not have to do it forever. Even a few extra payments a year add up.

Fourth, look into down payment help. Nevada's Home Is Possible program can offer down payment and closing-cost help for buyers who qualify. Programs like that can ease the upfront cost and help you buy sooner rather than later.

Fifth, keep refinancing on your radar. If rates fall in the next year or two, refinancing could lower your payment and your total interest. Watch the market, and be ready to act when the numbers make sense for you.

You can also consider buying down your rate. Some lenders let you pay points up front to lower your rate for the life of the loan. Whether that makes sense depends on how long you plan to stay in the home, so ask your lender to show you the break-even math before you decide. If you plan to stay many years, points can pay off. If you plan to move soon, they may not.

Finally, run your own numbers with a professional who knows the valley. A study can give you a national picture, but your situation is unique. The right plan depends on your income, your savings, how long you plan to stay, and the specific neighborhoods you are eyeing. A short conversation about your goals can save you from making a decision based on a headline that may not even apply to your situation.
