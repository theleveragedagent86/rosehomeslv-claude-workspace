How Much Have Las Vegas Property Taxes Risen?

Property taxes in the Las Vegas Valley went up about 3.4 percent from 2023 to 2024. That is the short answer, and it comes from a new study by LendingTree that was broken down by the Las Vegas Review-Journal. For a typical valley home, the median property tax bill rose from $1,990 in 2023 to $2,057 a year later. That is an increase of about $67 a year, or roughly $5.50 a month. It is real money, but it is a small jump compared to many other big cities in the country.

The bigger story is that Las Vegas remains one of the cheapest big metros in America for property taxes. Out of the 49 largest metro areas in the study, Las Vegas ranked 44th overall. That means only five large metros had lower property taxes than we do. So while no one likes to see a bill go up, valley homeowners are still paying far less than people in places like San Jose or Chicago. Below, I will walk you through exactly what the numbers say, why they matter for your wallet, and how Nevada's special tax cap laws keep your bill from jumping the way it does in other states.

What Happened

LendingTree, a national online lending marketplace, put out a report that looked at how fast property taxes climbed across the largest U.S. metro regions from 2023 to 2024. The Review-Journal then pulled out the numbers that matter to us here in the Las Vegas Valley.

Here are the key facts. The median property tax paid on a valley home was $1,990 in 2023. One year later, that median bill had risen to $2,057. That is the 3.4 percent jump. Las Vegas landed at 44th out of the 49 largest metros in the study, which puts us near the very bottom for how much homeowners pay. In plain terms, low is good here. A low ranking means a smaller tax bill.

The study also looked at the effective tax rate, which is the share of your home's value that you pay in taxes each year. Las Vegas came in at 0.48 percent. Only Phoenix was lower, at 0.44 percent, and Birmingham, Alabama tied us at 0.48 percent. Compare that to the highest-rate cities. Buffalo, New York sat at 2.0 percent, Chicago at 1.9 percent, and Cleveland at 1.6 percent. A homeowner in Buffalo pays more than four times the share that a Las Vegas homeowner pays on the same value home.

The biggest one-year increases in the study were in Tampa at 7.7 percent, Denver at 7.4 percent, and Miami at 7.1 percent. Las Vegas, at 3.4 percent, sat well below those hot spots. The smallest increases were in Milwaukee at 0.7 percent and Phoenix at 1.0 percent.

Matt Schulz, a financial analyst at LendingTree, made a point in the report that I want every buyer to hear. He said too many people take too narrow a view when they think about the cost of owning a home. They focus on the price, the interest rate, and the closing costs, but they forget about the recurring costs like property taxes, HOA fees, and insurance. Those costs add up over the years, and ignoring them can wreck a budget.

It is also worth putting this single year in context. The same study group noted that property taxes rose 10.4 percent nationwide between 2021 and 2023, and that across the country property taxes climbed about 30 percent from 2019 to 2024. A big part of that national surge came from the pandemic-era housing boom, when home values shot up fast and assessed values followed. Against that backdrop, the valley's 3.4 percent one-year move looks calm and contained. Nevada's tax cap is a big reason our homeowners did not feel the same shock that homeowners in many other states did.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Property taxes are part of your true monthly cost of owning a home, even if you never write a separate check for them. Most homeowners pay their property taxes through an escrow account that is built into the monthly mortgage payment. So when your tax bill goes up, your monthly payment can quietly creep up too, even if your loan and interest rate never change.

That is why this report matters. A 3.4 percent increase on a typical bill is small in dollar terms, only about $67 a year. But carrying costs do not move alone. Home insurance has been rising in many areas, HOA dues climb over time, and utility costs go up with each hot summer. When you stack all of those together, the cost of simply keeping the home you already own can grow year after year. Knowing where property taxes fit in that picture helps you plan.

For buyers, the news is mostly good. Las Vegas has long been a magnet for people moving from California, and one of the reasons is taxes. Nevada has no state income tax, and as this study shows, our property taxes are among the lowest of any major metro in the country. A family moving from San Jose, where the median property tax was $9,901, to Las Vegas, where the median was $2,057, can save thousands of dollars every single year on property taxes alone. That savings can make a higher monthly mortgage payment feel a lot more manageable.

For people already in their homes, the report is a reminder to check your tax bill and make sure you are getting the lower tax rate that Nevada gives to people who live in their own home. I will explain that rate in the next section, because it is one of the most important and most misunderstood parts of owning a home in Nevada.

Background and History

To understand why Las Vegas property taxes rise slowly, you have to understand Nevada's tax cap law. Back in 2005, Nevada lawmakers passed a law that limits how much your property tax bill can go up each year. This was a response to fast-rising home values during the housing boom, when people worried that climbing assessments would push families out of homes they could no longer afford to keep.

Here is how the cap works. If the home is your owner-occupied primary residence, meaning you live in it as your main home, the law caps the yearly increase in your property tax bill at 3 percent. So no matter how much your home's value shoots up in a given year, the tax bill on your primary home cannot rise by more than 3 percent from one year to the next. This is sometimes called the tax abatement.

Other property is treated differently. For homes that are not your primary residence, such as rental homes, second homes, and most commercial property, the cap is higher. By law it can be set as high as 8 percent in a given year. The exact cap on non-primary property is figured each year using a formula tied to growth and the local economy, and it can land anywhere up to that 8 percent ceiling. The simple takeaway is this. Your own home gets the strongest protection with the 3 percent cap. A rental or second home can see its tax bill rise faster, up to 8 percent.

This is why the LendingTree study showed Las Vegas with only a 3.4 percent increase while cities like Tampa and Denver saw jumps above 7 percent. Those states do not have the same kind of cap we do, so when home values surge, the tax bills surge right along with them. The small detail worth noting is that the valley's overall 3.4 percent average sits just above the 3 percent primary-home cap. That is because the valley-wide median blends together primary homes, rentals, second homes, and new construction. Newly built homes and properties that change hands can reset to current value, and non-primary homes can climb faster, so the blended average can land a bit above 3 percent even though most owner-occupied homes are held to that 3 percent line.

One thing every Nevada homeowner should double-check is that the county has you on file as the owner-occupant. The lower 3 percent cap is not automatic in every case. The Clark County Assessor sends out a card to confirm how a property is used, and if you do not return it or if records are out of date, your home could be billed at the higher non-primary rate by mistake. It is worth a quick call to the Assessor's office to make sure your primary home is getting the 3 percent cap it deserves.

It also helps to understand how a Nevada property tax bill is actually built, because it is different from a simple percentage of your purchase price. The county does not tax your full market value. Instead, it assesses your property at 35 percent of its taxable value, and the tax rate is then applied to that smaller assessed amount. On top of that, the cap is applied to the bill itself, so even when assessed values rise, the abatement holds the year-over-year increase down. This layered system is the engine behind those low effective rates the LendingTree study reported. It is also why two neighbors with similar homes can end up with different bills, since the year each one bought, and whether each home is a primary residence, both feed into the final number.

What Happens Next

Property values in the Las Vegas Valley have stayed strong, with the median home price sitting near $490,000 in recent local data. As long as values hold up or keep climbing, assessed values will keep rising, and tax bills on primary homes will keep inching up by that capped 3 percent each year. That is the system working as designed. Slow, steady, and predictable.

Each year, the Clark County Assessor sets new assessed values, and the cap rates for non-primary property get recalculated. So expect another modest bump on most owner-occupied homes in the next cycle, and watch for a possibly larger move on rentals and second homes if the formula lands higher. The exact figure for the next year's non-primary cap is set by the state and the county, so I would not guess at a number here. What I can tell you is that the 3 percent protection on your primary residence is locked into Nevada law and is not changing.

The broader trend to watch is the pile-up of carrying costs. Even with our low taxes, buyers are dealing with mortgage rates near 6.5 percent and home insurance that has been creeping up across the desert Southwest. Property taxes are the calm, predictable part of that equation in Nevada. The other pieces are the ones that move faster and need closer watching.

Ryan's Take

When I sit down with clients who are moving here from California, Washington, or other high-tax states, the property tax conversation is one of my favorite parts of the job. People are often shocked at how low the numbers are. A buyer might be nervous about a $490,000 valley home until they realize the annual property tax is closer to $2,000 than the $9,000 or $10,000 they were bracing for based on where they came from. That single fact changes how much home they feel comfortable buying.

My honest advice is to treat Nevada's 3 percent cap as one of the real, lasting reasons to own here, but do not let it lull you into ignoring the rest of your monthly costs. Schulz from LendingTree was right. The smartest buyers I work with look at the full picture from day one. That means the loan payment, the property tax, the insurance, the HOA dues if there are any, and a cushion for utilities and repairs. When you build your budget around the full cost rather than just the loan payment, you almost never get surprised, and surprises are what get people into trouble.

I also tell every homeowner to confirm their property is flagged as a primary residence with the Clark County Assessor. I have seen homeowners pay more than they had to simply because the paperwork was never returned and the home defaulted to the higher cap. A five-minute phone call can lock in your 3 percent protection. That is one of the easiest money-saving moves you can make as a Nevada homeowner, and a lot of people never do it.

What You Can Do

If you own a home in the valley, here are a few simple steps worth taking. First, pull up your most recent property tax bill and confirm it is being taxed at the owner-occupied rate with the 3 percent cap. If you are not sure, call the Clark County Assessor's office and ask them to confirm your primary residence status. Second, if you own a rental or a second home, understand that it can be taxed at a higher cap, up to 8 percent, so budget for a bigger possible increase on those properties than on your main home.

If you are thinking about buying, ask your lender to give you a full monthly payment estimate that includes property taxes, insurance, and any HOA dues, not just the loan payment. That full number is your true cost, and it is the one you should base your budget on. And if you are moving here from a higher-tax state, run the savings. The difference in property taxes alone can free up real room in your budget for a better home or a bigger cushion.

Property taxes are just one piece of the homeownership puzzle, but they are a piece where Las Vegas homeowners come out ahead of almost everyone else in the country. Knowing the rules, confirming your status, and planning for the full cost are the habits that keep your home affordable for the long run.
