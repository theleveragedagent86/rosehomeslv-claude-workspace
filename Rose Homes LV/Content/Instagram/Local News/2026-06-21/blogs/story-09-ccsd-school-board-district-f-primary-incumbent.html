CCSD School Board Incumbent Leads in District F Primary

Incumbent Trustee Irene Bustamante Adams led the Clark County School District (CCSD) Trustee District F race with 49.3 percent of the vote in preliminary results, according to the Las Vegas Review-Journal. District F covers part of the southwest valley. She finished ahead of two challengers, Robert Hutchinson, a district facilities director, and Laurence Neely, a Turning Point Action organizer who was endorsed by Moms for Liberty. The results were preliminary at the time of reporting, and union endorsements in the race were split between Bustamante Adams and Hutchinson.

If you own a home in the southwest valley, or you are thinking about buying one, this race matters more than it might seem. The people who sit on the school board help decide attendance zones, which schools get built, how money is spent, and how the district is run day to day. All of that touches your home value and your family's daily life. Below I break down what happened, why it matters, the history behind it, what comes next, and what you can do about it.

What Happened

In the District F primary, three candidates ran for one seat on the Clark County School District Board of Trustees. The early count showed incumbent Irene Bustamante Adams in first place with 49.3 percent. That is close to a majority, but at the time of reporting the results were still preliminary. The other two candidates, Robert Hutchinson and Laurence Neely, split the rest of the vote.

Here is a quick look at the three people on the ballot:

Irene Bustamante Adams is the current trustee for District F. As the incumbent, she already holds the seat and is asking voters to let her keep it. A former state lawmaker, she has experience in public office. In this race she picked up the support of some labor unions.

Robert Hutchinson works as a facilities director for the school district. That means his day job involves the buildings, grounds, and physical needs of schools. He also earned union backing in the race, with endorsements split between him and the incumbent.

Laurence Neely is an organizer with Turning Point Action, a conservative political group. He was endorsed by Moms for Liberty, a national group that has been active in school board races across the country. His campaign represents a more conservative lane in the contest.

It is worth slowing down on one detail. The union endorsements in this race were split. Unions did not all line up behind one person. Some backed the incumbent, and some backed Hutchinson. That split tells you the race was competitive and that even groups that often agree did not see a single clear choice. I am keeping this even-handed on purpose. My job here is to tell you who ran and what the early numbers showed, not to tell you who to support.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

You might wonder why a real estate agent is writing about a school board race. The honest answer is that schools and home values are tied together more tightly than most people realize. When buyers shop for a home in the Las Vegas valley, the assigned school is often one of the first things they check. A strong, well-run school can lift demand for homes in its zone. A struggling or uncertain school can soften it. The board of trustees helps shape all of that.

Let me walk through the main ways a school board touches your home and your family.

School zoning and attendance lines. CCSD decides which neighborhoods feed into which schools. These attendance boundaries can change. When a fast-growing area like the southwest valley adds rooftops, the district sometimes redraws lines or opens new campuses to handle the crowd. If your address shifts from a highly rated school to a lower-rated one, that can affect how buyers view your home. The reverse is also true. A rezoning into a sought-after school can give a home a real lift. The board of trustees has a say in these decisions, so who sits on it matters to property owners.

New school construction. The district plans and funds new buildings. In a growing part of the valley, families want schools close to home. A new, modern campus nearby can be a selling point. The pace and location of construction flow in part from board choices about budgets and priorities. It is no accident that one of the candidates in this race, Robert Hutchinson, works in facilities. Buildings and growth are a big part of what the district handles.

How families choose where to live. Many buyers, especially those with children, pick a neighborhood based on the school first and the house second. A stable, well-led district gives those families confidence. When a board is in turmoil or a district feels unpredictable, some families hesitate or look elsewhere, including at private schools, charter schools, or homes in a different zone. That hesitation can ripple into local demand.

Home values over time. National research has long shown a link between school quality and home prices. Homes in strong school zones often hold value better and sell faster. I am not saying a single election will move your home price overnight. It will not. But the board sets a direction over years, and direction matters. Steady leadership tends to support steady demand.

So when you see a school board race in the news, it is fair to read it as a housing story too. The southwest valley is one of the busier growth areas in the region, and District F sits right in that path. Decisions about schools there will shape how appealing those neighborhoods feel to the next wave of buyers.

Background and History

The Clark County School District is one of the largest school districts in the country. It serves hundreds of thousands of students across Las Vegas, Henderson, North Las Vegas, Boulder City, and the unincorporated parts of Clark County. A district that size is a huge operation, with thousands of employees and a budget in the billions. The Board of Trustees is the elected body that helps set policy and oversee the district at the top.

Trustees are elected by district, not at large. That means each trustee represents a specific geographic area, like District F in the southwest valley. Voters in that area choose their trustee. This is why a race in one part of town can feel local even though the district itself is enormous. The person who wins District F speaks for that slice of the valley on the board.

School board races used to be quiet, low-turnout affairs. In recent years that has changed across the country, and Clark County is no exception. These races have become flashpoints in larger debates. You see candidates backed by labor unions, candidates backed by conservative groups, and candidates who run as independents or as part of the existing leadership. That mix is exactly what showed up in this District F primary. An incumbent with union ties, a facilities director with union ties, and an organizer tied to conservative groups all on one ballot.

I want to be careful and factual here. Different groups bring different priorities to schools. Some focus on funding and staffing. Some focus on curriculum and parental rights. Some focus on buildings and operations. None of that is unique to Clark County. What is local is the result, and in District F the early count put the sitting trustee in the lead.

It also helps to remember the broader setting. Nevada has been one of the fastest-growing states in the nation, and the Las Vegas valley keeps adding people and homes. Growth puts pressure on schools. More families mean more students, more crowding in popular zones, and more pressure to build. That growth backdrop is part of why school governance has drawn so much attention. The decisions made now will shape schools for a population that keeps expanding.

What Happens Next

Because the reported results were preliminary, the first thing to watch is the final, certified count. Early numbers can shift as the last ballots are counted. The 49.3 percent figure for the incumbent was a snapshot, not a final word.

The next question is what 49.3 percent means for the path forward. In many Nevada races, the rules of the primary decide whether the contest is settled or moves on to the general election. If a candidate clears a majority in the primary, the race can end there. If no one does, the top finishers can advance to the November general election. Whether this specific seat is decided in the primary or heads to a runoff or general election was not fully clear from the early reporting, so I am marking that point as [NOT VERIFIED]. I do not want to tell you the contest is over when the final outcome was still uncertain at the time of the report.

Here is what I would keep an eye on in the weeks ahead:

The certified results. Once the count is final and official, you will know exactly where each candidate landed.

Whether a general election follows. If the seat advances to November, the same candidates, or the top two, could face voters again. That would give the contest a second act with higher turnout.

The board's makeup overall. District F is one seat. The full board sets direction together. Watching how this seat fits into the larger board will tell you more than any single race.

Decisions that touch the southwest valley. Keep an ear out for talk of new schools, rezoning, or boundary changes in growing parts of the valley. Those are the items that connect most directly to home values and family choices.

What You Can Do

If this race caught your attention, here are practical steps you can take, whether you own a home, rent, or are planning to buy.

Find your trustee district. Look up which CCSD trustee district covers your address. Knowing your district tells you whose race to follow and whose decisions affect your schools.

Check your assigned schools. Use the district's school locator to confirm which schools serve your home. If you are house hunting, do this for any address you are serious about.

Watch for the certified results. Since the early count was preliminary, follow the final numbers and find out whether the seat is settled or moves to a general election.

Attend or stream a board meeting. Board of Trustees meetings are public. You can watch how decisions get made, including anything about new schools or boundary changes near you.

Speak up on rezoning. If the district proposes a boundary change in your area, there is usually a public comment window. Your voice counts, and these changes can touch your home value.

Vote in local races. School board races often draw low turnout, which means each vote carries more weight. If you care about your neighborhood schools, mark your calendar for the next election.

Talk to a local expert before you buy or sell. If you are weighing a move and schools are part of your decision, get a clear read on the assigned schools and any changes on the horizon. That is exactly the kind of detail I help clients sort through.
