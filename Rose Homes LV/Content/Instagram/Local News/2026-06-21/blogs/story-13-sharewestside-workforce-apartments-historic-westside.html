shareWESTSIDE Workforce Apartments Open in Las Vegas Historic Westside

shareWESTSIDE is a five-story, 104-unit apartment complex that opened June 17 in the Historic Westside of Las Vegas.

A brand-new apartment building opened its doors on June 17, 2026, in one of the oldest and most overlooked neighborhoods in Las Vegas. The building is called shareWESTSIDE, and it sits at the corner of Jefferson Avenue and D Street in the Historic Westside. It is a five-story complex with 104 apartments and about 9,000 square feet of ground-floor retail space. More than 80 of those apartments are set aside as workforce housing, which means the rents are meant to be affordable for working people who hold everyday jobs in the valley. Rents start at around $1,275 a month, and the total cost of the project came to roughly $26 million.

The grand opening was a big deal for the neighborhood. Nevada Governor Joe Lombardo and Las Vegas Mayor Shelley Berkley both showed up for the ribbon-cutting. That kind of attention does not usually land on the Historic Westside, a community that developers have largely passed over for decades. The fact that a new mixed-use building opened here, with both housing and retail, signals something that residents have wanted for a very long time. This post breaks down exactly what opened, why it matters, the history behind the neighborhood, and what it could mean for the future.

What Happened

shareWESTSIDE is a five-story building with 104 apartment units. Workforce housing is built for people who earn a regular paycheck but find market-rate rents hard to keep up with. Think of the workers who keep the valley running every day, from healthcare aides and teachers to service and hospitality staff. Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

For most of the people who live in the Historic Westside, the biggest thing about shareWESTSIDE is simple. It is new housing in a place that has not seen much new investment in a very long time. Las Vegas has been struggling with housing costs for years. Rents climbed sharply in the early 2020s, and even though they have softened a little more recently, finding an apartment that fits a working budget is still hard for a lot of families. A building with more than 80 workforce units that start near $1,275 a month gives some of those families a real option they did not have before.

The location matters too. The Historic Westside sits close to downtown Las Vegas, which means residents of shareWESTSIDE are near jobs, transit, and services without having to drive far across the valley. When affordable housing gets built far out on the edges of the metro area, the people who live there often spend more time and money commuting. Building closer to the urban core keeps housing connected to the places where people actually work. That is a smarter way to grow, and it benefits the whole region, not just the people in the building.

There is also the retail piece. The roughly 9,000 square feet of ground-floor commercial space gives small businesses a chance to open in a part of town that has long lacked everyday services. A neighborhood with shops, a place to grab a coffee, or a small clinic feels different from one where residents have to leave the area for every errand. Retail that is built into a residential building can bring foot traffic, jobs, and a sense of life back to a corner that has been quiet for years. For long-time Westside residents, that is part of what makes this project feel like more than just housing.

Finally, projects like this matter because of what they signal to other developers. The Historic Westside has been seen as a place that is too risky or too overlooked to build in. When a $26 million mixed-use project opens here and draws the governor and the mayor, it sends a message that this neighborhood is open for investment. One successful building can change how the next developer thinks about the area.

Background and History

To understand why shareWESTSIDE is such a meaningful opening, you have to understand the Historic Westside itself. This neighborhood is one of the most historically important Black communities in all of Nevada. For much of the twentieth century, segregation and discriminatory housing practices in Las Vegas pushed Black residents into the Westside. At the same time, those same practices often kept investment, banking, and development out. The result was a community with deep roots and a rich cultural history that was also denied the kind of public and private investment that flowed freely to other parts of the valley.

The Westside has long been home to landmarks, churches, and businesses that anchored Black life in Las Vegas during decades when much of the rest of the city was closed off to those same residents. Jackson Avenue and the surrounding streets once buzzed with clubs, restaurants, and gathering places. Over time, though, freeway construction, the loss of major employers, and decades of disinvestment took a heavy toll. Buildings emptied out, lots sat vacant, and the neighborhood saw very little new construction while the rest of Las Vegas grew at a breakneck pace.

That pattern is exactly why the Las Vegas Review-Journal described the Westside as a community long overlooked by developers. For years, plans and promises came and went, but actual buildings rarely followed. Residents grew used to hearing about projects that never broke ground. So when a five-story, 104-unit complex with retail and workforce housing actually opened, it landed as something rare. It was not a plan or a study or a proposal. It was a finished building with people moving in.

The Historic Westside is one of the most culturally significant Black communities in Nevada and has gone decades with little new development.

In recent years, city leaders and community advocates have worked to bring more attention and resources to the Westside. There have been redevelopment plans, cultural preservation efforts, and a push to attract the kind of investment the neighborhood was denied for so long. shareWESTSIDE fits into that larger effort. It is not the first attempt to revitalize the area, but it is one of the more concrete results to come out of that work. The project's name itself, with WESTSIDE in capital letters, places the neighborhood front and center.

What Happens Next

With shareWESTSIDE now open, the first thing to watch is how quickly the apartments fill and how the retail space gets used. The success of a mixed-use building depends a lot on whether shops and services actually move into the ground floor. If that 9,000 square feet draws in businesses that residents want, the building becomes a true community hub rather than just a place to sleep. If the retail sits empty, the project still provides much-needed housing, but it falls short of its full promise. The coming months will show which way it goes.

The bigger question is whether shareWESTSIDE leads to more projects in the Historic Westside. One building does not transform a neighborhood on its own. But a successful, high-profile opening can lower the perceived risk for the next developer who looks at the area. If shareWESTSIDE fills up, if the retail thrives, and if residents are happy, it becomes a case study that advocates and city officials can point to when they pitch the next project. That kind of momentum is exactly what the neighborhood has lacked for decades.

State and local government attention will also matter. The presence of Governor Lombardo and Mayor Berkley at the ribbon-cutting suggests that workforce housing and Westside revitalization are priorities at the highest levels. Nevada has been grappling with a serious shortage of affordable and attainable housing across the whole valley, and projects like this one are part of the response. Whether more public support, funding, or partnerships follow will help determine how fast the Westside sees additional development.

It is also worth watching the broader Las Vegas housing market. Rents in the valley have been softening, which is good news for renters but can make it harder for developers to pencil out new affordable projects. Workforce housing often depends on a mix of public help, tax credits, and patient financing to make the numbers work. How those pieces hold up over the next year or two will shape how many more shareWESTSIDE-style projects actually get built.
