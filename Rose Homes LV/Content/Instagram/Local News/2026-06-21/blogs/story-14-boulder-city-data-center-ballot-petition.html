Boulder City Residents Rally Against Proposed Data Center as Ballot Petition Surges

Boulder City residents are pushing hard against a plan to build the town's first data center, and they may soon get to vote on it themselves. A company called Townsite Solar 2 LLC wants to put a data center on 88.5 acres of city-owned land just south of Interstate 11. Many residents do not want it. They started a citizens' petition, and the response has been huge. The petition needs 1,700 valid signatures by the end of June to qualify for the ballot. As of mid-June, organizers say they have collected more than 6,200 signatures. That is well over three times the number they need.

The Boulder City Planning Commission has already weighed in. In May, the commission voted 6 to 1 against the project. Now the matter heads toward the City Council, which could take it up as soon as July. If the petition qualifies, voters could decide whether to block data centers in their town for good. This is a big moment for Boulder City and a story worth watching for anyone who lives in Southern Nevada.

What Happened

The conflict centers on a piece of land that Boulder City owns. The site sits on 88.5 acres south of Interstate 11, the highway that connects Boulder City to Henderson and the rest of the Las Vegas Valley. Townsite Solar 2 LLC wants to lease or use that land to build a data center. A data center is a large building, or group of buildings, packed with computer servers. These servers store data and run the apps, websites, and artificial intelligence tools that people use every day. They run around the clock.

This would be the very first data center in Boulder City. The town has a long history of keeping growth slow and steady, so a project this large drew attention right away. Residents started asking questions about power use, water use, noise, and what the building would look like. Many did not like the answers they got. So a group of residents launched a citizens' initiative. That is a process where voters can put a measure on the ballot if they gather enough signatures.

The petition needs 1,700 valid signatures by the end of June to qualify. Organizers blew past that goal fast. By the middle of the month, they reported more than 6,200 signatures. In a town the size of Boulder City, which has roughly 15,000 to 16,000 residents, that is a striking number. It shows that opposition is not coming from just a small group. A large share of the community signed on.

The Planning Commission acted before the Council. In May, the commission voted 6 to 1 to recommend against the project. A planning commission is an advisory board. It studies projects and gives the City Council a recommendation. The Council does not have to follow that advice, but a 6 to 1 vote against sends a strong signal. The Council could take up the data center, the petition, or both as soon as July.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

You might wonder why a fight over land in Boulder City matters if you live in Summerlin, Henderson, or the southwest valley. It matters because this is part of a much bigger story playing out across Southern Nevada and the whole country. Data centers are popping up everywhere, driven by the rise of artificial intelligence and cloud computing. These buildings need two things in large amounts. They need electricity, and many of them need water for cooling. Both of those are precious in the desert.

Southern Nevada already worries about water. Lake Mead, the reservoir that supplies much of the region's water, has been near record lows for years. Power demand is also climbing as the population grows. When a new data center asks to plug into the grid and draw on local resources, neighbors take notice. The Boulder City fight is one of several across the region. Henderson recently introduced a 180-day pause on data centers. Lawmakers and local boards across Nevada are weighing how to handle these projects. Boulder City may be the first place where everyday voters, not just elected officials, get the final say.

That is the part that could ripple outward. If Boulder City voters decide to block data centers through the ballot box, other communities could follow the same path. A successful citizens' initiative becomes a model. It shows residents in other towns and valley neighborhoods that they can take a direct hand in shaping what gets built near their homes. For homeowners, decisions like this affect property values, traffic, noise, and the basic character of a neighborhood. Those are the things that make a place feel like home, and they are the things buyers ask me about all the time.

Background and History

To understand this fight, you have to understand Boulder City. It is one of the most unusual towns in Nevada. The federal government built it in the early 1930s to house the workers building Hoover Dam. For decades it was a company town with strict rules. To this day, Boulder City is one of only two places in Nevada where gambling is not allowed. That alone tells you how different it is from Las Vegas and Henderson.

Boulder City is also famous for controlling its own growth. In 1979, voters passed a growth control ordinance that limits how many new homes can be built each year. The city owns a large amount of surrounding land and has historically been careful about what it allows on that land. Residents take real pride in keeping the town small, quiet, and tied to its history. Many people move there on purpose to escape the rapid growth and bright lights of the valley. So when a large industrial-style building gets proposed on city-owned land, it touches a nerve that runs deep.

The land in question matters too. Because the city owns the 88.5 acres, residents feel they have a direct stake in the decision. This is not private land where an owner can do mostly what they want. This is public land, and the public is making its voice heard. The connection to Townsite Solar 2 LLC also raises questions for some residents about how a solar-named company ended up tied to a data center proposal. Data centers and power generation often go hand in hand, since the centers need so much electricity. But the details of who builds what, and who pays for the power and infrastructure, are exactly the kinds of things residents want spelled out before anything moves forward.

Across Nevada, the data center wave is fairly new. A few years ago, most people had never thought about these buildings. Now the state is becoming a hot spot because it has open land, sunshine for solar power, and a business-friendly climate. That has set up a statewide debate. Some leaders welcome the jobs and tax money these projects can bring. Others worry about strain on water and power and about the look and feel of their communities. Boulder City has become one of the loudest fronts in that debate.

It also helps to understand how the petition process works in Nevada. A citizens' initiative lets registered voters propose a law or a ban and put it on the ballot if they collect enough valid signatures. The required number is usually tied to how many people voted in a past election, which is why Boulder City's threshold landed at 1,700. Once organizers turn in their signatures, the city or county checks each one against the voter rolls. Only signatures from registered voters in the right area count. That is why having more than 6,200 signatures gives the Boulder City group a wide safety margin. They can lose hundreds of signatures to errors and still clear the bar with room to spare.

This is also not the first time Boulder City voters have used the ballot to steer their town's future. The 1979 growth control vote is the most famous example, but residents have weighed in on land sales and major projects more than once over the years. There is a real tradition here of voters reaching for direct democracy when they feel a decision is too important to leave to the Council alone. That history is part of why the petition gained steam so fast. People in Boulder City know the playbook, and they know it can work.

What Happens Next

The next few weeks are the important ones. The petition needs its 1,700 valid signatures certified by the end of June. With more than 6,200 reported, organizers have plenty of cushion. But signatures still have to be checked to make sure they come from registered Boulder City voters. Some signatures usually get tossed for small reasons, like a person not being on the voter rolls or signing twice. Even with a generous buffer, the official count matters.

If the petition qualifies, the City Council has a choice. In many initiative processes, the Council can either adopt the measure as written or send it to the voters in an election. The Council could take up the data center proposal and the petition as soon as July. So residents will be watching the Council's July meetings closely. The 6 to 1 Planning Commission vote against the project gives opponents momentum heading into those meetings.

There are a few ways this could go. The Council could reject the data center outright and end the matter. The Council could send the question to voters in an upcoming election. Or the project applicant could withdraw or change the plan in the face of strong opposition. Each path leads to a different outcome, and each will tell us something about how Southern Nevada handles the next data center proposal, and the one after that. I will be keeping an eye on the Boulder City Council agenda and will share updates as they come.

One more thing to watch is timing. Petitions, certification, and Council action all run on a calendar, and elections in Nevada happen on set dates. If the question goes to voters, it may have to wait for the next regular election rather than a quick special vote. That delay could keep the data center proposal in limbo for months. For residents, a long wait can be frustrating, but it also gives both sides more time to make their case to the public. Expect the conversation in Boulder City to stay loud well past July.

Ryan's Take

As a Las Vegas real estate agent, I pay close attention to stories like this one. They shape the neighborhoods my clients buy in and sell in. Here is my honest read. The strength of this petition is the headline. Gathering more than 6,200 signatures when you only need 1,700 is not a close call. That is a community speaking with one loud voice. When residents organize that quickly and that strongly, it usually means the issue touches something they truly value. In Boulder City, that something is the slow-growth, small-town way of life they have protected for decades.

I do not see this as anti-business, and I would caution anyone from reading it that way. Data centers can bring jobs and tax revenue, and Nevada has good reasons to want some of that investment. The real question is about the right place for these projects. A data center may make a lot of sense on industrial land near existing power lines and away from homes. It may make far less sense on city-owned land that residents want to keep open. Location is everything, and Boulder City residents are saying this particular location does not fit their town.

For homeowners across the valley, the lesson is bigger than one project. It is a reminder that what gets built near you affects your home's value and your daily quality of life. Traffic, noise, power reliability, and water supply all matter when you are deciding where to live. If you own in or near Boulder City, this outcome could affect your area for years. And if you are watching how Henderson, Clark County, and the state handle data centers, Boulder City is the case study to follow. The way this plays out could set the tone for the whole region.

What You Can Do

If you live in Boulder City and want a say, the most direct step is to follow the City Council schedule. Council meetings are open to the public, and residents can sign up to speak. The July meetings are the ones to watch, since the data center and the petition could come up then. You can check the Boulder City website for the agenda and meeting dates. Showing up, even just to listen, is one of the best ways to understand what is really being decided.

If you signed the petition, your job is mostly done, but you can still encourage neighbors to stay engaged through the certification and Council vote. If you have not signed and want to, check whether the deadline has passed and whether organizers are still collecting. Either way, you can contact your Council members directly to share your view. Local officials do read and count messages from residents, especially on a hot topic like this one.

For everyone else in the valley, the takeaway is simpler. Pay attention to the development plans near your own home. Sign up for your city or county planning notices. When a project comes up that affects your street, you will be glad you knew about it early.
