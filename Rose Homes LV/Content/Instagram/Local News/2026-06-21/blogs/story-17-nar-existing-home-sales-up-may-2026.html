NAR Reports U.S. Existing-Home Sales Up 3.2% in May

The National Association of Realtors (NAR) said existing-home sales rose 3.2% in May to a 4.17 million annualized pace. The national median sale price hit $429,300, up 1.3% from a year earlier, with 4.5 months of supply on the market. For Las Vegas buyers and sellers, the short version is this: national demand is ticking up a little, but our local market is still pricier and tighter than the country as a whole. The valley median sits closer to $490,000 with just 2.6 to 3.4 months of supply, which is well below the national figure.

If you are trying to decide whether to buy or sell this summer, that gap between the national story and the Las Vegas reality matters a lot. Below, I break down exactly what NAR reported, why it lands differently here in the valley, how we got to this point, and what I think it means for your next move.

What Happened

On its monthly report, NAR said existing-home sales climbed 3.2% in May compared with April. That pushed the seasonally adjusted annual rate to 4.17 million units. In plain language, an "annualized pace" is the number of homes that would sell over a full year if May's pace held steady for twelve months. It is a way to compare one month to another without getting tripped up by seasonal swings.

Existing-home sales count completed deals on previously owned single-family homes, townhomes, condos, and co-ops. They do not count brand-new construction, which NAR tracks separately. Existing homes make up the large majority of the market, so this report is one of the most watched housing numbers in the country.

NAR also reported that the national median existing-home price reached $429,300 in May. That is up 1.3% from the same month a year earlier. The median is the middle price, meaning half of homes sold for more and half sold for less. A 1.3% yearly gain is modest. It signals prices are still inching up, but the days of double-digit jumps are behind us for now.

It helps to separate two ideas that often get blurred together: sales volume and prices. Sales volume is how many homes change hands. Prices are how much those homes cost. In May, volume rose 3.2% while the median price rose only 1.3% over the year. That combination tells a useful story. More buyers are stepping back into the market, but they are not bidding prices sharply higher. That is a sign of a market finding its footing rather than overheating. For anyone who lived through the wild swings of past cycles, a steady, modest pace like this is actually healthy.

The report put national inventory at 4.5 months of supply. Months of supply tells you how long it would take to sell every home currently listed if no new homes came on the market and sales kept their current pace. A reading around five to six months is often called balanced, meaning it favors neither buyers nor sellers too heavily. At 4.5 months, the national market still leans a little toward sellers, but it is closer to balanced than it has been in a while.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Here is where the national headline and the Las Vegas reality split apart. Our valley median runs around $490,000, which is well above the national figure of $429,300. So if you read a national story saying homes cost about $429,000, you might walk into a Las Vegas open house and get sticker shock. The typical valley home costs roughly $60,000 more than the national middle price.

The supply picture is even more telling. Nationally, there are 4.5 months of supply. In the Las Vegas valley, supply runs much tighter, somewhere between 2.6 and 3.4 months. That difference is huge. When supply sits below about three months, sellers tend to hold more power, homes move faster, and buyers face stiffer competition for the listings that do come up. Las Vegas is firmly in that tighter zone, while the nation as a whole is drifting toward balance.

What does that mean for you in practical terms? If you are a buyer in Summerlin, Henderson, the southwest, or anywhere across the valley, you should not expect the breathing room that national headlines might suggest. Good homes priced right still attract multiple offers here. If you are a seller, the tight local supply works in your favor more than a national report would hint, because there is simply less competition on the market than in much of the country.

The modest national price gain of 1.3% is another reason to stay grounded in local numbers. Las Vegas does not always move in lockstep with the national median. Our valley has its own job growth, its own steady stream of new residents, and its own supply crunch. National data sets the backdrop, but the contract you sign is written in local terms.

There is also a payment side to all of this that buyers feel every day. With the national median at $429,300 and the valley median near $490,000, the gap is not just a price difference. It is a monthly payment difference too. On roughly $60,000 more in purchase price, the extra principal and interest can add a meaningful amount to a monthly budget, especially with rates near 6.5%. That is why I always tell valley buyers to run the actual numbers on a Las Vegas price point rather than a national one. The right home is the one you can comfortably carry every month, and that math starts with our local median.

For sellers, the tighter local supply has a real day-to-day effect on how a sale unfolds. Fewer competing listings can mean fewer price reductions, shorter time on market for well-presented homes, and stronger negotiating footing. It does not mean every home flies off the shelf at any price. Buyers are still careful at today's rates. But the supply backdrop here is friendlier to sellers than the national 4.5-month figure would lead you to believe.

Background and History

To understand why a 3.2% monthly bump is worth noting, it helps to remember where the housing market has been. After mortgage rates climbed sharply in 2022 and 2023, home sales slowed across the country. Many homeowners locked into low rates years ago and did not want to sell and trade up to a much higher rate. That kept inventory unusually low and kept a lot of would-be sellers sitting on the sidelines.

Las Vegas felt this shift the same way the rest of the country did, but our starting point was different. The valley has been a magnet for people moving in from higher-cost states for years. That steady demand, paired with limited land for new building in some areas, has kept a floor under prices even when sales activity cooled. So while sales volume dipped during the high-rate stretch, valley prices held up better than many expected.

NAR's monthly existing-home sales report has been a key gauge for decades. Realtors, lenders, builders, and economists all watch it to read the direction of the market. A single month does not make a trend, but a 3.2% rise after a long stretch of sluggish activity suggests buyers are slowly adjusting to today's rate environment. People who waited on the sidelines hoping for big rate cuts are starting to accept that life moves forward, jobs change, families grow, and homes still need to be bought and sold.

It is also worth remembering that the national median has been grinding higher in small steps rather than big leaps. The 1.3% yearly gain fits a pattern of prices staying firm without runaway growth. For Las Vegas, that backdrop of firm but calmer national prices, combined with our local supply crunch, is part of why valley values have stayed resilient.

Another piece of history worth keeping in mind is the so-called lock-in effect. When millions of homeowners hold mortgages at rates well below today's, they have a strong reason to stay put. Selling would mean giving up a cheap loan and taking on a more expensive one. That instinct has kept inventory unusually low for a few years now, both nationally and in the valley. A 3.2% rise in sales suggests that some of that frozen activity is beginning to thaw. Life events do not wait for perfect rates. People relocate for work, downsize after the kids leave, or upsize as families grow. Over time, those needs pull homes onto the market regardless of where rates sit.

What Happens Next

One month of stronger sales does not guarantee the trend continues. The biggest factor to watch is mortgage rates. Rates have been hovering near 6.5%, and that level shapes how many buyers can afford to act. If rates ease even a little, the pool of qualified buyers grows, and you could see sales pick up further. If rates climb, some of that improving demand could fade.

For Las Vegas specifically, keep an eye on local supply. Our 2.6 to 3.4 months of inventory is the number that really drives competition here. If more sellers decide to list this summer, supply could loosen a bit and give buyers slightly more room. If listings stay scarce, the tight conditions that favor sellers will continue. New construction also plays a role, since builders add to the supply that the existing-home report does not capture.

I expect the rest of 2026 to look a lot like what we are seeing now: steady demand, firm prices, and tight local supply. Barring a big surprise on rates or the job market, I do not see signs pointing toward a sharp drop in valley values. The more likely path is continued small moves, with Las Vegas staying pricier and tighter than the national average. That makes timing less about waiting for a crash and more about finding the right home at the right terms for your situation.

Ryan's Take

When clients show me a national headline and ask if it applies to their search, my honest answer is usually "partly." The NAR report is useful because it tells us the direction of demand across the country, and right now that direction is gently upward. But the numbers that will sit in your contract are local, and Las Vegas tells a tighter, pricier story than the national average.

I have watched plenty of buyers wait for the national median to "come down" to a number they read online, only to find that valley homes never matched that figure to begin with. Our median near $490,000 is the reality on the ground. I would rather have you plan around the real local number than chase a national average that does not reflect what is happening in our neighborhoods.

The piece I want sellers to hear is the supply gap. With local inventory sitting well below the national 4.5 months, you have less competition than a seller in many other markets. That is leverage, and it is easy to underestimate if you only read national coverage. The flip side for buyers is that you need to be ready to move when the right home appears, because the good ones do not sit long here.

My advice is the same one I give in any market: get your financing lined up, know your must-haves, and work with someone who reads the local data, not just the national headlines. The fundamentals in Las Vegas, steady population growth and limited supply, are not flashy, but they are the things that keep this market durable.

I also want to push back gently on the idea that you should try to perfectly time the market. Nobody, including me, can tell you the exact month that rates or prices will hit a low. What I can tell you is that a 3.2% national bump in sales shows other buyers are starting to move. Markets rarely send out an all-clear signal that everyone hears at once. By the time the news feels obviously good, competition has usually already heated up. If your finances are solid and you find a home that fits your life, the right time is often when the right home shows up, not when a headline gives you permission.

What You Can Do

If you are thinking about buying this summer, start by getting pre-approved with a lender so you know your real budget at today's rates near 6.5%. That step alone puts you ahead of buyers who are still guessing. Then focus your search on the neighborhoods that fit your life and your number, and be prepared to act quickly given our tight local supply.

If you are a seller, get a clear read on what your specific home is worth in today's valley market. National medians will not tell you that. A local price check that looks at recent sales in your area, your floor plan, and your condition will give you a realistic range and a smart pricing strategy. With inventory tight, well-prepared listings priced right are still drawing strong interest.

It also pays to think one level deeper than the valley average. Even within Las Vegas, conditions differ by neighborhood and price band. A starter home in the northwest can behave very differently from a luxury property in Summerlin or a condo near the Strip. The valley median near $490,000 and the 2.6 to 3.4 months of supply are helpful guideposts, but the market that matters most to you is the one in your zip code and your price range. Ask for data that narrows down to your actual search, not just the headline figures.

Whether you are buying or selling, the smartest move is to ground every decision in Las Vegas numbers rather than national ones. The national report is the weather forecast for the whole country. Your home sits in a specific neighborhood with its own conditions, and that is the data that should guide your next step.
