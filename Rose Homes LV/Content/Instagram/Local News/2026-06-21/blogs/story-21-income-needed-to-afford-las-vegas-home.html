It Takes $111,088 a Year to Afford a Las Vegas Home

If you want to buy a typical home in the Las Vegas Valley right now, you need to earn about $111,088 a year. That number comes from a Redfin study, and it assumes you put 20 percent down and borrow at a mortgage rate near 6.46 percent. In plain terms, a Las Vegas household has to bring in roughly six figures just to keep the monthly payment from eating too much of its paycheck. That is the income wall facing buyers today, and it is the single biggest reason so many renters feel stuck.

To see how far things have moved, look back about ten years. In December 2015, a household needed only about $41,701 to afford a typical valley home. The income required has nearly tripled since then. Wages have grown over that span, but nowhere close to that fast. This article breaks down what that $111,088 figure really means, why it landed where it did, and what local buyers can actually do about it.

What Happened

Redfin, a national real estate brokerage that tracks housing data, ran the math on what it takes to comfortably afford a median-priced home in major metro areas. For the Las Vegas Valley, the answer came back at $111,088 in yearly household income. That study was published in early 2026, and the figure still describes the affordability picture buyers face today.

The calculation rests on a few key assumptions. First, the buyer puts down 20 percent of the purchase price. Second, the mortgage rate sits at about 6.46 percent, which lines up with where 30-year fixed rates have hovered through 2026. Third, the buyer spends no more than 30 percent of gross income on the housing payment. That 30 percent line is the standard rule lenders and budget experts use to decide whether a home is affordable. Spend more than that, and money gets tight fast.

When Redfin plugged the valley's median home price into that formula, the required income came out to $111,088. The headline that grabbed attention was the comparison to a decade earlier. In December 2015, the same kind of calculation produced a figure of about $41,701. So the income needed to buy a typical Las Vegas home has climbed to nearly three times what it was ten years ago. That is a massive shift in a single decade.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

This number matters because it puts a hard dollar figure on something many valley families already feel. You may have a steady job, a decent income, and good credit, and still find that the math on a home purchase does not work. The $111,088 threshold explains why. For a large share of Las Vegas households, the income simply does not reach that bar.

Consider the local picture. The median household income in the Las Vegas area sits well below $111,088. That gap means a typical local family, earning a typical local wage, falls short of the income needed to comfortably buy a typical local home. When the median household cannot afford the median home, you have a real affordability squeeze. This is not just a problem for low earners. It reaches deep into the middle class.

The squeeze hits first-time buyers hardest. Move-up buyers often have equity from a home they already own, which they can roll into the down payment on the next place. First-time buyers have no such cushion. They have to come up with the full 20 percent down out of savings, then carry the monthly payment on their income alone. For a young couple or a single buyer trying to get into the market, the $111,088 wall can feel impossible to climb.

There is a ripple effect, too. When fewer households can afford to buy, more of them stay in rentals longer. That keeps demand for rental homes high even as more apartments come online. It also slows the natural turnover that usually feeds the market. People who would normally sell a starter home and move up stay put, which keeps the supply of affordable homes thin. Affordability problems at the entry level back up through the whole chain.

The number also shapes who can put down roots here. Las Vegas has long sold itself as a place where working families could own a home without the sky-high prices of coastal cities. That promise gets harder to keep when the income needed crosses six figures. Teachers, nurses, hospitality workers, and service employees form the backbone of the valley economy, and many of them earn well under $111,088. When the people who keep the city running cannot afford to buy in it, the long-term health of the community is at stake, not just the personal finances of individual buyers.

Background and History

To understand how the valley got here, it helps to walk through what changed since 2015. Three forces did most of the work: home prices, mortgage rates, and the down payment math that connects them.

Start with prices. Las Vegas home values have risen sharply over the past decade. The valley was still recovering from the housing crash in the early 2010s, and prices in 2015 were relatively low by today's standards. Then came years of strong population growth, a flood of new residents from higher-cost states like California, and a limited supply of land to build on. Most of the open land around the valley is owned by the federal government, which means builders cannot simply add lots whenever they want. Limited supply plus rising demand pushed prices up year after year.

Now add mortgage rates. Back in 2015, rates were low, and they fell even lower during the pandemic, dipping below 3 percent at one point. Cheap money let buyers afford larger loans on smaller incomes. But starting in 2022, the Federal Reserve raised rates to fight inflation, and mortgage rates climbed with them. By 2026 the 30-year fixed sat around 6.46 percent. A higher rate means a bigger monthly payment on the very same loan amount. That alone pushed the income needed to qualify way up.

The two forces stack on top of each other. Higher prices mean a bigger loan. Higher rates mean a bigger payment on that loan. And the 20 percent down assumption ties them together. On a more expensive home, 20 percent down is a larger pile of cash. So buyers need more savings to get in the door and more income to carry the payment once they do. That is the full recipe behind the jump from $41,701 to $111,088.

It is worth being clear about the timing. The Redfin study that produced the $111,088 figure was published in early 2026. Housing numbers move around month to month, so the exact figure can drift a little as prices and rates shift. But the broad story it tells, that affording a valley home now takes roughly six figures and nearly triple what it took ten years ago, remains the affordability reality buyers are living with right now.

What Happens Next

So where does affordability go from here? A few things will shape the answer over the next year or two.

The first is mortgage rates. Most forecasters expect rates to stay near 6.5 percent through 2026, with no big drop on the horizon. The Federal Reserve has held rates steady at recent meetings rather than cutting them. If rates do ease later, the income needed to afford a home would fall, because a lower rate trims the monthly payment. Even a half-point drop would help. But buyers should not count on relief that may not come. Planning around a rate near 6.5 percent is the safer bet.

The second is home prices. Las Vegas prices have leveled off compared to the rapid run-ups of earlier years, helped by a market with more homes for sale and buyers who are stretched. At the same time, the valley keeps growing. Nevada was recently named the fastest-growing state in the country by one measure, and steady job gains keep pulling new residents in. That ongoing demand puts a floor under prices, so a sharp drop is unlikely. The more probable path is prices holding roughly flat or rising slowly, which keeps affordability tight rather than easing it much.

The third is wages. Local incomes have been growing, and the Las Vegas job market has been adding jobs. If wages keep climbing while prices and rates hold steady, the affordability gap could narrow over time. That is the slow, grinding way affordability tends to improve when prices do not fall. It will not happen overnight, but a stronger local economy is the most realistic path toward closing the gap between what households earn and what homes cost.

Ryan's Take

I work with valley buyers every week, and I want to be straight with you about this number. The $111,088 figure is real, and it is a useful gut check. But it is not a locked gate. It is an average built on a strict set of assumptions, and your situation may look very different from the average.

Here is what I mean. That figure assumes a median home and 20 percent down. Plenty of buyers do not need a median home. A condo, a townhome, or a smaller house in an outer part of the valley can cost well under the median, which lowers the income you need. And almost nobody is required to put down a full 20 percent. There are loan programs that let qualified buyers put down far less. A smaller down payment changes the savings math entirely, even if it adds a bit to the monthly cost.

I also see buyers get discouraged by a single headline and stop looking, which is the worst outcome. The truth is that affordability is personal. It depends on your income, your debts, your credit, your savings, and the specific home you target. Two people with the same paycheck can have very different buying power based on those factors. The only way to know your real number is to sit down and run it for your own life, not for some statewide average.

My honest read on the market is that it favors patient, prepared buyers right now. Prices are not soaring the way they were a few years back. There are more homes to choose from. Sellers are more willing to negotiate. If you do the prep work, get your finances in order, and stay realistic about the type of home that fits your budget, this can actually be a reasonable time to buy. The income wall is high, but it is not the whole story.

One more thing I tell every buyer who feels priced out: do not wait for the perfect moment that never arrives. People spend years waiting for rates to crash or prices to tumble, and meanwhile they pay rent that builds someone else's equity instead of their own. The smarter move is to buy what you can afford today, build equity, and refinance later if rates fall. You marry the home and date the rate, as the saying goes. The home you can afford now is almost always a better long-term bet than the home you keep waiting for.

What You Can Do

If a six-figure income wall feels far away, do not give up on owning a home. There are concrete steps that move you closer, and several of them shrink the number you actually need.

Start by getting a real picture of your buying power. Talk to a lender and get pre-approved. Pre-approval tells you the actual loan amount you qualify for, based on your income, debts, and credit, rather than a generic average. You may find you can afford more than you thought, or you may learn exactly what to fix first. Either way, you are dealing in facts instead of fear.

Next, look hard at down payment assistance. Nevada's Home Is Possible program can provide help with the down payment and closing costs for qualified buyers, which directly attacks the biggest barrier for first-time buyers, the cash needed up front. There are also loan types that allow much lower down payments than 20 percent. Lowering the down payment lowers the savings you need to get started, even though it may raise the monthly payment a little. For many buyers, that trade is worth it.

Then, broaden where and what you look at. The median home is just the midpoint. Below it sit thousands of homes that cost less and require less income to afford. Consider condos and townhomes, look at neighborhoods on the edges of the valley, and stay open on size. A smaller first home that you can actually afford beats a dream home you cannot. You can always move up later as your income grows and you build equity.

Finally, work on the pieces of your finances that lenders care about. Pay down high-interest debt, since lower debt payments free up room in the 30 percent rule. Keep building your credit score, because a better score can earn you a lower mortgage rate, and a lower rate cuts the income you need. Small improvements in these areas add up to real buying power over time.
