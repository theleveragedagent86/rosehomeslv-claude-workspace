Trustees Hear Student Safety Perceptions Rise to 83 Percent

More Clark County students say they feel safe at school than at any point in recent years. At the June 11, 2026 meeting of the CCSD Board of School Trustees, a district presentation showed that 83 percent of students reported feeling safe. That is up 4 points since the 2022-23 school year. The number comes from more than 156,000 responses to the 2025-26 Districtwide Survey. District leaders credited the new single points of entry now in place at every middle school and high school as a big reason for the jump.

That is the short version. If you are a parent or a buyer trying to figure out what this means for your family and your home, the rest of this article walks through the details, the history behind it, and how school safety quietly shapes where families choose to live in the Las Vegas valley.

What Happened

During the regular meeting of the Clark County School District Board of School Trustees on June 11, 2026, staff gave trustees a presentation on the results of the 2025-26 Districtwide Survey. The survey asks students, parents, and staff a range of questions about their school experience. One of the headline findings was about safety.

According to the presentation, 83 percent of students who took the survey said they feel safe at school. That figure is up 4 percentage points compared with the 2022-23 school year. To put that in plain terms, a larger share of students across the district now report a basic sense of security during the school day than they did three years ago.

The result is not based on a small sample. The district reported more than 156,000 responses to the survey. In a district the size of CCSD, which is one of the largest school systems in the entire country, that is a meaningful pool of feedback. A response count that high gives the numbers more weight than a quick poll of a single campus would.

When district staff explained why the perception of safety has climbed, they pointed to one change in particular. CCSD has rolled out single points of entry at all middle schools and high schools. A single point of entry means that during the school day, visitors and students enter and exit through one controlled, monitored door rather than through many open doors scattered around a campus. That setup makes it easier for staff to see who is coming and going and to keep unauthorized people out.

The trustees heard this as part of a broader update on district operations and climate. The takeaway the board was given was straightforward. Investments in physical security appear to line up with how students feel about their own safety.

It is also helpful to understand what a single point of entry looks like in practice. On a typical secondary campus, the main entrance is staffed, and visitors are buzzed in or checked through one monitored doorway. Other exterior doors are locked from the outside during class hours, even though they can still be used to exit in an emergency. The goal is not to make a school feel like a fortress. It is to make sure that the people inside the building during the day are the people who are supposed to be there. For many parents, that single change addresses one of their most basic worries about dropping their child off each morning.

The 4-point gain may sound modest at first glance, but in a district with hundreds of thousands of students, a few points represents a very large number of young people who feel better about their school day than they did a few years ago. Small shifts at that scale are not easy to produce, which is part of why district leaders highlighted the figure for trustees.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

If you own a home in Clark County, rent here, or plan to buy, school safety is not just an education story. It is a housing story too. For families with kids, and even for buyers who do not have children, the reputation and feel of local schools plays a quiet but powerful role in where people choose to put down roots.

Here in the valley, CCSD is the school district for nearly the entire metro area. Las Vegas, Henderson, North Las Vegas, Summerlin, Spring Valley, Enterprise, and the rest of the populated parts of the county all fall under one giant district. Because there is essentially one district covering everyone, the way families compare neighborhoods often comes down to the specific schools that serve a given home, plus the overall sense that the district is safe and improving.

When a parent tours a neighborhood, they are usually asking two questions at once. Is this a home I want? And is this a place I trust to send my kids every morning? A district-wide statistic like 83 percent feeling safe, up from a few years ago, helps answer that second question in a positive direction. Numbers that move the right way build confidence. Confidence keeps families willing to buy in established neighborhoods rather than looking elsewhere.

This matters for home values too. Areas tied to schools that feel safe and well run tend to hold demand. Strong demand supports steady prices over time. When families feel uneasy about safety, that worry can show up as softer interest in certain pockets, longer days on market, and more price sensitivity. So a piece of news that sounds like it belongs only on the education page can ripple into the real estate market in very real ways.

It is also worth being clear about what this number is and is not. It is a perception measure. It tells us how students feel, which is genuinely important, because a student who feels safe is more able to focus and learn. It is not the same as a hard count of incidents at every campus. A smart buyer uses both kinds of information. The district-wide trend gives you the big picture. School-level details and a visit in person give you the local picture for the exact home you are considering.

There is one more angle worth mentioning for anyone thinking about resale down the road. Buyers tend to research schools, and they form impressions from the broad reputation of the district as much as from any single statistic. A district that is visibly improving on safety builds a reputation that follows it. That reputation can help a seller in a few years just as much as it helps a buyer today. Real estate is a long game, and the slow build of trust in local schools is part of what keeps a community desirable over time.

Families relocating to Las Vegas from other parts of the country often ask me directly how the schools are. They have read headlines, sometimes good and sometimes alarming, and they want a grounded answer. Being able to point to a measured, survey-based trend that is moving in the right direction gives those conversations a fact to stand on instead of a rumor or a single bad story.

Background and History

To understand why an 83 percent safety figure is being celebrated, it helps to remember where the conversation started. School safety has been a front-and-center topic for CCSD for several years. Like many large districts across the country, Clark County has faced public pressure to show that campuses are secure and that students and staff are protected during the school day.

The 2022-23 baseline that this new number is compared against was a period when the district was still working through a long list of safety priorities. Over the years that followed, CCSD made physical security upgrades a clear focus. The single point of entry program is one of the most visible parts of that effort. Instead of campuses with many doors that could be propped open or left unwatched, the goal has been to funnel daytime access through one staffed and monitored entrance at each secondary school.

The Districtwide Survey itself has become a regular tool for measuring how the community feels. By asking students, families, and staff the same kinds of questions year after year, the district can track whether things are getting better or worse over time. A survey is only as useful as the number of people who respond to it, which is why the 156,000-plus responses this cycle matter. A large response pool makes it harder to dismiss the results as an outlier.

It is fair to note that perception data has a history of being debated. Some community members point out that feeling safe and being safe are not always the same thing, and that a single district-wide number can hide big differences between individual campuses. Those are reasonable points. They are also a good reminder that a positive trend is encouraging news but not the final word. The district reporting an upward move on a closely watched measure, backed by a concrete security change, is the kind of progress trustees and families have been hoping to see.

What Happens Next

The presentation to trustees was an update, not a finish line. Expect CCSD to keep tracking the safety question in future rounds of the Districtwide Survey. If the 83 percent figure holds or climbs again next year, the district will likely point to that as proof its approach is working. If it slips, that will prompt new questions about what changed.

The single point of entry program will probably remain a talking point. Now that the setup is in place at all middle and high schools, attention may turn to how consistently it is used day to day, how it is handled at the elementary level, and whether the district pairs it with other measures such as staffing, mental health support, and bullying prevention. Physical security is one layer of safety. How students treat one another is another layer entirely, and surveys often capture both.

Budget season is another place to watch. Security spending costs money, and trustees regularly weigh it against other needs. A survey result that shows safety perceptions improving gives the district evidence to defend that spending. Parents and taxpayers who follow board meetings can look for how safety dollars are discussed in upcoming budgets and whether the data continues to support those choices.

For families, the practical next step is simple. Pay attention to the schools that serve the specific homes and neighborhoods you care about. District-wide trends set the backdrop. The campus your child would actually attend is what affects your daily life. Both deserve a look.

What You Can Do

If you are a parent or a buyer, there are a few easy ways to put this news to work. Start by finding out which schools serve any home you are considering. School assignments are tied to address, so two houses a few minutes apart can feed into different campuses. Knowing the exact assigned schools is step one.

Next, look up how those specific schools are doing and read any recent updates from the district about them. Pair that with a visit if you can. Seeing a campus during the school day and watching how arrival and dismissal are handled will tell you a lot about how the single point of entry approach plays out in real life.

If you already own here, stay engaged. CCSD board meetings are open to the public, and the Districtwide Survey results are shared so the community can see them. Following how the district reports safety over time helps you understand the trend in your own backyard, which is useful whether you plan to stay put or sell down the road.
