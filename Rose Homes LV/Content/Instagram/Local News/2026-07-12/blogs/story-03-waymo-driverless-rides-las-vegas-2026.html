Waymo Is Bringing Fully Driverless Rides to Las Vegas, Starting With Employee Trips First

Waymo is bringing cars with no human behind the wheel to Las Vegas streets. On Wednesday, July 8, 2026, the company announced it will begin fully autonomous driving in the greater Las Vegas area, including the Las Vegas Strip. That means a real car, driving itself, with no safety driver sitting up front.

This is a big moment for the valley. Self-driving cars have been tested here for years, but full driverless service for the public is a different thing entirely. Soon you may look over at the car next to you on the freeway and see an empty driver's seat. Here is what is happening, how it will work, and what it could mean for how you get around town.

What Happened

Waymo, the self-driving car company owned by Google's parent company Alphabet, made the announcement on July 8, 2026. The company said it will soon start fully autonomous driving in Las Vegas and three other new cities. In each of these places, the cars will drive with no human behind the wheel. FOX5 first reported the news, which was confirmed in Waymo's own announcement that same day.

The rollout will happen in steps. First, Waymo will offer rider-only trips to its own employees across the greater Las Vegas area, including the Strip. These are trips where a person rides in the car but no one is driving it. After that testing phase, the company plans to open the service to the public in the coming months. Waymo did not give an exact date for when regular Las Vegas residents will be able to book a ride.

Las Vegas riders will be among the first anywhere to try the Waymo Ojai, the company's newest vehicle. Waymo describes the Ojai as "an oasis on wheels," with small comforts built into the car. It is designed to make the ride feel calm and easy for passengers who no longer have to think about driving.

Waymo said it has visited Las Vegas many times over the years for research and testing. The company said its self-driving system, which it calls the Waymo Driver, has learned to handle the city and its unique streets. Waymo pointed to a safety study that looked at more than 220 million fully autonomous miles driven through the end of March 2026. Compared to human drivers, the study found Waymo cars were involved in 94% fewer crashes that caused serious or deadly injuries, 82% fewer crashes where an airbag went off, and 82% fewer crashes with any reported injury.

The company also said its approach is what lets it grow faster than before. Waymo described an operational plan focused on going fully autonomous in a reliable way, paired with what it calls a community-first approach built on its safety record. In plain terms, the company says it has reached a point where it trusts the technology enough to pull the human safety driver out of the front seat and still scale the service to new cities. Las Vegas is one of four new markets in this latest expansion, which puts the valley in the same group of early cities that get the newest technology first.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

For millions of people who live in and visit Clark County, this changes the picture of getting around. Las Vegas is a car town. Most families here own at least one vehicle, and the valley spreads out across Summerlin, Henderson, North Las Vegas, Spring Valley, and beyond. A reliable driverless ride service could give people another way to move around without needing to drive themselves or find parking on the Strip.

Think about the everyday uses. A night out downtown or on the Strip could end with a driverless ride home instead of a risky drive after drinks. Older residents who no longer feel safe driving could keep their independence. Teens, tourists, and people without a car could get around parts of the valley more easily. For a city built on 24-hour activity, a service that runs around the clock without a tired human driver has real appeal.

There is also the safety angle, which hits home in this valley. Clark County has struggled with a high number of traffic deaths and pedestrian crashes for years. If the safety numbers Waymo shared hold up on local streets, more driverless cars could mean fewer serious crashes over time. That is a big "if," and it will take real-world results here to prove it, but it is the promise that has regulators and residents paying attention.

Not everyone is cheering. This shift also raises worries about jobs. Las Vegas has thousands of professional drivers, from taxi and rideshare drivers to limo and shuttle operators. Many of them support families here. As more driverless services roll out, some of those jobs could be at risk, and that is a real concern for a lot of households in the valley.

There is a tourism angle too, and in Las Vegas tourism touches almost everything. The Strip draws tens of millions of visitors every year, and many of them do not have a car while they are here. A visible fleet of driverless cars could become part of the Las Vegas experience itself, another futuristic draw in a city that markets itself on being ahead of the curve. That matters to residents because a strong visitor economy supports jobs, tax revenue, and the local housing demand that keeps our neighborhoods active.

For homeowners, the long-term question is what all of this does to daily convenience. If a driverless ride becomes as easy to grab as pulling up an app, some households may decide they do not need a second or third car. That could free up money that families currently spend on car payments, insurance, and gas. In a valley where the median home price sits in the high four hundred thousands, every bit of monthly savings can matter to a buyer trying to qualify for a mortgage.

Background and History

Waymo did not just show up overnight. The company demonstrated its self-driving technology in Las Vegas back in January 2026, ahead of these public rides. Before that, it spent years quietly testing on valley roads, learning how its cars handle the Strip, the freeways, and the wide surface streets that make up so much of the metro area.

Waymo is also not the first driverless company to target Las Vegas. Earlier in 2026, two other robotaxi companies made moves here. Zoox announced a partnership with Uber in March, and Motional launched a commercial robotaxi service with Uber around the same time. That means Las Vegas has become one of the key testing grounds for this technology in the whole country. The valley's wide roads, sunny weather, and steady flow of visitors make it an attractive place to prove out self-driving cars.

The road has not been perfectly smooth for Waymo. In May 2026, the company issued a voluntary software recall after one of its cars, with no people inside, drove into flood waters in Texas. Waymo said it made changes after that incident. Moments like that are a reminder that the technology is still new and still learning, even as it expands into more cities.

At the same time, the debate over rules and worker protections has been building in Nevada. In April 2026, local union leaders with the Teamsters pushed the state to take action on automation and artificial intelligence. They want more regulations on autonomous vehicles and protections for workers who could lose jobs as these services grow. That tension between fast-moving technology and the people whose livelihoods depend on driving is now front and center in the valley.

It helps to remember how quickly this all came together. Just a few years ago, a self-driving car on a Las Vegas street was a novelty you might see once and talk about for a week. Now three major companies are competing to put driverless cars in front of local riders. Waymo joining Zoox and Motional means the valley is no longer testing the idea of robotaxis. It is testing which companies and which approaches actually work at scale in a real, busy city.

What Happens Next

The first phase is already underway. Waymo will run rider-only trips with its own employees across the greater Las Vegas area, including the Strip. This lets the company gather data and fine-tune the service before opening it up to everyone. The exact date when regular members of the public can book a Waymo ride in Las Vegas remains unclear, but the company said it plans to open the service in the coming months.

When it does open, riders will likely book trips through the Waymo One app, the same way people use other rideshare apps. Keep an eye out for the Waymo Ojai, the new vehicle that Las Vegas riders will be among the first to experience. As the service grows, watch for how far it reaches beyond the Strip and into neighborhoods across Henderson, Summerlin, and the rest of the valley.

The other thing to watch is the policy fight. State lawmakers and local leaders are weighing how to regulate autonomous vehicles, and pressure from labor groups is not going away. New rules could shape how fast these services expand, where they can operate, and what protections exist for drivers whose jobs are affected. The next several months will tell us a lot about how driverless cars fit into daily life in Clark County.

Watch how residents react on the street too. Las Vegas is a city that films everything and shares it, so expect plenty of videos of empty driver seats rolling down the Strip and through neighborhoods. Some of that reaction will be excitement, and some will be nerves. How comfortable everyday drivers, cyclists, and pedestrians feel sharing the road with these cars will play a big role in how welcome the service becomes. Public trust is not something Waymo can announce. It has to earn it here, one safe trip at a time.

Ryan's Take

Transportation and real estate are tied together more than people think. When it gets easier to move around a city, neighborhoods that once felt far out can suddenly feel a lot closer. Driverless rides could, over time, make some outlying communities more appealing to buyers who do not want to fight traffic or worry about a long commute.

I am watching this with an open mind. The safety numbers Waymo shared are impressive on paper, and fewer serious crashes would be a genuine win for a valley that has lost too many people on its roads. At the same time, I do not take those claims as the final word until we see how these cars perform on our streets over time. I also feel for the local drivers whose jobs could be on the line. My honest take is that this technology is coming whether we are ready or not, so the smart move for Las Vegas is to shape it with good rules rather than just react to it.

The part that interests me most as a real estate professional is what easy, reliable rides do to the map. For decades, a home's value has been tied to its location and its commute. If getting around becomes cheaper and simpler, the gap between a central neighborhood and a further-out community like parts of North Las Vegas, the far southwest, or the outer edges of Henderson could shrink. I am not predicting a boom in any one area, and I would never tell a client to buy a home just because a robotaxi might drive by someday. But convenience shapes demand, and demand shapes prices. This is a trend worth watching closely as it plays out over the next few years.
