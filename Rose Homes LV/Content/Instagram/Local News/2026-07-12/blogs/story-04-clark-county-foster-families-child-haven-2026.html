Clark County Urgently Seeks Foster Families as Child Haven Reaches Capacity

Clark County put out an urgent call this week for more foster families after Child Haven, the county's emergency shelter for kids, filled up. County officials say too many children are coming into care and there are not enough homes to place them, so they are asking valley residents to step up and consider fostering.

This is not a small ask, and it is not a quiet one. When the county's own children's campus runs out of room, that means real kids are sleeping in a shelter instead of a home. The county wants families across Las Vegas, Henderson, North Las Vegas, and the rest of Clark County to learn what fostering takes and to think about opening their doors.

What Happened

On July 10, 2026, Clark County announced that Child Haven had reached capacity. Child Haven is the county-run campus that serves as a temporary safe place for children who have been removed from their homes. Kids end up there when they cannot stay with their parents because of abuse, neglect, or a family crisis. It is meant to be a short stop, not a place where children live for weeks or months.

The problem is simple to explain and hard to solve. More children are entering the system than there are foster homes to receive them. When homes run short, kids stack up at Child Haven. That is exactly what pushed the campus to capacity and led the county's Department of Family Services to sound the alarm.

County officials framed it as a direct plea to the community. They are asking everyday residents to consider becoming licensed foster parents. They are also asking people who cannot foster to help in other ways, whether that means donating, mentoring, or spreading the word so more families sign up. The message was that the county cannot fix this alone.

Clark County's Department of Family Services runs the local foster care and child welfare programs. The agency licenses foster homes, screens families, and works to place children with relatives when possible. When a relative placement is not available and no foster home has an open bed, Child Haven becomes the fallback. That is why a full shelter is treated as an emergency and not just a scheduling headache.

Summer tends to make the strain worse. When school is out, teachers and counselors who often spot signs of neglect are not seeing kids every day, and cases can pile up in different ways once families are under the heat and the pressure of the season. Add in the fact that some longtime foster families take a break over the summer, and the timing of this shortage is not a surprise to the people who work in the system. The county chose to go public because quiet recruitment was not filling the gap fast enough.

The children who come through Child Haven range from infants to teenagers. Some stay only a night or two before a relative or foster home is found. Others wait much longer, especially older kids and sibling groups who need to stay together. Those larger placements are the hardest to make, because most homes have room for one child, not three or four brothers and sisters. When the county asks for help, homes that can take siblings and older kids are in especially short supply.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

This story hits home for a lot of valley families because it is about kids who live right here, in our own neighborhoods. The children at Child Haven are from Las Vegas, Henderson, North Las Vegas, Spring Valley, and every corner of Clark County. They go to CCSD schools. They play at the same parks your kids do. When the shelter is full, that is a local problem with local faces.

For homeowners and renters alike, fostering is one of the most direct ways to help a child in your own community. You do not need a mansion. You need a safe, stable home with room for a child and the willingness to care for one. Many people assume they do not qualify, and that assumption keeps homes empty while kids wait. Single people can foster. Renters can foster. Working parents can foster.

There is also a practical side that matters to families thinking about their budget. Foster parents in Nevada receive a monthly reimbursement to help cover the cost of caring for a child. The payment is meant to offset food, clothing, and basic needs, not to be an income. Still, it means fostering is not something only wealthy households can consider. That opens the door to a much wider group of valley residents.

Even if you never foster, this affects the community you live in. Kids who move through stable foster homes tend to do better in school and stay out of deeper trouble. Kids who bounce between shelters and short placements often struggle. Stronger foster care means stronger neighborhoods over time, and that is something every resident has a stake in.

Location matters more than most people realize, too. When a child is placed with a family close to their old neighborhood, they can often stay at the same CCSD school and keep the same friends, teachers, and routines. That stability makes a hard situation a little less scary. So a family in Henderson who fosters helps keep a Henderson kid rooted in Henderson. A family in the northwest helps a northwest kid stay near home. Every part of the valley needs homes, not just one zip code.

For parents already raising kids, fostering can also be a powerful lesson for your own household. Children who grow up watching their family open the door to a kid in need learn empathy in a way no classroom can teach. Many foster families say their biological children became more grateful, more patient, and more aware of the world once a foster sibling joined the home. It is not always easy, but it can shape a family in lasting ways.

Background and History

Child Haven sits on a campus near the heart of the valley and has served as Clark County's emergency children's shelter for years. It was built to be a safety net, a place a child can go the same day they are removed from a dangerous home. The goal has always been to move kids out of the shelter and into a family setting as fast as possible, because a home beats a facility every time.

Foster home shortages are not new in Clark County, and they are not unique to Las Vegas. Communities across Nevada and the country have struggled to recruit and keep enough licensed foster families. The need tends to rise faster than the supply of homes. When that gap widens, shelters like Child Haven take on more kids than they were designed to hold comfortably.

Several forces feed the shortage. Some longtime foster families age out or step back after years of service. Others adopt the children in their care and then take a break. Recruiting new families takes time because there is training, paperwork, background checks, and a home study before anyone can be licensed. That pipeline cannot be filled overnight, which is why the county pushes hard when the numbers get tight.

Clark County has run recruitment campaigns before, often around the summer months and the holidays when needs spike. This July call is part of that ongoing effort, but the fact that Child Haven actually reached capacity gives it real urgency. It is one thing to say more homes are needed. It is another to say the shelter has no more room.

It is worth remembering how fast the valley has grown, too. Clark County is now home to well over two million people, and the child welfare system has to keep pace with all of that growth. More residents means more families, and more families means more kids who, through no fault of their own, sometimes end up needing a safe place to land. Recruitment of foster homes has simply not kept up with the population, and shortages like this one are the result. That is the backdrop behind every one of these urgent calls for help.

What Happens Next

The county will keep pushing recruitment while it works to place the children already in care. Interested residents can reach out to Clark County's Department of Family Services to start the process. That first step usually means attending an information session or orientation, where staff explain what fostering involves and answer questions before anyone commits to anything.

After that, families who want to move forward go through training, a background check, and a home study. The home study is a walk-through and interview process that makes sure the home is safe and the family is ready. Licensing takes some weeks, so the sooner people start, the sooner a bed opens up for a child who needs one. The county is counting on a steady stream of new applicants, not just a one-time bump.

Watch for the county to keep this in the spotlight through the summer. Expect more information sessions, more community outreach, and likely partnerships with local nonprofits that support foster families. If enough valley residents respond, the pressure on Child Haven eases. If the response is thin, the shelter stays crowded and the call for help will only get louder.

It also helps to know that becoming a foster parent does not lock you into anything permanent. Families choose the age range and the number of children they feel ready for. You can foster one child and see how it goes. You can say yes to a placement and, if it is not the right fit for your household, work with the county to find a better match. The system is built with the expectation that families need flexibility, not an all-or-nothing commitment. That flexibility is worth understanding before anyone rules themselves out.

Keep an eye on the numbers, too. If Child Haven stays at or near capacity into August, expect the county and the school district to work more closely as classes resume and more cases surface. A crowded shelter in the summer can turn into an even bigger challenge in the fall. The best outcome is a wave of new applicants now, so licensed homes are ready before the next surge of need arrives.

Ryan's Take

I spend my days helping people find a house, so a story about kids who do not have a stable place to sleep really lands for me. A home is more than an address or a number on a listing. It is safety, routine, and a door that locks behind you at night. When the county says its shelter is full, that means kids in our valley are missing exactly the thing I help families secure every week.

Here is what I would tell my neighbors. You do not have to buy a bigger house or be a perfect parent to make a difference for one of these kids. A spare bedroom, a steady schedule, and a willing heart go a long way. Las Vegas has grown fast, and with growth comes more families and more kids who need support. The strength of a community is not just measured by home prices or new development. It is measured by how we take care of the people who live here, especially the youngest ones. If a fraction of the families I work with looked into fostering, Child Haven would not be full.

What You Can Do

If you have ever thought about fostering, this is the moment to learn more. Start by contacting Clark County's Department of Family Services and asking about the next foster parent information session. There is no obligation to sign up just for showing up and learning. You will get real answers about the requirements, the support available, and what daily life as a foster family looks like in the valley.

Not everyone can foster, and that is okay. There are still ways to help. You can donate items like clothing, diapers, and school supplies that support kids in care. You can help spread the word by sharing this with friends, family, and coworkers who might be a good fit. Sometimes the right family just needs one person to nudge them and say, "You should look into this." Local nonprofits that serve foster youth also welcome volunteers and mentors.

If you are a homeowner with a spare room and a stable household, you are exactly the kind of person the county is hoping to reach. Talk it over with your family, ask your questions, and take that first step. One home really can change the course of a child's life, and right now Clark County needs as many of those homes as it can get.

If fostering is not the right fit but you still want to make a difference in the housing side of a child's life, there are related ways to help. Some valley residents open their homes to relatives' children through what is called kinship care, which keeps kids inside the family. Others support young adults aging out of foster care, who often face housing insecurity the moment they turn eighteen. Programs across Clark County help these young people find a first apartment and learn to live on their own, and they always need mentors and community backing.

Whatever path fits your household, the through-line is the same. Kids do best when they have a stable place to call home. That belief is at the center of what I do for a living, and it is why this story matters far beyond one crowded shelter. A safe home is the foundation everything else in a child's life gets built on. Clark County is asking, and a lot of good people in this valley have the room and the heart to answer.
