California Drives Nearly a Third of Out-of-Market Demand for Las Vegas Homes, and LA Leads It

Southern California and the Bay Area now account for close to one third of every out-of-town home search in the Las Vegas Valley. A new report from Realtor.com found that the Los Angeles metro alone made up 23.7 percent of all outside-the-valley views of Las Vegas homes in the first quarter of 2026, and the San Jose metro added another 8.5 percent. Put those two together and you get about 32 percent of all the interest coming from beyond our borders.

In plain terms, when someone from another city is shopping for a home here, there is roughly a one in three chance they live in Southern California or the Bay Area. That single fact shapes prices, competition, and how fast homes sell across Henderson, Summerlin, the southwest, and North Las Vegas. If you are buying or selling here, you are not just competing with your neighbors. You are competing with a steady stream of buyers driving in on Interstate 15.

What the Realtor.com Report Found

The report came from Realtor.com, the online real estate marketplace, and it landed in the Las Vegas Review-Journal on July 7, 2026. It looked at where people were searching for Las Vegas Valley homes during the first quarter of the year. The numbers tell a clear story about who wants to live here.

The Los Angeles, Long Beach, and Anaheim metro made up 23.7 percent of all views of Las Vegas homes that came from outside the valley. The San Jose, Sunnyvale, and Santa Clara metro came in second at 8.5 percent. No other market came close. Anthony Smith, a senior economist for Realtor.com, told the Review-Journal that Los Angeles and San Jose combined generate more demand than the next eight metros combined. That is a striking level of concentration from just two California regions.

The report also broke down where all the searches for Las Vegas homes come from. About 57 percent of views came from other states. Around 38 percent came from within the metro itself. About 2.9 percent came from international searchers, and roughly 1 percent came from within Nevada but outside the valley. In other words, more than half the eyes on our housing market belong to people who do not live in Nevada at all.

Smith also pointed out something important about the flow going the other way. When Las Vegas residents search for homes in other cities, their interest is spread out. The top outbound destination was the Phoenix, Mesa, and Chandler metro at 6.1 percent of views. Next came the Riverside, San Bernardino, and Ontario area in California at 4.3 percent, then Tucson, Arizona at 4.2 percent. As Smith put it, outbound demand from Las Vegas is far more dispersed than the concentrated inflow from Southern California and the Bay Area. People want in here far more than locals want out.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

This report is not just a fun fact for a dinner party. It touches your wallet whether you own, rent, or are trying to buy your first place. When a big share of demand comes from higher-cost markets like Los Angeles and San Jose, it puts steady pressure under home prices here. Buyers coming from those cities often see our prices as a bargain, and that mindset keeps the market moving even when local wages have not caught up.

Think about the math a California buyer runs. The median single-family home in Las Vegas is sitting near a record $490,000 right now. In many parts of Los Angeles or the Bay Area, that same amount might buy a small condo or nothing at all. So a family selling a modest home in California can walk into the valley with real cash, buy a bigger house, and still have money left over. That gives them an edge over a local buyer who is stretching to make a payment.

For sellers, this is good news. A larger pool of motivated, well-funded buyers means your home is more likely to sell and less likely to sit. For buyers who already live here, it is a tougher spot. You may find yourself bidding against someone who just cashed out of a pricier market and can move faster or offer more. That is the double edge of being a place everyone wants to move to.

Renters feel it too. When outside buyers snap up homes, some turn them into rentals, and strong demand for housing keeps upward pressure on rents. It also affects the pace of new building. Builders in areas like the southwest, North Las Vegas, and the far northwest keep breaking ground in part because they know the buyers are there, including plenty coming from out of state.

Background and History

The California-to-Nevada move is not new. People have been trading the coast for the desert for decades, and the reasons rarely change. Nevada has no state income tax. California has one of the highest in the country. For a retiree on a fixed income or a remote worker with a good salary, that difference alone can be worth thousands of dollars a year. Add lower home prices, and the pitch almost sells itself.

The pandemic years poured fuel on this trend. Remote work untied millions of people from their offices. Suddenly a software worker in San Jose could keep a Bay Area paycheck and live in Summerlin or Henderson for a fraction of the housing cost. Las Vegas became a natural landing spot because it is close, it is warm, and it has an airport that gets you back to California in about an hour when you need to be there in person.

Realtor.com has tracked this migration for years, and Las Vegas keeps showing up near the top of the lists. Earlier in 2026, a separate Redfin report ranked Las Vegas as the fifth most-searched housing market in the entire country. That national interest and this California-heavy demand are two sides of the same coin. The valley has become one of the most watched housing markets in America, and the people watching most closely live just across the state line.

What Happens Next

Expect the California pipeline to stay strong through the rest of 2026. Nothing in the data points to a sudden slowdown. As long as Nevada keeps its tax advantage and homes here cost far less than they do on the coast, buyers will keep pointing their moving trucks east. The Realtor.com numbers are a first-quarter snapshot, and the same pattern has held for years, so it is reasonable to think it holds through the summer buying season and beyond.

Watch a few things in the months ahead. First, watch mortgage rates. They recently ticked up to about 6.49 percent. Higher rates cool demand a bit, but cash-rich California sellers are less sensitive to rates because many pay in cash or put down large amounts. Second, watch inventory. The valley has moved toward a more balanced market with around 3.5 months of supply. If out-of-state demand stays high while supply stays tight, prices hold firm.

Also keep an eye on where the growth lands. Newer master-planned areas in the southwest, Skye Canyon, and North Las Vegas tend to draw a lot of relocating families because the homes are newer and the entry prices are friendlier than Summerlin. If California demand keeps climbing, those growth corridors are the places most likely to feel it first in the form of quicker sales and firmer prices.

Ryan's Take

I see this every single week on the ground, so the Realtor.com numbers match what my clients live. A big share of my buyer calls start with a Southern California area code. These folks are not tourists kicking tires. They have usually done the math, sold or are about to sell a home back home, and they arrive ready to make a decision. That energy is real, and it is one reason our market has stayed resilient while other cities wobble.

My honest advice depends on which side you are on. If you own a home in the valley and have thought about selling, know that your buyer pool is bigger than the neighborhood around you. That is leverage, and it is worth using with the right pricing and marketing. If you are a local buyer, do not panic and do not overpay. You can still win, but you need to be prepared, pre-approved, and ready to move when the right home shows up. The buyers coming from California are fast, so you have to be sharp. That is where having someone who knows these neighborhoods block by block makes the difference.

What You Can Do

If you are thinking about buying here, start by getting your financing lined up before you shop. A strong pre-approval letter puts you closer to even footing with a cash buyer from out of state. Know your target neighborhoods, know your budget, and be ready to act. In a market with this much outside demand, hesitation is the most expensive mistake a buyer can make.

If you are a seller, this is a moment to think bigger about who might buy your home. Price it right, present it well, and make sure it shows up in the online searches that California and Bay Area buyers are running every day. A home that looks great in photos and video travels far, and right now a lot of your potential buyers are shopping from hundreds of miles away.

And if you are just trying to understand what all this means for your own street, ask questions. Every part of the valley feels this demand a little differently. What is happening in Summerlin is not the same as what is happening in Aliante or Inspirada. Getting neighborhood-level numbers, not just headlines, is how you make a smart move instead of a rushed one.
