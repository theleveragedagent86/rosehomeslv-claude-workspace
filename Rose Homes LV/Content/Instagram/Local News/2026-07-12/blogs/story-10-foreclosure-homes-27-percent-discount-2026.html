Realtor.com Says Foreclosed Homes Sell at a 27% Discount as Listings Hit a 6-Year High

A new report from Realtor.com says foreclosed homes are selling for about 27% less than regular homes, and the number of foreclosure listings just hit a six-year high. In plain terms, a buyer willing to take on a distressed property could pay tens of thousands of dollars less than they would for a similar move-in-ready house down the street.

That headline sounds like a dream deal, and for the right buyer it can be. But here in Las Vegas, the story looks very different than it does nationally. Distressed sales made up just 1.4% of all Las Vegas transactions in June. So while the national foreclosure discount is real, the supply of these bargain homes in the valley is thin. Let me walk you through what the report actually says, why the Vegas picture is so different, and how to think about it if you want a deal.

What the Realtor.com Report Actually Says

Realtor.com released the report on July 7, 2026. The main finding is simple. Homes sold through foreclosure went for roughly 27% less than comparable non-distressed homes. That is a big gap. On a typical home, that discount can add up to a five-figure or even six-figure savings, depending on the market and the price point.

The report also found that foreclosure listings hit their highest level in six years. That does not mean foreclosures are anywhere near the flood we saw during the 2008 to 2011 crash. It means the number is climbing back up from the record lows we saw during the pandemic years. For most of 2021 and 2022, foreclosures were almost nonexistent because of government relief programs and a red-hot market that let struggling owners sell instead of losing their homes.

Realtor.com framed the story as a value opportunity. Their point is that buyers who are priced out of the regular market might find better deals by looking at distressed listings. With home prices at record highs and mortgage rates stuck in the mid-six-percent range, a 27% discount is a real way to stretch a budget.

Here is the fine print, though. That 27% number is a national average, and it blends together thousands of very different homes and markets. A deep discount often reflects a home that needs serious work. Foreclosed properties are frequently sold as-is, sometimes without a normal inspection, and occasionally at auction where you cannot even walk through first. The discount is not free money. It is often the price of taking on risk, repairs, and hassle that a normal buyer would never touch.

It also matters where these foreclosures actually are. The report reflects the whole country, and the biggest discounts tend to show up in markets that never fully recovered from past downturns or that have softer local economies. High-growth, high-equity markets like Las Vegas produce far fewer foreclosures and much smaller discounts when they do happen. So the average tells you the national mood, not what you will find in your own zip code. Always read a national number through a local lens.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

If you live in the Las Vegas valley, the first thing to know is that these deals are hard to find here right now. Distressed sales, which include both foreclosures and short sales, made up only 1.4% of all Las Vegas transactions in June. That is near the lowest level we have seen in years. So the national headline about foreclosure bargains does not translate into a wave of cheap homes in Summerlin, Henderson, or North Las Vegas.

Why so few? Las Vegas homeowners are sitting on a lot of equity. The median single-family home price held at a record $490,000 in June. When prices are that high and an owner runs into trouble, they almost always sell on the open market and walk away with cash instead of letting the bank foreclose. Foreclosure usually only happens when someone owes more than the home is worth, and very few valley owners are in that spot today.

For buyers, this is a double-edged sword. On one hand, you are not going to stumble onto a 27% discount on a nice home in Green Valley. The bargains are rare, and when a real one pops up, it draws a crowd of investors and cash buyers fast. On the other hand, the low distressed rate is a sign of a healthy, stable market. You are not buying into a neighborhood full of empty, bank-owned homes that drag down every value on the block. That was the nightmare of 2010, and Las Vegas is nowhere near that today.

For homeowners, the low foreclosure rate is reassuring. It means your neighbors are mostly stable, and your home value is not being undercut by distressed sales next door. During the last crash, a single foreclosure on your street could knock thousands off your own home value. That domino effect is simply not happening in most of the valley right now.

There is also a lesson here for anyone comparing Las Vegas to the national headlines. Our market is running on record prices and strong demand, with the valley ranking among the most-searched housing markets in the country. Buyers from Southern California and other pricier states keep flowing in, and that steady demand keeps a floor under prices. When demand stays this strong, distressed inventory gets absorbed quickly instead of piling up. That is the opposite of what a true foreclosure wave looks like.

Background and History

To understand why a 27% foreclosure discount feels so loaded in Las Vegas, you have to remember what this city went through. During the 2008 housing crash, Las Vegas was the epicenter. At the worst of it, more than half of all local sales were distressed. Whole neighborhoods in areas like the southwest valley and North Las Vegas were dotted with foreclosure signs. Prices fell by roughly 60% from the peak. Families who bought at the top were suddenly underwater by hundreds of thousands of dollars.

Back then, buying a foreclosure was not a niche strategy. It was the market. Investors poured into Las Vegas and scooped up cheap homes by the thousands, often paying cash and turning them into rentals. That era shaped how a lot of locals still think about foreclosures. For many longtime residents, the word carries the memory of a painful decade.

The recovery was long but strong. By the late 2010s, prices had climbed back. The pandemic then sent them soaring, and foreclosures nearly vanished as relief programs kept people in their homes. Now, in 2026, we are seeing foreclosure numbers creep up nationally from those historic lows. That is what the Realtor.com report is capturing. It is a normalization, not a crisis.

The key difference between now and 2010 is equity. Today's rising foreclosure count is coming off a floor that was near zero, and most owners have real cushion in their homes. Back then, owners had none. That is why a rising foreclosure number today is a footnote, while the same trend fifteen years ago was a five-alarm fire.

What Happens Next

Nationally, expect the foreclosure count to keep drifting up through the rest of 2026. This is not a signal of a crash. It is the market returning to something closer to normal after years of unusually low numbers. As the pandemic-era relief programs fully wind down and higher rates squeeze some stretched borrowers, a slow rise is the natural result.

In Las Vegas, watch the distressed sales share month to month. If that 1.4% figure starts climbing meaningfully, say toward 3% or 4%, that would be worth paying attention to. It would suggest more local owners are getting into trouble. But at 1.4%, we are still near the bottom of the range, and there is no reason to expect a sudden jump given how much equity valley owners hold.

For buyers hoping to find a deal, the reality is that competition for any genuine foreclosure bargain will stay fierce. Cash buyers made up about 23% of Las Vegas transactions in June, and those buyers move fast on distressed properties. If a real foreclosure deal hits the market at a true discount, it often sells within days, frequently to an investor who can close in cash and take on the repairs. A regular buyer using a mortgage is often at a disadvantage in that race.

The bigger picture for Las Vegas is a market that keeps normalizing rather than swinging to extremes. June brought about 3.5 months of supply, which is close to a balanced market where neither buyers nor sellers hold all the cards. With inventory building slowly, distressed sales near lows, and prices at records, the valley looks stable heading into the second half of the year. That backdrop makes a sudden flood of cheap foreclosures very unlikely, so buyers should plan around the market we actually have, not the bargain-hunting fantasy the national headline might suggest.

Ryan's Take

I get asked about foreclosure deals all the time, and my honest answer is this. In today's Las Vegas market, chasing foreclosures is usually not the smart play for a typical buyer. The supply is tiny at 1.4% of sales, the competition is heavy with cash investors, and the homes that do sell at a steep discount often come with problems that eat up your savings in repairs. That 27% headline discount can shrink fast once you add a new roof, HVAC, and flooring.

Where I do see value is for the right buyer with the right situation. If you have cash, some construction know-how or a trusted contractor, and the patience to wait for the right property, a distressed home can be a genuine opportunity. But go in with open eyes. Get the property inspected if you possibly can, budget generously for repairs, and never assume the listed discount is your true savings. For most families, a well-priced regular home with a normal inspection and clear title is the better path, even without the flashy discount.

What You Can Do

If you are curious about distressed deals in the valley, start by getting clear on your own numbers. Know your budget, get pre-approved for a mortgage, and figure out how much cash you have for repairs on top of the purchase. A foreclosure that needs $40,000 of work is only a deal if you can actually cover that work. Do this math before you fall in love with a listing.

Next, learn the difference between the types of distressed sales. A bank-owned home, sometimes called an REO, is one the lender already took back, and you usually buy it much like a normal home. An auction property is riskier because you often cannot inspect it first and may need to pay cash on the spot. A short sale is one where the owner sells for less than they owe with the bank's approval, and those can drag on for months. Each type has its own rules and traps.

Most of all, work with someone who knows the local market and can tell you when a deal is real and when it is a money pit dressed up as a bargain.
