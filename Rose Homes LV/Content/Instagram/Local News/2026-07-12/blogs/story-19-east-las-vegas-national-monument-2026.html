Push for East Las Vegas National Monument Gains Momentum

A group of supporters wants to turn more than 30,000 acres of open desert just east of the Las Vegas Valley into a new national monument, and their petition is picking up steam. The land sits near Frenchman Mountain, also called Sunrise Mountain, and it draws hikers, fossil hunters, and families looking for a free place to get outside.

The goal is simple. Supporters want to give the East Las Vegas side of the valley the same kind of protected outdoor space that other parts of town already have. They also want to stop the land from being paved over or handed off for development down the road.

What Happened

Supporters of a proposed East Las Vegas National Monument are circulating a petition to place more than 30,000 acres of Bureau of Land Management land under stronger federal protection. That land sits just east of the Las Vegas Valley. The effort was highlighted in a FOX5 report published July 10, 2026.

The heart of the proposal is Frenchman Mountain, which many locals also know as Sunrise Mountain. It is a familiar shape on the eastern skyline. People hike it, hunt for fossils on it, and use the surrounding desert for all kinds of outdoor recreation. Right now most of that land is open BLM ground with very little formal protection.

Bertha Gutierrez is the senior program director of the Conservation Lands Foundation. She is one of the voices leading the push. She said the area is dealing with real problems like illegal dumping and graffiti. Standing on the land, she pointed to the rock layers and explained just how old they are. "This rock right here, this line of rock, is a few billion years difference from the one that's on top," Gutierrez said.

The area is not just pretty desert. It holds a rare geological feature called the Great Unconformity. This is a spot where you can see a huge gap in the rock record, with layers that are billions of years apart sitting right next to each other. It is only visible in two places on Earth. One is off Lake Mead Boulevard in Las Vegas. The other is at the bottom of the Grand Canyon. That puts this stretch of East Las Vegas desert in very rare company.

The petition itself lives at eastlasvegasmonument.org, and supporters are using it to show how much public interest is behind the idea. A monument designation would not turn the land into a locked-off zone. Instead, it would bring stronger management, clearer rules, and a national spotlight on a place that many people in the valley drive past every day without a second thought. Supporters say that spotlight is exactly what the land needs after years of quiet neglect. The FOX5 report on the effort was filed by reporter Kim Passoth.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

This story is really about fairness in outdoor access, and that hits close to home for a lot of valley families. Gutierrez laid out the gap in plain terms. People on the west side have Red Rock Canyon. People in the north have the Desert National Wildlife Refuge and the Spring Mountains. People in the south have Sloan Canyon, Lake Mead, and Avi Kwa Ame.

East Las Vegas does not have that same list. "But people in the east side only have access to Lake Mead and Lake Mead is a fee area," Gutierrez said. In other words, if you live on the east side and you want free, easy access to protected desert, your options are thin. You either pay to get into Lake Mead or you drive across the valley to reach the trails everyone else enjoys.

For homeowners and renters on the east side, protected open space is more than a nice-to-have. Parks, trails, and natural areas tend to lift the quality of life in a neighborhood. They give kids somewhere to explore. They give walkers and cyclists somewhere to go. And over time, easy access to the outdoors can help hold up home values in areas that sometimes get overlooked.

There is also a daily-life angle here. Frenchman Mountain is a quick trip for people in East Las Vegas, Sunrise Manor, and the neighborhoods along Lake Mead Boulevard. A monument would help protect that close-to-home escape instead of letting it slip away to dumping, vandalism, or future development. Gutierrez said the designation would secure free outdoor access for the East Las Vegas community for generations.

It is worth being honest about the split this creates in the valley. The east side has long felt like it gets less attention than places like Summerlin or Henderson when it comes to parks, trails, and public investment. A national monument would be a big, visible change to that pattern. For families who have watched newer parts of town get shiny amenities, this feels like a chance to close part of the gap. That emotional pull is a big reason the petition is spreading.

Free access matters more than it might seem at first. When a trailhead charges a fee, plenty of families simply skip it, especially if money is tight. A protected monument with free entry lowers the barrier to getting outside. That means more weekend hikes, more school field trips, and more casual after-work walks for people who might not otherwise make the drive. Small things like that shape how a neighborhood feels to live in, and they show up in how buyers rate an area.

There is a health and community side too. Easy, free access to open space gets people moving and gives neighbors a shared place to gather. For a part of the valley that has fewer of these spots, that is not a small win. It is the kind of amenity that can slowly change how people talk about East Las Vegas, both the folks who live there and the buyers deciding where to plant roots.

Background and History

National monuments are not a new idea in Southern Nevada. The president can create one using the Antiquities Act, and Nevada already has several protected areas that started this way or through acts of Congress. Avi Kwa Ame, down in the southern part of the region, is a recent example of the same kind of grassroots push turning into lasting protection. Supporters of the East Las Vegas plan are following a well-worn path.

The land in question carries deep meaning beyond the geology. It is considered sacred to the Southern Paiute people. That cultural history is a big part of why supporters believe the area deserves national recognition rather than being treated as leftover open desert. Gutierrez put it directly. "It deserves more. It deserves better management. It deserves more recognition at a national level," she said.

The desert here is also home to life you cannot find anywhere else. The Las Vegas Bear Poppy grows nowhere else in the world. That kind of rare plant is exactly the sort of thing monument status is designed to protect. Fossil hunter Francisco Hernandez said he visits the area often to search for ancient sea creatures. "I love looking for trilobites here," Hernandez said. Those trilobite fossils are a reminder that this dry desert was once under an ocean.

At the same time, the land has faced pressure. Illegal dumping and graffiti have crept in over the years. Without stronger protection and better management, supporters worry those problems will only grow. That worry is a big reason the petition exists in the first place.

It also helps to understand what a national monument actually is. It is a way to protect land that has special natural, cultural, or scientific value. Unlike a national park, which usually needs an act of Congress, a monument can be created by the president under the Antiquities Act. That is the same tool that has protected many well-known sites across the country. It gives supporters a realistic path, since they do not have to wait for a full park bill to pass.

Southern Nevada has leaned on this process before. Avi Kwa Ame, near the southern tip of the state, grew out of years of local advocacy from tribes, conservation groups, and everyday residents before it earned protection. Supporters of the East Las Vegas plan point to that story as proof that a grassroots petition can turn into something lasting. It usually starts exactly where this one is now, with people gathering signatures and telling the story of why a place matters.

The plants and fossils only add to the case. The Las Vegas Bear Poppy, found nowhere else on Earth, is the kind of species that can vanish if its home is disturbed. The trilobite fossils that Francisco Hernandez searches for tell a story that goes back hundreds of millions of years, to a time when this desert sat under a shallow sea. Once ground like this is graded for roads or rooftops, that history is gone for good. Supporters argue that is exactly why the time to protect it is now.

What Happens Next

For now, the effort is in the petition stage. Supporters are gathering names and building public support at eastlasvegasmonument.org. The more momentum they build, the stronger their case becomes when they ask elected officials and federal leaders to act. A national monument designation usually needs either action from the president or a bill through Congress, so community backing is the first big step.

One thing to watch is the tension between conservation and housing. There have been efforts to use federal land on the eastern edges of the Las Vegas Valley to build affordable housing. Advocates for the East Las Vegas Monument have made it clear they do not want that to happen to this particular land. That sets up a real debate the valley will need to work through, because the region needs both open space and more homes.

Expect this conversation to keep growing over the coming months. If the petition keeps gaining signatures, look for public meetings, comment periods, and statements from local and federal officials. Anyone who cares about the outcome, on either side, will have chances to weigh in before anything becomes final.

The housing angle deserves a close watch because it touches a nerve across the whole valley. Las Vegas has a genuine shortage of homes, and much of the developable land around us is controlled by the federal government. That is why proposals to release BLM land for housing come up again and again. If this monument is created, it would take a big block of eastern land off the table for future building. That trade-off is at the center of the debate, and it is not going to be settled quietly.

Keep an eye on how elected leaders line up on this. Land questions in Nevada tend to draw in city councils, the county commission, tribal leaders, conservation groups, and members of Congress. Each of those voices can move the timeline. The petition is the opening move, but the real decisions will play out in public meetings and, eventually, in Washington.

Ryan's Take

As someone who works with buyers and sellers all over the valley, I pay close attention to what makes a neighborhood a place people want to live. Access to the outdoors is a bigger factor than a lot of people realize. Red Rock Canyon is a genuine selling point on the west side. Buyers ask about it. A protected space near Frenchman Mountain could do something similar for East Las Vegas over time.

The trickier part is the housing question. Our valley has a real need for more homes, and federal land is one of the few places that need can be met. So this is not a simple good-versus-bad story. It is about balance. My honest take is that East Las Vegas can absolutely use more protected open space, and the community deserves the same access other parts of town enjoy. The smart move is to protect the truly special land while keeping room in the conversation for housing where it makes sense.

From a market view, this is one more reason to keep an eye on the east side. For years buyers chased the west and the south. But as prices climb, value hunters have been looking harder at neighborhoods like Sunrise Manor and the areas along Lake Mead Boulevard. A protected outdoor amenity nearby is the kind of thing that can nudge those areas up over the long run. If you already own on the east side, this is a story worth following, because it could quietly work in your favor.

What You Can Do

If you want to support the monument push, the first step is easy. You can find and sign the petition at eastlasvegasmonument.org. That is where supporters are tracking public interest and building their case. Signing takes just a minute, and it is the clearest way to show you care about the outcome.

If you are still deciding, go see the land for yourself. Frenchman Mountain and the desert around it are open to hikers, fossil hunters, and anyone who wants a closer look at the Great Unconformity. Seeing those rock layers in person, or spotting a trilobite fossil, helps you understand what the debate is really about. Just pack water, watch the summer heat, and pack out whatever you bring in.

You can also stay informed and speak up when the time comes. Keep an eye out for public meetings and comment periods tied to the proposal. Reach out to your local and federal representatives to share where you stand, whether you favor protection, more housing, or a mix of both. Local land decisions like this one shape neighborhoods for decades, so your voice matters.
