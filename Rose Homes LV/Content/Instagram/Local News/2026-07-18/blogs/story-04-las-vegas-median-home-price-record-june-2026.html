Las Vegas Median Home Price Holds at Record $490,000 in June

The median price of an existing single-family home in Southern Nevada stayed at $490,000 in June 2026. That matches the all-time record set in May and sits about 1 percent above where prices were in June 2025.

Las Vegas Realtors put those numbers out on July 7, 2026. They come from homes that actually closed through the local Multiple Listing Service, so they track real sales and not computer estimates. For sellers, this is proof that the top of the market is holding. For buyers who have been waiting for prices to slip, it is a second straight month of waiting.

The bigger story is not just the price. It is why the price is not moving. Supply is still thin, demand is steady, and Las Vegas keeps behaving differently than the national housing headlines suggest it should.

What Happened

Las Vegas Realtors, the local trade group that runs the MLS most Clark County agents use, released its June 2026 housing report on July 7. The headline number was the median sales price for an existing single-family home in Southern Nevada, which came in at $490,000. That is the exact same figure the group reported for May 2026, and both months tie for the highest median ever recorded in this market.

Sales volume backed up the price. Total transaction value for single-family homes in June topped $1.4 billion. Across all property types, 2,823 existing homes, condos, and townhomes closed during the month. Single-family sales alone jumped 18.3 percent compared to June 2025, which is a large year-over-year move in a market people keep describing as frozen.

George Kypreos, the president of Las Vegas Realtors, credited tight supply and steady demand for the record. He also made a point that has been true for a while now. As he put it, "local home prices don't always follow national trends, but they have been lately." That line matters, because the national median also hit a record in June at $440,600 while national sales fell 2.4 percent for the month.

Inventory tells the rest of the story. At the end of June, 7,147 single-family homes sat on the market without an offer. That is up 2.2 percent from a year earlier, so supply is growing, just slowly. Seven thousand listings against the current pace of sales works out to roughly three and a half months of supply. Nationally, the figure was 4.6 months. Anything under about six months is generally considered a seller's market, so Las Vegas is still tilted toward sellers, and more so than the country as a whole.

The attached-home side of the market moved the opposite direction. The median price for an existing condo or townhome in Southern Nevada fell to $292,000 in June, down 4.3 percent from a year earlier. Condo and townhome listings without offers totaled 2,690 units, up 4.9 percent year over year. That is a faster inventory build than the house side, and it shows up directly in the price.

One honest note about the $490,000 figure. If you search Las Vegas home prices online right now, you will see very different numbers. Redfin, Zillow, and Movoto have reported Las Vegas medians anywhere from about $430,634 to $482,000 in recent updates. None of those sources are lying. They are counting different things. The Las Vegas Realtors number covers existing single-family homes sold through the local MLS across Southern Nevada. Aggregator numbers often blend in condos and townhomes, sometimes include new construction, and sometimes limit the map to Las Vegas city limits instead of the full metro. They also use different time windows and different math. When you compare one month to another, use the same source both times. Mixing sources is how people end up thinking the market swung when it did not.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

If you own a house in Clark County, this report is the closest thing to a scoreboard you get. Two straight months at $490,000 means the equity you gained over the last few years has not started leaking away. Homeowners in Summerlin, Henderson, Centennial Hills, and Mountains Edge who were nervously watching national crash headlines can exhale a little. The local number simply is not cooperating with those headlines.

If you are trying to buy, the picture is harder. A $490,000 median means half the single-family homes that sold went for more than that. Pair that price with a 30-year fixed mortgage rate near 6.55 percent, which is where rates sat in mid-July and the highest level since August 2025, and the monthly payment math gets tight fast. Waiting has not helped. Buyers who sat out the spring watching for a dip are now looking at the same price and a slightly worse rate than a year ago, when the average was 6.75 percent but local prices were lower.

There is also a gap worth knowing about. The Las Vegas median sits roughly $49,400 above the national median of $440,600. For a long stretch of the 2010s, Las Vegas was the affordable alternative to California. That gap has closed and then flipped. A family relocating here from the Midwest or the South is not landing in a bargain market anymore, and local buyers competing with those relocating households feel it.

Renters feel this too, even though they are not buying. When the median holds at a record and mortgage payments stay high, fewer renters make the jump to ownership. They stay in the rental pool longer, which shapes demand for apartments and single-family rentals across North Las Vegas, Spring Valley, and the southwest valley. The one bit of relief is that local rents have actually softened over the past year, which gives renters a bit more room to save while they wait.

Finally, this affects your property tax picture and your insurance renewal. Assessed values in Nevada do not move one for one with market prices, and Nevada's tax abatement caps limit how fast the bill can climb on an owner-occupied home. Still, a market holding at record levels tends to push replacement cost estimates up, and that is what insurance carriers price against. Watch your renewal notice this year.

First-time buyers feel the squeeze most directly, because the down payment target moves with the price. Three percent down on a $490,000 home is $14,700 before closing costs. Five percent is $24,500. Twenty percent is $98,000. Every time the median ticks up, the savings goal moves with it, and wages do not move that fast. This is the single biggest reason younger Clark County households are staying in rentals longer than their parents did.

Background and History

To understand why $490,000 feels like such a big number locally, it helps to remember how far this market has traveled. Las Vegas was the epicenter of the housing crash. Values here fell further and stayed down longer than almost anywhere in the country. Whole neighborhoods in North Las Vegas and the southwest valley were underwater for years. That memory is baked into how locals talk about real estate, and it is why every national crash headline gets shared here so fast.

Recovery came slowly through the mid-2010s, then accelerated hard in 2020 and 2021. Remote work, low rates, and California outmigration pushed Southern Nevada prices to a peak in 2022. When rates spiked, the market cooled and the median dipped. Many people assumed that was the start of a long slide. It was not. Prices stabilized, then began grinding upward again, and the last two years have been a slow climb rather than a spike.

The engine behind that climb is supply, not speculation. Southern Nevada is boxed in. The federal government owns most of the land around the valley, so builders cannot simply expand outward the way they can in Phoenix or Dallas. Land releases are limited and slow. At the same time, a huge share of existing homeowners locked in mortgage rates in the 3 percent range and have no financial reason to sell and re-buy at 6.5 percent. That keeps existing inventory low year after year.

Demand has been steady rather than explosive. Population growth continues, and the local economy has diversified past pure gaming and hospitality into sports, healthcare, logistics, and data centers. Those jobs bring households that need housing. When steady demand meets constrained supply, prices grind up even when rates are high. That is exactly the pattern the June report shows.

What Happens Next

The next Las Vegas Realtors report covers July 2026 and should land in the first half of August. That release will answer the question everyone is asking, which is whether $490,000 was a ceiling or a landing. Three straight months at the same number would signal a real plateau. A move in either direction would be the first meaningful shift since spring.

Watch inventory more closely than price. Single-family listings without offers were up 2.2 percent year over year, and condo listings were up 4.9 percent. If that build keeps accelerating through the back half of summer, price pressure eases naturally. Summer is also when Las Vegas inventory typically peaks, since families try to move before the school year starts. CCSD classes begin August 10 this year, so the seasonal window closes soon and listing activity usually slows into the fall.

Rates are the other variable. The 30-year fixed sat at 6.55 percent in mid-July, an eleven-month high. If rates ease this fall, expect more buyers to re-enter, which would tighten supply again and support prices. If rates keep climbing, the more likely outcome is fewer sales rather than falling prices, because sellers with 3 percent mortgages tend to pull their listing rather than cut deeply.

Also keep an eye on new construction. Builders in Las Vegas, North Las Vegas, and Henderson rolled out aggressive summer incentives, including advertised rate buydowns near 3.99 percent and savings packages worth tens of thousands of dollars. Builders can buy down a rate in ways an individual seller cannot. If those incentives keep pulling buyers toward new homes, resale sellers will feel more competition this fall than they did this spring.

The high end is worth tracking separately. A recent Redfin report found Las Vegas luxury home prices rose 13.7 percent year over year, the third fastest growth among major U.S. metros behind only Tampa and Miami. That segment is not behaving like the rest of the valley. If luxury keeps climbing at that pace while the broad median flattens, the overall median can stay pinned at $490,000 even as different price tiers pull in opposite directions. A flat median does not always mean a flat market.

Ryan's Take

I have watched a lot of buyers try to time this market over the last three years, and almost none of them came out ahead. The waiting strategy assumed that higher rates would force prices down here the way they did in some other metros. That has not happened, and the reason is structural. We do not have enough land and we do not have enough sellers. You cannot wait out a supply problem.

What I would push back on is treating $490,000 like it describes your house. It does not. It is a valley-wide midpoint that blends a 1,400 square foot home in North Las Vegas with a Summerlin house at three times that price. Some pockets are running hot right now, especially the higher end, where luxury prices have risen sharply. Other pockets, particularly condos and townhomes, have actually given ground. If you are pricing a listing off a headline number, you are going to get it wrong. Price off the last six comparable closings within a mile of your front door, and be honest about condition.

For buyers, here is the version I give people at my kitchen table. The condo and townhome side is the one part of this market where you currently have leverage, with the median down 4.3 percent and inventory building. If a townhome fits your life, this is a genuinely better moment to buy one than it was a year ago.

What You Can Do

If you are thinking about selling, start by pulling your own comparable sales instead of relying on an online estimate. Look at homes in your zip code, similar in size and age, that closed in the last 60 to 90 days. Then compare that to what is currently listed and sitting. The listings that are sitting tell you where the ceiling really is. Homes priced correctly in Clark County are still moving, and 2,823 closings in a single month proves buyers are active.

If you are buying, get fully underwritten before you shop, not just pre-qualified. In a market with three and a half months of supply, a clean, verified offer beats a slightly higher offer with a soft letter attached. Ask your lender to price out both a standard loan and a temporary buydown, and then ask new-home sales offices what their current incentive is in writing. Builder incentives change monthly and they are often worth more than a small price cut on a resale.

If you own and are simply curious, check whether your assessed value and your homeowners insurance coverage still make sense at today's values. Nevada's tax caps protect owner-occupied homes from sharp jumps, but insurance replacement cost is a different calculation and it does drift with the market. A ten minute call to your carrier can save you from an ugly surprise at renewal time. You can also monitor the monthly Las Vegas Realtors reports yourself, since they are published publicly each month and cover the same MLS data agents use.

One more thing for long-time owners. Record values mean record equity on paper, and that changes what options are available to you. If you have been putting off a roof, a remodel, or paying down higher-interest debt, it is worth running the numbers on what your current equity position actually supports. You do not have to sell to use what the market has handed you, and knowing the number beats guessing at it.
