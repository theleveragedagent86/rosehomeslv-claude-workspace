Clark County Task Force Releases 75 School Traffic Safety Recommendations

A Clark County task force has put 75 specific school traffic safety fixes on paper, and many of the biggest ones do not have money behind them yet. The countywide School Traffic Safety Working Group released its report on July 13, 2026, less than four weeks before Clark County School District classes start on August 10.

The list runs from small repairs to major road work. Some items are meant to happen in the next few months. Others are five-year projects. The report sorts every recommendation into four groups: education, enforcement, engineering and planning, and policy.

This is the first time the county has published a numbered, organized plan for the streets around schools. It is also the first time the funding gap has been stated so plainly. Several of the larger engineering items depend on agencies finding money that has not been secured.

What the Report Actually Says

The School Traffic Safety Working Group is a countywide group. It brings together local agencies and state agencies that each control a different piece of the problem. One agency paints the crosswalk. Another sets the speed limit. A third hires the crossing guard. That split is part of why fixes have been slow.

The report identifies 75 proposals that these agencies could put in place over a window that starts in the next few months and stretches out five years. Sorting them into four buckets was deliberate. Education means teaching drivers, students, and parents. Enforcement means police presence and citations. Engineering and planning means physical changes to streets, signs, and signals. Policy means the rules and laws that govern all of it.

The immediate first steps are the ones parents will notice fastest. The group called for extra police presence around schools during the first two weeks of class. It called for replacing missing and damaged signs. It called for repairs to pavement markings and landscaping that blocks sight lines. It also called for hiring more crossing guards.

Those four items share something in common. They are relatively cheap, they use existing crews and budgets, and they can happen fast. A faded crosswalk can be repainted in an afternoon. An overgrown bush that hides a child from a turning driver can be trimmed the same week. That is why they lead the list.

The harder items sit further out. Redesigning an intersection, adding a signal, building a new sidewalk, or changing how a major road handles school traffic all cost real money and take real time. The report is clear that many of those depend on agencies finding funding. That funding is not locked in.

It also helps to be clear about what this document is. These are recommendations, not orders. The working group cannot force a city to repave a street or force a school to change a drop-off pattern. What it can do is name the fix, name the agency that owns it, and put a rough timeline next to it. That turns a vague community complaint into a specific request with an address on it.

The four-bucket structure matters more than it might sound. Education and policy items are the cheapest to act on because they mostly cost staff time. Enforcement costs officer hours, which agencies can shift around in the short term. Engineering and planning is where the real dollars live, because concrete, signals, and construction crews all carry a price tag. Sorting the list this way shows at a glance which ideas can move now and which ones are waiting on a check.

The context behind the report is difficult. During the past school year, 427 young people age 18 and under were struck by vehicles while going to or from school in Clark County. Four of them died. That total is a jump of more than 350 percent over the prior year. Those are the numbers that pushed the working group to form and to publish.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

School starts Monday, August 10, and every school in the district is running a new bell schedule. Middle schools run 7:30 a.m. to 1:41 p.m. High schools run 8:30 a.m. to 2:41 p.m. Elementary schools run 9:15 a.m. to 3:26 p.m. That means the pattern of foot traffic on your street is about to change.

If you live near a middle school, kids will be walking earlier than you are used to. If you live near an elementary school, the morning rush now lands closer to mid-morning. Drivers who have driven the same route for years will be meeting children at different hours in different places. The habits everyone built last year do not automatically apply.

Enforcement is also changing. A new Nevada law that took effect July 1 doubles both the fines and the license points for violations in active school zones. Jeff Clark, the new chief of the CCSD Police Department, was sworn in July 13 and said traffic safety is a top priority. He put it directly: "if we are there and you're speeding through a school zone, we will hold you accountable. We will be writing tickets."

Put those pieces together and the first two weeks of the school year will look different than last August. More officers near campuses. Higher penalties. New start times. If you drive through a school zone on your commute, budget a few extra minutes and slow down early.

There is a housing angle too. Clark County School District serves more than 280,000 students across 499 campuses. Almost every neighborhood in the valley sits near at least one school. The condition of the crosswalks, signs, and sidewalks around those campuses is part of what people are buying when they buy a home. Families ask me about walkability constantly, and they should.

Renters feel it as well. A parent choosing between two apartment complexes in Spring Valley or North Las Vegas will weigh whether their child can safely reach the campus on foot. A missing sign or a dark corner is not a small detail to that family. It is the deciding factor.

The geography of the valley is part of the challenge. Many Clark County schools sit near wide arterial roads with long blocks between signals. A child who wants to reach a campus on the other side of one of those roads either walks a long way to a signal or crosses somewhere that was never designed for them. That pattern shows up in Henderson, in the northwest, in Spring Valley, and in the older parts of the central valley alike.

Drop-off lines feel the effect too. When parents do not trust the walking route, more of them drive, and the carpool line gets longer. Longer lines push cars onto the surrounding streets, which puts more traffic right where the kids on foot are walking. Fixing the walking route is one of the few changes that helps drivers and walkers at the same time.

Background and History

The crash numbers did not appear overnight. Reporting on students being struck by vehicles ran for months before this report came out. Parents have been raising the issue at meetings, in neighborhood groups, and with their local elected officials since last school year.

What makes this week different is the format. Until now, the response was a series of separate concerns and separate agency answers. The 75-item report is the first single document that names the problems, groups them, and assigns rough timelines. It gives residents something concrete to point at and ask about.

A large piece of the picture involves e-bikes and e-scooters. About half of the 427 students struck by cars last school year were riding an e-scooter or an e-bike. Those devices moved into Clark County neighborhoods fast, and the streets were not redesigned for them.

Local trauma centers have been tracking the same trend from the hospital side. Sunrise Hospital and Medical Center has logged 424 traumatic e-bike and e-scooter injuries so far in 2026, averaging about 65 a month, and projects roughly 780 by year's end. University Medical Center reported 271 injuries as of May, including 28 involving children under 14.

John Pope, vice president of Sunrise's Trauma Center, said roughly 30 percent of the injuries they treat are children. E-scooter cases show up most among riders ages 11 to 13. E-bike cases show up most among high schoolers ages 14 to 18. Those are the exact age groups walking and riding to campus every school morning.

That mix explains why the report covers so much ground. A crosswalk designed for a child walking at three miles an hour behaves differently when that child arrives at fifteen or twenty miles an hour on an e-bike. Drivers scanning for a walker may not register a rider moving that fast. Signs, sight lines, and signal timing were all set before these devices were common on Clark County streets.

What Happens Next

The near-term items should show up before and during the first two weeks of class. Watch for crews replacing damaged or missing school zone signs, repainting crosswalks and stop bars, and trimming landscaping near corners. Watch for more marked patrol cars near campuses in the mornings and afternoons. These are the pieces the working group said could move quickly.

Crossing guard hiring is the one to track closely. More guards is a recommendation, not a completed hire. Staffing these positions has been a challenge across the valley, and the number of guards actually on corners in August will tell you a lot about how seriously the recommendation was taken.

The larger engineering work runs on a different clock. Road redesigns, new signals, and sidewalk construction move through budget cycles at Clark County, the City of Las Vegas, Henderson, North Las Vegas, Boulder City, and the Nevada Department of Transportation depending on who owns the road. Each of those bodies has to find room in its own budget. That is the open question the report leaves on the table.

Policy items may move through the 2027 Nevada legislative session or through local ordinances before that. The July 1 school zone penalty increase shows that state lawmakers are already engaged on this topic. Whether more follows depends partly on how loudly residents keep asking.

There are a few concrete things to watch this fall. Count the crossing guards at the corners near your school in the first week and again in October. Notice whether the faded paint at your nearest school crossing gets refreshed. Check whether the extra police presence continues past the first two weeks or fades once the school year settles in. Those three signals will tell you whether this report has momentum behind it.

The practical test comes in about a year. If the crash numbers for the 2026-27 school year come down, the quick fixes worked. If they do not, the conversation shifts to the expensive items that still need funding.

Ryan's Take

I look at a lot of neighborhoods with a lot of families, and school walkability comes up in nearly every conversation with buyers who have kids. People ask about the school rating first and the walk to that school second, and the second question is usually the one that changes their mind about a street.

Here is what I would watch as a homeowner. Streets that get the quick fixes early, meaning fresh paint, working signs, clear sight lines, and a staffed corner, tend to be streets where residents spoke up. The county cannot see every faded crosswalk on its own. Neighborhoods that report problems get attention sooner than neighborhoods that assume someone else already did.

The funding gap is real and worth watching without panic. A 75-item list where the cheap items move first and the expensive items wait is how most public safety work actually happens. The value of this report is that residents can now name the specific fix they want and ask exactly where it sits on the list.

What You Can Do

Walk the route before August 10. Take the actual path your child will take, at the actual time of day they will take it, and look for the trouble spots. Faded crosswalks, missing signs, blocked sight lines at corners, cars parked too close to the crossing, and gaps in the sidewalk are all things you can report.

Report them to the agency that owns the road. If it is a county road, that is Clark County Public Works. If it is inside a city, contact the public works or traffic department for Las Vegas, Henderson, North Las Vegas, or Boulder City. If it is a state highway, that is the Nevada Department of Transportation. When you call, give the exact cross streets and the name of the nearby school. Specific reports get fixed faster than general complaints.

Talk with your kids about the rules before the first day. Cross at the corner. Walk the bike or scooter through the crosswalk. Wear a helmet every ride. Make eye contact with drivers before stepping out. These sound basic, and the injury data says they still matter.

Show up if you can. Clark County Commission meetings, city council meetings, and CCSD Board of Trustees meetings all take public comment. Three minutes from a parent naming a specific intersection carries more weight than most people expect, especially now that there is a published list to reference.

Consider organizing with neighbors. A walking group where two or three adults take turns escorting a handful of kids to campus costs nothing and covers the gap while the bigger fixes wait on funding. Several parents reporting the same intersection also lands harder than one parent calling alone. Your HOA board or neighborhood group can be a useful place to start that conversation.

Finally, adjust your own driving. The new bell times mean children are on the sidewalks at hours you may not be used to. School zone fines and points doubled on July 1. Slowing down early costs you a minute and protects a kid you will never meet.
