Federal Lawsuit Claims North Las Vegas School Security Monitor Concussed a 9-Year-Old

A federal lawsuit filed on Wednesday, July 8, claims a school security monitor at Jesse D. Scott Elementary School in North Las Vegas grabbed a 9-year-old boy by the arms and slammed him against a wall, causing a concussion. No judge or jury has ruled on any part of it, so every single detail in that filing is still an allegation and not a finding of fact.

The case landed in U.S. District Court and is drawing attention across the Las Vegas Valley because of who the complaint says was involved. According to the complaint, the child has ADHD and was eligible for special education services when the incident is alleged to have happened. His mother, Gloria Terrazas, brought the suit on his behalf. It seeks more than $150,000 in damages and asks for a jury trial.

The Clark County School District has not responded to the specific claims in public. A district spokesperson said CCSD cannot discuss pending litigation or personnel matters. The district did confirm one thing on the record: the employment of the man named in the lawsuit ended in August 2025.

Below is a careful walk through what the filing claims, what the district has and has not said, how the federal court process usually works from here, and what parents in North Las Vegas can actually do while the case is open.

What the Lawsuit Claims

The complaint was filed in U.S. District Court on July 8, 2026. It names a Scott Elementary School security monitor, Anthony Boone, along with the Clark County School District. The Las Vegas Review-Journal first reported the filing on July 10.

According to the complaint, the incident happened on April 4, 2024. The lawsuit alleges that Boone grabbed the 9-year-old boy by the arms and slammed him against a wall. The filing claims the boy suffered a concussion as a result. Again, these are allegations. The court has not weighed the evidence, and no ruling has been issued.

The complaint says the boy has ADHD and was eligible for special education services at the time. That detail matters legally, because federal and state rules put extra limits on how school staff may physically handle students with disabilities. The lawsuit frames the alleged incident as a violation of those protections.

The filing also describes what happened after the alleged incident. According to the complaint, the boy could not return to school for about a month for mental health reasons. For a nine-year-old, a month away from a classroom is a long stretch. It covers reading groups, math units, friendships, and routines that are hard to rebuild once a child has been out that long.

One of the more serious claims in the filing is about history. The lawsuit alleges that Boone had a pattern of what the complaint calls unlawful aversive interventions against students, and that he was never disciplined for it. The complaint uses that alleged pattern to argue the district should have known and should have acted sooner. That claim has not been tested in court, and the district has not addressed it publicly.

The suit asks for more than $150,000 in damages. It also requests a jury trial, which means that if the case is not dismissed or settled first, a panel of Clark County residents could eventually hear the evidence and decide.

The Clark County School District's public response has been narrow and consistent. The district said it cannot comment on pending litigation or on personnel matters. That is a standard position for a large public employer facing an active federal case, and it is not an admission or a denial of anything. The one factual item CCSD did confirm is that Boone's employment with the district ended in August 2025. The district did not say why it ended, and the lawsuit does not settle that question either.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Clark County School District is one of the largest school systems in the country. It serves families in North Las Vegas, Las Vegas, Henderson, Boulder City, and every unincorporated pocket in between. When a case like this surfaces, it does not stay inside one school. Parents in Aliante, Eldorado, Centennial Hills, and Summerlin all read the same headline and ask the same question about their own kid's campus.

The most immediate reason this story hits hard is that it involves a child who, according to the complaint, has a disability. Thousands of Clark County students have an Individualized Education Program or a 504 plan. Those students often need extra support with behavior, transitions, and frustration. Parents of those kids already spend a lot of energy making sure the adults at school understand their child. A case like this makes that worry sharper.

There is also the accountability question, and it is a fair one to ask without assuming an answer. The complaint alleges a pattern that went unaddressed. If that turns out to be accurate, families will want to know how the reporting system missed it. If it turns out to be inaccurate, that matters too, because a person's name and reputation are on the line in a public filing. Both outcomes are possible right now, and neither has been decided.

Timing plays a role in how loud this story is. The incident is alleged to have happened in April 2024, and the lawsuit was filed in July 2026. That gap is normal in civil litigation, but it can feel jarring to a parent reading about it for the first time two years later. It is a reminder that legal timelines and news timelines do not move at the same speed.

There is a practical angle as well. School security monitors are a familiar presence on CCSD campuses. They watch entrances, monitor hallways and lunch areas, and help staff respond when something goes sideways. Most of them do difficult work well, in crowded buildings, with limited backup. One lawsuit does not describe an entire job category. It does, however, put a spotlight on training, supervision, and how complaints move up the chain.

Finally, this is a school year story. Clark County families are heading into a new school year in a matter of weeks. Parents are picking bus stops, filling out forms, and meeting new teachers. Questions about safety, restraint policy, and who to call when something goes wrong are exactly the questions worth asking during those first weeks, not after something happens.

It is also worth thinking about the ripple effect on kids who are not directly involved. Elementary students talk. When a story circulates through a campus community, younger children pick up pieces of it without the context adults have. Teachers and counselors often end up managing that anxiety on top of everything else on their plate. If your child comes home with questions, a calm and simple answer usually works better than a detailed one.

Background and History

Jesse D. Scott Elementary School sits in North Las Vegas and serves a working-class part of the valley where many families have deep roots. Like most CCSD elementary campuses, it draws from surrounding neighborhoods, so the parents who read this news are often neighbors on the same streets. That is part of why a story like this travels fast in North Las Vegas.

Nevada has rules about how school employees may physically handle students, and those rules are stricter for students with disabilities. State law limits the use of aversive intervention and physical restraint in schools, and it requires schools to document and report incidents when restraint is used. The general idea behind those rules is simple. Physical force is meant to be a last resort used to prevent immediate harm, not a routine response to a behavior problem.

Those protections exist because of a long national history of restraint being used too often on students with disabilities. Advocacy groups and federal reports have raised the issue for years. Nevada lawmakers and the Nevada Department of Education have revisited restraint and seclusion rules multiple times, and districts across the state are required to train staff on them. The lawsuit against CCSD is built on the argument that those protections were not followed in this case.

It also helps to understand why this is in federal court rather than state court. Claims involving the constitutional rights of a student, or federal disability protections, often belong in the federal system. That choice tends to signal that the plaintiff is arguing the case is about civil rights and not only about a personal injury.

CCSD, for its part, has faced litigation before, as any district serving hundreds of thousands of students eventually does. The district's standard practice in active cases is to say nothing publicly and to respond through court filings. That silence is frustrating for parents who want answers today, but it is how public agencies typically handle open litigation on the advice of their attorneys.

What Happens Next

The next step in a federal civil case is a response. The defendants named in the complaint have a set window to file an answer or a motion to dismiss. An answer addresses the allegations point by point. A motion to dismiss argues the case should not move forward at all, either because of a legal defect or because the complaint does not state a valid claim. Either filing will be the first time the other side's version becomes part of the public record.

If the case survives that stage, it moves into discovery. That is the long middle of a lawsuit where both sides exchange documents, take depositions, and gather records. In a case like this, discovery could involve personnel files, incident reports, training records, and testimony from school staff. Discovery in federal court often takes many months and sometimes longer than a year.

Most civil cases never reach a jury. They settle, get dismissed, or resolve through motions before trial. The lawsuit requests a jury trial, but requesting one and getting one are different things. If this case does go to trial, it would likely be well into the future, not this school year.

Parents should also expect that CCSD will keep declining to comment while the case is open. Personnel matters are confidential, and pending litigation is handled by counsel. That is not evasive, it is standard. The information that does become public will most likely arrive through court filings rather than press statements.

One thing worth watching is whether this case prompts any broader review of restraint policy or security monitor training at the district level. School board meetings are the place where that kind of conversation would surface. If community members raise it during public comment, trustees may ask staff for a policy briefing. That is a normal path for a single case to turn into a district-wide discussion.

Local news coverage is the other thing to track. The Las Vegas Review-Journal broke this story, and outlets in the valley usually follow federal cases involving CCSD as new filings hit the docket. Federal court records are also public, so anyone who wants to follow the case directly can look it up rather than relying on secondhand summaries. Just remember that a filing is one side making its argument, whichever side wrote it.

Ryan's Take

I want to be careful here, because there is a real child at the center of this and there is also a person named in a filing who has not had his day in court. Both of those things deserve respect. What I will say is that stories like this shape how families feel about a school long before a court decides anything, and that is worth naming honestly.

In my work I talk to buyers about schools constantly. People move across the valley for a campus they trust. When a case like this surfaces, some parents get nervous about a whole zip code, and that reaction is usually bigger than the facts support. One lawsuit involving one campus and one employee is not a verdict on North Las Vegas schools, and it is not a verdict on North Las Vegas as a place to live. Aliante, Eldorado, and the neighborhoods around Scott Elementary are full of families who like where they are.

My honest advice is to slow down before drawing conclusions from a headline. Visit the school. Ask about their behavior support and restraint policies directly. Talk to parents who are already there. That kind of firsthand information is worth far more than a reaction to a story where the facts are still being sorted out in court.

What You Can Do

If your child has an IEP or a 504 plan, ask the school directly how behavior situations are handled and who responds when a student is in crisis. You can request that the plan spell out specific de-escalation steps and name the staff members who are trained in them. That written detail gives you something concrete to point to later if anything goes wrong.

Learn the reporting chain before you need it. Start with the teacher, then the school administration, then the CCSD region office, and then the Board of School Trustees if the issue is unresolved. Nevada also requires schools to notify parents and document incidents when restraint is used, so ask what that documentation looks like and how quickly you would receive it. Write down dates, names, and what you were told each time. A simple log is the single most useful thing a parent can keep.

Show up where policy actually gets set. CCSD Board of Trustees meetings include public comment, and trustees pay attention when several families raise the same concern. If you want restraint and de-escalation training reviewed district-wide, that room is where the request has to land. Meeting dates and agendas are posted on the district website.

And if a school year has already gone sideways for your family, talk to a Nevada attorney who handles education or disability law before you make decisions. This article is local news reporting, not legal advice, and the details of every situation are different.
