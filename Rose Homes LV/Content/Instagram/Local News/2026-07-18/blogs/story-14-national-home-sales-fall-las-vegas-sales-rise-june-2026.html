National Home Sales Fell in June. Southern Nevada Sales Went Up

U.S. existing home sales fell 2.4 percent in June 2026, but Southern Nevada went the opposite direction. Las Vegas Realtors logged 2,823 existing homes, condos and townhomes sold last month, and single-family home sales jumped 18.3 percent compared to June of last year.

That is the entire story in two numbers. The country slowed down. Clark County sped up. If you have been reading headlines that say the housing market is frozen, those headlines are not describing the valley you live in.

The National Association of Realtors released the national figures on July 9, 2026. Las Vegas Realtors put out the local report on July 7, 2026. Same month, same economy, two very different results. And the gap is not small. The Las Vegas median sits roughly $49,400 above the national median right now.

This matters because most people build their picture of the housing market from national news. That national picture is an average of hundreds of metro areas stitched together. It tells you almost nothing about what a buyer in Henderson or a seller in Centennial Hills should actually expect this summer.

What Happened in Both Markets

Start with the national report. The National Association of Realtors said existing home sales fell 2.4 percent from May to June 2026. That put the pace at 4.09 million homes on a seasonally adjusted annual basis. In plain language, if the country kept selling homes at June's rate for a full year, and you smoothed out the normal seasonal swings, you would end up with about 4.09 million sales.

Prices went the other way. The national median sales price hit a record $440,600 in June. That is up 1.8 percent from a year earlier, and it marked the 36th straight month of annual price gains. Three full years of year over year increases. So the national story is not falling prices. It is fewer transactions at higher prices.

National supply landed at 4.6 months. Months of supply is a simple idea. It answers one question: at the current sales pace, how long would it take to sell every home currently on the market if nothing new got listed? Anything under six months usually favors sellers. Anything above six months usually favors buyers.

Now flip to Southern Nevada. Las Vegas Realtors reported 2,823 total closings in June across existing homes, condos and townhomes. Sales rose compared to May and compared to June of last year. Single-family sales alone were up 18.3 percent year over year and up 11.4 percent from May. An 18.3 percent annual jump is not a rounding error. That is a meaningful pickup in activity.

The local median price for an existing single-family home came in at $490,000. Local supply was tighter too. At the end of June, 7,147 single-family homes sat listed without an offer, up 2.2 percent from a year ago. That works out to roughly three and a half months of supply, compared to 4.6 months nationally.

It helps to separate the two local figures. The 2,823 number counts every closing across existing single-family homes, condos and townhomes together. The 18.3 percent gain applies to single-family homes on their own. Both moved up, which is why Las Vegas Realtors described June as a pickup versus both the prior month and the same month last year. The 11.4 percent jump from May is the one worth staring at, because May to June is not normally a stretch when local closings accelerate that sharply.

Three and a half months of supply against 4.6 months sounds like a small difference. It is not. That gap means a typical Clark County listing faces a shorter runway of competition than the national average, which is the mechanical reason local prices held up while local sales climbed. Supply and price move together far more reliably than sales volume and price do.

One honest note on the comparison. The national median of $440,600 covers all existing homes, including condos and co-ops. The $490,000 Las Vegas figure is for existing single-family homes specifically. The categories are not a perfect match. Even so, the direction of travel is clear. Vegas sales climbed while national sales slipped, and local inventory is thinner than the national average.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

If you are thinking about selling, this is the practical takeaway. Do not price your home off a national headline. A seller in Spring Valley who reads "home sales fell 2.4 percent" and decides to cut $20,000 off the asking price is reacting to data from Ohio, Texas and Florida. Meanwhile buyer activity in their own zip code went up.

If you are thinking about buying, the takeaway cuts the other way. A lot of Clark County buyers have been sitting on the sidelines waiting for a crash. June did not deliver one. Sales rose, inventory stayed under four months of supply, and the median held at $490,000. Waiting has a cost, and right now that cost is competing with more buyers, not fewer.

The $49,400 gap between the Las Vegas median and the national median is worth sitting with. A buyer moving here from a cheaper metro often shows up expecting national pricing and gets a surprise. A buyer moving here from Southern California often shows up expecting worse and gets a pleasant one. Both reactions are common, and both come from using the wrong reference point.

For current homeowners who are not moving at all, this still matters. Your equity position is tied to local values, not national ones. Three straight years of national price gains combined with tight local supply means most Clark County owners who bought before 2024 are sitting on real equity. That affects refinancing options, home equity lines, and whether a move-up purchase is realistic.

First-time buyers feel the price gap most directly. A $490,000 purchase requires a bigger down payment and a bigger monthly payment than a $440,600 purchase, and that difference compounds across a 30-year loan. That is the real cost of living in a metro that prices above the national middle. It is also why down payment assistance programs and builder incentives get so much attention in this valley.

Appraisals are another place this shows up. An appraiser pulls comparable sales from your immediate area, not from a national database. When local sales volume rises, there are simply more recent comparable sales to work with, and deals tend to hit fewer valuation surprises. Thin sales activity is what creates appraisal problems, and June handed Clark County appraisers more data to work from, not less.

Renters watching from the sidelines should pay attention to the supply number specifically. With 7,147 single-family homes listed without an offer and only 2.2 percent more than last year, inventory is barely growing. Thin supply is what keeps prices firm even when interest rates make monthly payments uncomfortable.

Background and History

Thirty-six consecutive months of national price gains means values have risen year over year every single month since mid-2023. That streak survived rate hikes, affordability complaints, and a steady drumbeat of crash predictions. Prices have been stubborn because supply has been stubborn. Not enough homes for sale, in most of the country, for most of that stretch.

Las Vegas has its own version of that story, plus a few local wrinkles. The valley is boxed in by federal land. The Bureau of Land Management controls a huge share of the ground around the metro area, which limits how far builders can push outward without a land release. Developable dirt is finite here in a way it simply is not in Phoenix or Dallas.

Population and job growth keep pulling people in. Tourism drives the base, but the local economy has widened out over the last decade with sports, healthcare, logistics and tech-adjacent employers. More households arriving into a market with capped land supply produces exactly what June showed. Rising sales, thin inventory, firm prices.

There is also a psychological factor unique to this valley. Las Vegas was hit harder than almost anywhere in the 2008 crash. Values collapsed, foreclosures piled up, and neighborhoods emptied out. That memory is baked into how locals hear housing news. Any national headline with the word "fell" in it gets read here as the first domino. It usually is not, and June is a clean example of why.

National averages hide this kind of split by design. When the country posts a 2.4 percent decline, some metros fell far more than that and others rose. Averaging them together produces a single tidy number that describes no actual place. June simply made the spread visible, because Las Vegas landed on the opposite side of the average by a wide margin.

The 2.2 percent increase in single-family listings without an offer is the quiet number in this report. Inventory did rise, just barely. That tells you sellers are not flooding the market, and it explains why 18.3 percent more sales did not push supply down further.

What Happens Next

July and August are usually slower months in Southern Nevada. Families finish their moves before school starts, the heat pushes casual shoppers indoors, and closing volume typically eases. That seasonal dip is normal and should not be confused with a market turning. The number to watch is the year over year comparison, not the month to month one.

Las Vegas Realtors publishes its monthly report in the first week or so of each month, so the July local numbers should land in early August. The National Association of Realtors follows a similar monthly cadence with its existing home sales report. Reading both, side by side, is the single best habit a Clark County buyer or seller can build.

Watch three specific figures. First, whether single-family listings without an offer climb meaningfully above 7,147. A sharp jump would signal sellers getting nervous and would loosen the market. Second, whether months of supply moves off roughly three and a half. If it drifts toward five or six, negotiating power shifts toward buyers. Third, whether the $490,000 median holds, slips, or pushes higher.

Also keep an eye on what sellers do with unsold inventory. Homes that sit without an offer eventually become price reductions, expired listings, or rentals. If a large share of those 7,147 listings start cutting price this summer, that will show up in the median before it shows up in the sales count. Price reductions are usually the earliest honest signal that a market is cooling.

Mortgage rates remain the wild card everywhere. Rates affect the monthly payment far more than a small move in price does, and they shape how many buyers can qualify at all. A meaningful drop would likely bring sidelined buyers back into competition for that same thin inventory.

One last piece of context. June is one month. A single month never proves a trend, nationally or locally. What June does prove is that national and local markets can move in opposite directions at the same time, which is exactly the point worth remembering.

Ryan's Take

I get some version of "is the market crashing" almost every week, and it almost always traces back to a national headline someone saw. June 2026 is the cleanest example I have seen in a while of why that instinct leads people wrong. The country posted a 2.4 percent drop. Our valley posted an 18.3 percent year over year gain in single-family sales. Both facts are true. Only one of them is about your house.

The national number blends more than 400 metro areas into one figure. Las Vegas is a single line item inside that blend, and it got outvoted. When I actually help someone price a home or write an offer, the national number never enters the conversation. What matters is the zip code, the price band, the condition, and what comparable homes have done in the last 30 to 60 days.

The other thing I would say is that a strong sales month does not mean every seller wins. It means more buyers showed up. Homes that are priced correctly and presented well are the ones capturing that traffic. Overpriced listings still sit, in a busy market and a slow one alike, and those 7,147 homes waiting without an offer are proof that activity alone does not sell a house.

I will add one caution, because balance matters. Single-family and attached housing are not moving together right now, and a strong single-family number does not automatically mean a strong condo or townhome number. Ask about your specific property type before you draw conclusions from a valley-wide headline, including this one.

What You Can Do

Start by changing where you get your housing information. The Las Vegas Realtors monthly report is free, public, and specific to Southern Nevada. Reading it takes about five minutes and it will tell you more about your home's value than a week of national coverage. Pair it with the National Association of Realtors release if you want the wider context.

If you are selling, ask for neighborhood-level data before you set a price. Valley-wide medians are useful for understanding direction, but they do not price a specific house on a specific street. Pull comparable sales from the last 30 to 60 days within your immediate area, and pay attention to how long each one sat before it went under contract.

If you are buying, get fully pre-approved before you start touring homes. With roughly three and a half months of supply, well-priced homes in desirable pockets of Henderson, Summerlin and Centennial Hills still see competition. Know your maximum monthly payment, not just your maximum purchase price, because rates change that math more than list price does.

Talk to a lender before you talk to anyone else. Rates, loan programs and qualifying guidelines change more often than home prices do, and a conversation that costs you nothing can reset your entire budget. Ask specifically what you qualify for today, and what you would qualify for if rates moved half a point in either direction. Knowing both numbers keeps you from freezing when the market shifts.

And whichever side you are on, walk some open houses. Numbers in a report are a lagging picture of what already closed. Standing in a living room on a Saturday afternoon and counting how many other people walk through tells you what is happening right now.
