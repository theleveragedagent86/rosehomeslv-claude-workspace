New CCSD Police Chief Sworn In, Promises Tickets for Speeding in School Zones

The Clark County School District has a new police chief, and his first public message to Las Vegas drivers was a warning. Jeff Clark took the oath of office on Monday, July 13, and said plainly that his officers will write tickets to drivers who speed through active school zones.

Clark is a former Las Vegas Metropolitan Police Department captain with 21 years in law enforcement. He now leads the police department that covers more than 280,000 students and about 40,000 staff members across 499 campuses in Clark County.

The timing is what makes this news you can use. A new Nevada law took effect on July 1 that doubles both the fines and the license points for violations in active school zones. School starts back on August 10. That leaves Las Vegas parents, commuters, and delivery drivers a short window to change their habits before the buses roll and the crossing guards return to the corners.

His words left almost no room for interpretation. "If we are there and you're speeding through a school zone, we will hold you accountable," Clark said. "We will be writing tickets."

This is not a quiet policy note buried in a school board packet. It is a public promise from the person in charge, made a few weeks before the first bell of the new school year.

What Happened

Jeff Clark was sworn in as chief of the Clark County School District Police Department on Monday, July 13, 2026. Superintendent Jhone Ebert administered the oath of office. The ceremony was held at Cimarron Memorial High School in northwest Las Vegas, which is Clark's own alma mater.

That detail is not just a nice touch for the photos. Clark grew up here and went to a CCSD school. He is now running the police department that protects the district he graduated from. In a valley where a lot of leadership roles get filled by people from out of state, a hometown hire tends to land differently with parents.

Clark comes to the job after 21 years in law enforcement, most recently as a captain with the Las Vegas Metropolitan Police Department. That is a long run in a big-city agency, and it means he already knows the valley's roads, its traffic patterns, and the neighborhoods where school zones back up against six-lane arterials.

The scope of the job is enormous. The CCSD Police Department is responsible for safety across 499 campuses. That covers more than 280,000 students and roughly 40,000 employees. Very few school police departments in the country operate at that scale. It is bigger than the entire police force of many mid-sized American cities.

When Clark talked about his priorities, traffic enforcement came up right away. He said traffic safety is top of mind for him going into the school year, and then he made the point in language nobody could misread. "If we are there and you're speeding through a school zone, we will hold you accountable. We will be writing tickets."

The enforcement message pairs with a change in state law. A new Nevada law that took effect on July 1 doubles both the fine and the license points for violations committed in an active school zone. So the same behavior that would have cost a driver one amount in June now costs double, and the hit to the driving record is double as well.

Notice the word "active." Nevada school zone rules apply during the hours posted on the sign at each zone, which are the windows around drop-off and pickup when kids are actually walking. Outside those hours, the regular speed limit applies. Inside them, the new doubled penalties are in play.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

If you drive anywhere in this valley between about 7 a.m. and 8 a.m. or 2 p.m. and 3 p.m. on a weekday, this affects you. Clark County built its neighborhoods around schools, which means school zones sit on some of the busiest surface streets we have. You do not have to be a parent to drive through three of them on your way to work.

Think about the routes. Rainbow, Fort Apache, Buffalo, Eastern, Charleston, Sahara, Green Valley Parkway, Craig Road. There are elementary and middle schools sitting right on those roads across Summerlin, Spring Valley, Centennial Hills, Henderson, North Las Vegas, and the southwest. Drivers who are running late tend to treat those stretches like the rest of the arterial. Starting this school year, that habit is more expensive.

The doubled fine is the part that gets attention, but the doubled license points may be the bigger long-term problem. Points stay on your Nevada driving record and follow you. Enough of them can put your license at risk, and insurance companies price your policy off your record. A single school zone ticket could raise what you pay for car insurance for years, which is real money on top of the ticket itself.

For parents, the pickup line is where this gets personal. Every school in the valley has that one parent who rolls through the zone at 30 to beat the line, or who does a U-turn across a double yellow to grab the good spot. Those moves have always been risky. Now they are risky and expensive, and the chief just told everyone officers will be out there writing.

There is also the safety side, which is the whole point of the law. Kids walk to school here. Kids ride bikes and e-bikes to school here. A car doing 35 in a zone posted much lower gives a driver almost no time to react to a fourth grader stepping off a curb. Clark County has already seen far too many students struck by vehicles, and enforcement is one of the few levers a police chief actually controls.

Homeowners near campuses feel this in another way. If you live on a street that turns into a parking lot twice a day, more consistent enforcement helps. Speeding cut-through traffic through residential streets near schools is one of the most common complaints that comes up at neighborhood meetings across the valley, and it is one of the first things buyers ask about when they tour a home near a campus.

It hits working drivers too. Rideshare drivers, delivery vans, contractors heading to a job site, and anyone with a route that changes daily are the people most likely to hit a school zone they were not expecting. If you drive for a living in Clark County, a doubled points penalty is not just an annoyance. Depending on your job, your driving record is part of your employment, and one bad morning near a campus can create a much larger problem.

Background and History

School zone safety has been building as a local issue for a while. It did not start with this swearing-in ceremony. It has been a steady drumbeat of crashes, injuries, community meetings, and policy fixes over the past several years.

Earlier this summer, a Clark County task force released 75 school traffic safety recommendations after 427 students were hit by vehicles. That is a staggering number for one community, and it is the kind of figure that pushes lawmakers to act. You can read more about that work in our breakdown of the Clark County school traffic safety plan.

At the same time, e-bikes and e-scooters have completely changed how a lot of Clark County kids get to school. Local trauma doctors have reported more than 700 e-bike and e-scooter injuries this year. More kids are moving through school zones at 20 miles per hour on two wheels than ever before, and drivers are still adjusting to that. Our post on the rise in Las Vegas e-bike injuries covers what doctors are seeing.

The new state law that doubled school zone fines and points is the legislative answer to that pressure. Lawmakers concluded that the old penalty was not changing behavior, so they raised the cost. The law took effect July 1, several weeks before students returned, which gave the public some runway to learn about it.

Leadership at CCSD police has also been in transition. Bringing in a veteran LVMPD captain signals that the district wants someone who can operate at the scale of a major metropolitan agency and coordinate with Metro, Henderson Police, and North Las Vegas Police, since CCSD campuses sit inside all of those jurisdictions.

What is new here is not the concern. It is the combination. Tougher penalties, a fresh chief, a public commitment to enforcement, and a task force list of fixes all landed within a few weeks of each other. That is more alignment than this issue has had in a long time.

What Happens Next

The first date to circle is August 10. That is the first day of the 2026-27 school year, and it is when every school zone in the valley switches back on. Expect traffic to change overnight on your normal commute, especially on the arterials that run past elementary schools.

There is a second wrinkle this year. Every CCSD school is getting a new bell time for 2026-27, so the hours you memorized last spring may no longer be right. If your drive used to miss the school rush by ten minutes, it might not anymore. Our guide to CCSD registration and the new bell times walks through what families need to handle before day one.

Watch the first two weeks of the school year closely. That is typically when enforcement is heaviest and when drivers are least used to the new patterns. A chief who publicly promises tickets before the year starts is generally planning to make that promise visible early, because the point of the message is deterrence, not revenue.

Over the fall, watch how many of the county task force's 75 recommendations actually get funded and built. Enforcement is only one piece. Crosswalk upgrades, better lighting, flashing beacons, sidewalk gaps, and signal timing are the physical fixes that reduce crashes even when no officer is parked nearby. Those items move through county and city budget processes, so they show up on public agendas.

Also worth watching is staffing. A department covering 499 campuses can only put so many officers on traffic detail at 7:15 a.m. If the district adds positions or shifts assignments toward traffic enforcement, that will tell you how serious the commitment is beyond opening day.

One more thing to track is how CCSD police coordinate with the other agencies in the valley. A school in Henderson sits in Henderson Police jurisdiction, a school off Craig Road sits in North Las Vegas, and most of the rest fall under Metro or county patrol. School zone enforcement works best when all of those agencies are pushing the same message on the same mornings, so joint operations in August would be a good sign.

Ryan's Take

I show homes near schools every single week, and I can tell you that traffic is one of the top three questions buyers ask when a listing sits within a few blocks of a campus. They want to know how bad the drop-off backup gets, whether cars cut through the neighborhood, and whether their kid can safely walk or ride to class. Those answers move offers.

Here is the part people miss. A home near a good school is usually worth more, but a home on the wrong side of the drop-off route can be a harder sell. The difference between those two outcomes often comes down to whether the street feels safe at 7:30 in the morning. Real enforcement and real infrastructure change how that street feels, and over time that shows up in what buyers will pay.

So while a new police chief promising tickets sounds like a story only parents care about, I read it as a neighborhood quality story. Streets where drivers actually slow down are streets where families let their kids walk. Those are the neighborhoods that hold their value and keep drawing buyers with young children, which is a big share of the Clark County market.

My honest advice to anyone reading this: do not treat the doubled fine as the reason to slow down. Treat it as the nudge. The real reason is that August mornings in Las Vegas are bright, hot, and chaotic, and there are kids on foot everywhere at 7 a.m.

What You Can Do

Start with the simplest fix, which is leaving earlier. Most school zone speeding is not aggressive driving. It is people running eight minutes behind. Add ten minutes to your morning for the first two weeks of school and most of the problem solves itself.

Next, check your actual route. Pull up your commute and find every school you pass. Look at the posted school zone hours on those signs, because they vary by campus and the district changed bell times this year. If a zone is active during your drive, plan for it instead of discovering it at 7:20 a.m.

If you have kids, walk or drive the route with them before the first day. Point out where drivers turn without looking, where the sidewalk stops, and where the safest crossing is. If your child rides an e-bike or scooter, set the rule that they cross at the crosswalk on foot, not riding through the intersection.

It also helps to know what an active school zone actually looks like where you drive. Some are marked with flashing beacons, some rely on the hours printed on the sign, and some sit right next to a crosswalk with a guard posted. Take thirty seconds at the zones on your route to read the sign instead of guessing. The doubled penalty only applies while that zone is active, and most drivers have honestly never read one of those signs closely.

Finally, speak up about the spots that scare you. If there is an intersection near your neighborhood school with a bad sight line, a burned-out light, or a missing crosswalk, report it to your city or to Clark County, and bring it up at a school board or council meeting. The task force list came out of exactly that kind of public pressure, and specific addresses from residents are what get those fixes prioritized.
