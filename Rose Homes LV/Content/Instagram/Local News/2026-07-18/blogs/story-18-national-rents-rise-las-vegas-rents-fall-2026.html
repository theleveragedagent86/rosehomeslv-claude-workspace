National Rents Are Climbing Again This Summer. Las Vegas Rents Are Falling

Rents across the country went up again in June 2026. Rents in Las Vegas went down. That is the headline, and if you rent an apartment anywhere in Clark County, it is the most useful thing you will read this week.

Apartment List reported that the national median rent rose 0.4 percent in June to $1,385. That was the fifth month in a row that national rents climbed. Meanwhile, RentCafe data shows the average apartment rent in Las Vegas sitting at $1,464, down 0.63 percent from $1,473 a year ago. The units most people actually rent fell even harder. One-bedroom rents dropped 4.67 percent year over year to $1,119. Two-bedroom rents dropped 4.12 percent to $1,550.

So the national trend and the Las Vegas trend are pointing in opposite directions right now. National rents are rising month over month. Las Vegas rents are lower than they were last summer. If you have a lease renewal coming up, that gap is your leverage.

One thing to keep straight before we go further. The national number and the Las Vegas number are not measured the same way, so you cannot line them up side by side and draw a conclusion. We will explain exactly why in the next section, because it changes how you should read every rent headline you see for the rest of the year.

What Happened

Apartment List released its July 2026 national rent report covering June data. The national median rent came in at $1,385, up 0.4 percent from May. That marks five straight monthly increases heading into the peak summer moving season. Summer is normally when rents rise, because more people move between May and August than any other stretch of the year.

But the report carried a second number that got less attention. Even after five months of gains, the national median rent is still down 1.2 percent compared to a year ago. So rents are climbing off a lower base. The country spent much of the past two years working through a wave of new apartment construction, and that supply pushed rents down. The recent monthly gains have not erased the annual decline yet.

Apartment List also reported the national multifamily vacancy rate at 7.2 percent. Here is what made that figure notable. Vacancy is falling for the first time in more than four years. For most of the period since 2022, vacancy climbed as new buildings opened faster than renters filled them. A falling vacancy rate means landlords nationally are getting more of their units leased, and that is usually the signal that rent increases are coming.

Las Vegas is running its own race. RentCafe puts the average apartment rent across the metro at $1,464. A year ago that same average was $1,473. That is a decline of 0.63 percent. Small, but it is a decline, and it comes at a time when the national figure is moving up.

Break it down by unit size and the local picture gets clearer. One-bedroom apartments in Las Vegas average $1,119, down 4.67 percent from a year ago. Two-bedroom apartments average $1,550, down 4.12 percent. Those two categories cover the large majority of renters in Clark County. A drop of more than 4 percent on a one-bedroom is roughly $55 a month, or about $660 a year back in a renter's pocket.

Now the part you need to understand. The national figure of $1,385 is a median published by Apartment List. The Las Vegas figure of $1,464 is an average published by RentCafe. Those are two different math problems. A median is the middle value in a list. An average adds everything up and divides. Averages get pulled higher by expensive units at the top of the market. Medians do not.

That means you should not read $1,464 versus $1,385 and conclude that Las Vegas rents are higher than the national number. The two figures come from different companies, use different property samples, and use different math. The honest comparison here is the direction of change, not the dollar gap. National rents are moving up. Las Vegas rents are moving down. That comparison holds. The dollar-to-dollar comparison does not.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Clark County is a renter's county. A very large share of households here rent instead of own, especially in the central valley, the east side, North Las Vegas, and the apartment corridors near the resort corridor. When national rent headlines say prices are climbing, thousands of local renters read that and quietly accept whatever increase their landlord puts in front of them. They assume the increase is normal and unavoidable.

The local data says otherwise. If one-bedroom rents across the metro are down 4.67 percent from a year ago, and your landlord hands you a renewal with a 5 percent increase, that increase is running against the local market. You are allowed to say so. You are allowed to ask for a different number, or ask for a longer lease term at the current rate, or ask for concessions like a free month or waived parking.

This matters most for the households where a $60 or $80 monthly swing is real money. A worker on the Strip, a nurse in Green Valley, a teacher in Centennial Hills, a family in Mountains Edge. For those households, an extra $700 a year decides whether they can build any savings at all. Renewal season is not a formality. It is a negotiation, and right now the local numbers are on the renter's side.

It also matters for anyone weighing renting against buying. The Las Vegas median home price recently set records, and mortgage rates have been sitting near multi-month highs. When rents fall while home prices and rates stay high, the math tilts toward renting a little longer for some households. That is not universal advice, and it depends entirely on your down payment, your timeline, and how long you plan to stay. But it is a real factor worth running the numbers on instead of guessing.

And it matters for small landlords. Plenty of Clark County rentals are owned by regular people with one or two properties, not by big institutions. If you own a rental here, softer rents mean you should be thinking hard about tenant retention. A good tenant who stays another year at the same rate is usually worth more than a vacancy and a turnover cost while you chase a higher number the market may not support.

There is one more group this affects, and people forget about them. Anyone moving to Las Vegas this summer for a job. New arrivals often price their housing budget using national articles before they get here. If someone budgeted around the national median and then shopped only luxury new construction, they walk away thinking Las Vegas is expensive. If they shop the full market, including the older properties competing with the new buildings, they find a very different picture. Where you shop inside the valley changes your number more than the metro average ever will.

Background and History

To understand why Las Vegas is moving the opposite direction from the country, you have to look at what got built here.

Las Vegas went through a serious apartment building boom coming out of the pandemic years. Developers saw a fast-growing metro, strong in-migration, and rents that had spiked hard in 2021 and 2022. They responded the way developers always do. They built. Projects broke ground across the southwest valley, the northwest, Henderson, and North Las Vegas.

Apartment buildings take two to three years to go from groundbreaking to move-in. That lag is why supply always arrives late to the party. The units approved when rents were spiking are the units opening now, when the urgency has cooled. Roughly 4,200 new multifamily units are forecast to deliver across the Las Vegas metro in 2026. That is a meaningful amount of new inventory hitting the market in a single year.

When several hundred new units open in a submarket at once, those buildings have to fill up. New properties lease up by offering deals. A month free. Reduced deposits. Waived application fees. Those concessions do not always show up in the headline rent figure, but they absolutely pull the effective rent down. And once a new building down the street is offering a month free, the older properties nearby have to compete or watch their tenants leave.

That is the mechanism holding Las Vegas rents in check right now. It is not a mystery and it is not a sign of a broken market. It is supply doing what supply does. The rest of the country went through a similar wave earlier, which is exactly why the national median is still down 1.2 percent year over year even after five months of increases. Las Vegas is just running a bit behind the national cycle.

What Happens Next

The national picture is the easier one to forecast. With vacancy at 7.2 percent and falling for the first time in over four years, the country is running out of the excess supply that pushed rents down. Fewer new projects broke ground in 2024 and 2025 than in the peak years. That means less new inventory arriving in 2027 and 2028. If demand holds steady and supply thins out, national rents should keep climbing.

Las Vegas has a window before that catches up. The roughly 4,200 units delivering in 2026 still have to lease up, and lease-up takes months. As long as new properties are competing for tenants with concessions, the pressure on local rents stays soft. That window likely runs through the rest of 2026 and into 2027, depending on how quickly those units fill.

What could close the window faster? Job growth. Las Vegas is still adding people, and every new household needs somewhere to live. If in-migration stays strong while the construction pipeline thins, the 4,200 units get absorbed quicker than expected and local rents turn around. Watch the delivery numbers for 2027. If they drop off sharply from 4,200, that is your signal that the soft patch is ending.

The other thing to watch is the for-sale market. High mortgage rates keep would-be buyers in rentals longer, which props up rental demand. If rates fall meaningfully, some of those renters become buyers, and that pulls demand out of the apartment market. That would extend the soft rent period here even further.

Keep an eye on the concessions too, because they move before the headline rent does. When new properties stop offering a free month and start charging full price on day one, that is the earliest warning that landlords feel demand returning. Concessions disappearing across the southwest valley or Henderson would tell you more about where local rents are headed than any national report.

The next Apartment List national report will show whether June's increase became a sixth straight month. Local RentCafe figures update regularly too. Both are free to check, and checking them once a quarter takes about five minutes. If you rent in Clark County, that five minutes is worth real money at renewal time.

Ryan's Take

I have watched people talk themselves into bad decisions using national headlines for years now. Someone reads that rents are climbing across the country, panics, and signs a renewal at a number they never should have accepted. Or they rush to buy a house before they are ready because they think renting is about to get unaffordable. Neither move is based on what is actually happening in Clark County.

Las Vegas real estate is local. Always has been. Our supply pipeline, our job market, our in-migration patterns, and our construction cycle are not the national average. Right now that works in favor of renters here, and I would rather people know that than get spooked by a chart about the whole country.

The thing I keep telling people is to be careful with the numbers themselves. A median from one company and an average from another are not interchangeable, and a lot of the confusion I hear from clients comes from comparing two figures that were never meant to be compared. Use the direction of change. Direction is honest. The dollar gap between two differently measured numbers is noise.

If you are renting in Las Vegas right now, you have more room to negotiate than you did two years ago. Use it. If you are a small landlord, protect your good tenants. And if you are trying to figure out whether this is the year to stop renting and buy, run the actual math on your actual situation instead of trusting a headline written for someone living somewhere else.

What You Can Do

If your lease is up in the next few months, start early. Look up what comparable units in your own complex and in the two or three complexes nearest you are asking right now. Check listing sites, and call the leasing office of a competitor to ask about current specials. Write the numbers down. That is your negotiating file.

When the renewal offer arrives, respond in writing. Be polite and be specific. Point to the comparable units you found, mention that one-bedroom rents across the metro are down more than 4 percent year over year, and ask for a rate that reflects the market. If the landlord will not move on rent, ask for something else instead. A longer term at the current rate protects you if rents turn around. Waived fees, free parking, or a covered maintenance item all have real value.

Remember that turnover is expensive for the property. An empty unit, a make-ready, a new listing, and a leasing commission usually cost more than the increase they are asking you for. A tenant who pays on time and takes care of the place has leverage, especially in a market with new inventory opening nearby.

If you are shopping for a new place, ask every single property about move-in specials. In a year with roughly 4,200 new units delivering, concessions are common and they are often not advertised on the listing. You have to ask.

And if you are weighing renting against buying, get real numbers before you decide. Compare your actual renewal offer to a real monthly payment on a real price point, including taxes, insurance, and any HOA. Guessing is how people end up in the wrong housing for years.
