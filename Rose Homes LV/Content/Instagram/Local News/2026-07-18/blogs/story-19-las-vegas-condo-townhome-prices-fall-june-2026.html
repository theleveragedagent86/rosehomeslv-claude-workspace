Las Vegas Condo and Townhome Prices Fall 4.3% While Houses Set Records

The median price for an existing condo or townhome in Southern Nevada fell to $292,000 in June 2026, down 4.3 percent from a year earlier. In that exact same month, in that exact same market, the median price for an existing single-family home set a record at $490,000.

That is not a mistake. Two halves of the Las Vegas housing market are moving in opposite directions right now. Houses are climbing to new highs while attached homes are giving money back. The condo and townhome median is now about $23,000 below its October 2024 peak of $315,000, and it sits roughly $13,000 under where it was last June.

For anyone who has been priced out of a Clark County house, this is the first real opening in a long time. It is also the part of the market that comes with the most homework. Falling prices are only good news if you understand what you are buying, and condos in Las Vegas carry costs and rules that single-family homes simply do not have.

What Happened in the June 2026 Numbers

Las Vegas REALTORS released its June 2026 market report in early July, and the split between the two housing types was the headline nobody expected. The association tracks sales through its Multiple Listing Service, and it reports single-family homes and attached homes as separate categories. This month those two categories told two completely different stories.

On the attached side, which covers condos, townhomes, and similar properties, the median sales price landed at $292,000. That is a 4.3 percent drop compared to June 2025. It is also a meaningful step down from the segment's high water mark. In October 2024, the attached median reached $315,000. Prices have been drifting since then, and June's figure puts the segment about 7 percent below that peak.

Inventory tells the same story from a different angle. At the end of June, Southern Nevada had 2,690 condos and townhomes listed for sale without any offer on them. That is up 4.9 percent from a year earlier. In plain terms, more attached homes are sitting on the market unsold, and the pile is growing faster on the condo side than it is on the single-family side.

Meanwhile, the detached market went the other way. The median price for an existing single-family home in Southern Nevada hit $490,000 in June, a record for the local market. Buyers competing for houses are still running into short supply and stubborn pricing, while buyers looking at condos are finding more choices and more room to negotiate.

The gap between the two medians is now $198,000. Put another way, the typical Las Vegas condo or townhome sells for about 60 cents on the dollar compared to the typical Las Vegas house. That spread has widened over the past two years, and June pushed it wider still.

One thing worth remembering about that $292,000 figure is that it blends very different properties together. The attached category includes a modest two bedroom townhome off Eastern Avenue, a suburban condo in a gated Henderson community, and a high rise unit near the Strip. Those three properties have almost nothing in common in terms of price, dues, or buyer pool. A median is a useful summary, but it is not a price tag for any particular building. Your own condo may be doing better or worse than the countywide number suggests.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

For first-time buyers in Clark County, this is the most useful number in the entire June report. A $292,000 purchase price is a completely different conversation than a $490,000 purchase price. The down payment is smaller. The monthly principal and interest payment is smaller. The income you need to qualify is smaller. For a lot of local renters, a condo is the only path to ownership that math currently allows.

It also matters because the leverage has shifted. When 2,690 attached homes are sitting without offers, sellers start paying attention. Buyers in that segment can ask for closing cost help, repairs, or a rate buydown and actually get a yes. That is not the experience buyers are having on the single-family side, where multiple offers on well-priced houses are still common in neighborhoods like Summerlin, Green Valley, and Centennial Hills.

Renters should watch this too. Southern Nevada rents have been soft, and a growing pile of unsold condos usually means some of those units end up back on the rental market. More rental supply generally keeps a lid on what landlords can charge. If you are renewing a lease in Henderson or the southwest valley this year, the condo inventory number is quietly working in your favor.

Downsizers should look closely as well. Plenty of Clark County homeowners in their sixties and seventies are sitting on a paid off or nearly paid off house worth close to that record $490,000. Selling into a strong detached market and buying into a soft attached market is about as favorable a trade as this valley has offered in years. A lock and leave townhome with no yard to water in July has real appeal for anyone who travels or who is tired of maintenance.

Current condo owners are on the other side of this trade. If you bought an attached home in Las Vegas near the October 2024 peak, you are probably close to flat or slightly underwater on paper once you account for selling costs. That does not mean you made a bad decision, especially if you plan to stay put. It does mean that selling in the next year or two will likely require patience and realistic pricing rather than the quick, easy sale your neighbors with houses are getting.

The Honest Part: What Condo Buyers Need to Check

Falling prices are not automatically a bargain. A condo or townhome comes with an HOA, and the HOA comes with a monthly bill that never goes away. In Clark County, attached home dues commonly run from roughly $150 to well over $400 a month depending on the community, and high rise buildings near the Strip can run far higher. That payment is on top of your mortgage, taxes, and insurance, and lenders count it against you when they qualify you. A $292,000 condo with $400 dues can carry a bigger monthly obligation than a $340,000 townhome with $120 dues.

Some Las Vegas communities also sit inside a master association, which means you pay a sub-HOA and a master HOA. Always ask for both numbers, not just the one on the listing.

Then there are special assessments. When an association has not saved enough for a new roof, new pipes, fresh paint, or elevator work, it can vote to charge every owner a one time amount. Those bills can run into the thousands. Nevada law requires the seller to give you a resale package with the association's budget, reserve study, rules, and financial statements, and you get a short window after receiving it to cancel the deal. Read that package. A thin reserve fund is the single biggest warning sign in a condo purchase, and it is sitting right there in the paperwork.

Financing is the third piece. Condo loans are approved at the project level, not just the borrower level. FHA and VA both maintain approved condo lists, and if a community is not on it, those loan programs are off the table. Conventional lenders review the association's insurance, reserves, deferred maintenance, litigation, delinquency rate, and how many units are owned by investors rather than residents. A building that fails those checks becomes hard to finance, which shrinks the buyer pool and is often part of why the price looks so attractive. Before you fall in love with a unit, have your lender confirm the project qualifies.

Background and History

Las Vegas has a long and complicated relationship with attached housing. During the mid 2000s boom, developers built condos and converted apartments at a furious pace, and much of that supply landed in the same few corridors. When the market turned in 2008, condos fell harder and recovered slower than houses. Plenty of longtime locals still remember that stretch, and it shapes how buyers here think about the segment.

The segment recovered strongly during the 2020 through 2022 run, when low rates and remote work pulled buyers into anything they could afford. Attached homes were the affordable option, and prices climbed fast. The peak arrived in October 2024 at a $315,000 median. Since then, the trend has slowly reversed while single-family prices kept grinding higher.

Two things changed after that peak. First, mortgage rates stayed high longer than most people expected, and higher rates hit the entry level buyer hardest. That buyer is the condo buyer. Second, the cost of owning an attached home went up in ways that have nothing to do with the purchase price. Insurance premiums for multi unit buildings rose across the country, and lenders tightened their review of association reserves and deferred maintenance after the 2021 Surfside collapse in Florida. Associations responded by raising dues and funding reserves more aggressively. Every dollar of that increase reduces what a buyer can borrow.

Single-family homes avoided most of that pressure. Clark County has limited developable land because the federal government controls so much of the surrounding acreage, and that constraint keeps detached supply tight. Tight supply plus steady demand is how you get a record $490,000 median in the middle of a high rate environment. The two segments are not really competing in the same market anymore.

What Happens Next

Watch the inventory number first. Las Vegas REALTORS releases its report each month, usually in the first two weeks. If the count of condos and townhomes listed without offers keeps climbing past 2,690 through the late summer, expect the median to keep sliding. If that count flattens or falls, June may end up looking like the bottom of this stretch.

Watch mortgage rates second. The attached segment is the most rate sensitive part of the Las Vegas market because its buyers have the least cushion. A meaningful drop in rates would bring entry level buyers back quickly, and 2,690 unsold units would not last long against a wave of returning demand. A move in the other direction would extend the current softness into the fall.

Also watch the HOA dues trend. Associations across the valley are still working through insurance increases and reserve funding requirements. If dues keep rising faster than incomes, that alone can hold condo prices down even if rates improve. The purchase price is only half of what a buyer can afford, and the monthly obligation is the half that has been moving in the wrong direction.

Finally, keep an eye on the gap. A $198,000 spread between the typical house and the typical attached home is historically wide for this market. Gaps that wide tend to pull value shoppers toward the cheaper side eventually. That does not mean it corrects this year, but it does mean the condo segment is where the value argument currently lives.

Ryan's Take

I have shown a lot of condos and townhomes this year, and the mood is completely different from the single-family side. On a house, my buyers are bracing for competition. On a condo, we are asking for concessions and getting them. That is a real advantage, and I think buyers who are getting frustrated with the house market should at least look at what their budget buys in the attached segment right now.

That said, I will not let a client buy a condo on price alone. The first thing I do is pull the HOA dues and add them to the payment, because that is the number that actually determines what you can afford. The second thing is read the reserve study. I have walked buyers away from units that looked like great deals because the association had almost nothing saved and a roof replacement coming. The discount you get at closing means nothing if you get a $9,000 assessment two years later.

My honest read is that this is a good window for a buyer who plans to stay five years or more, who has checked the financials, and who is buying a well run community. It is a rough window for someone who needs to sell an attached home quickly. Both of those things can be true in the same market, and in Las Vegas right now they are.

What You Can Do

If you are thinking about buying, start with a full payment estimate rather than a purchase price. Ask your lender to include the HOA dues, property taxes, and insurance in the qualification, then compare that total against a similar single-family payment. Sometimes the condo wins by a wide margin. Sometimes it barely wins at all. You will not know until you run both.

Once you are under contract, treat the HOA resale package like an inspection report. Look for the reserve balance, the percentage of dues going into reserves, any pending or recent special assessments, the delinquency rate among owners, and any active litigation. Nevada gives you a cancellation window after you receive that package for exactly this reason. Use it. If the numbers look thin, walk and find a better community.

If you already own a condo or townhome here, do not panic over a 4.3 percent move. Attach a real number to it by getting a current valuation, then decide based on your timeline rather than the headline. If you are staying, this year barely matters. If you need to sell in the next twelve months, price it correctly out of the gate, because 2,690 competing listings will punish an overpriced one.

You can also follow the data yourself. Las Vegas REALTORS publishes its monthly report publicly, and reading it a few months in a row will tell you more about this market than any national headline.
