Tourism Still Drives $50.8 Billion Into Southern Nevada Despite Visitor Drop

Visitors spent $50.8 billion in Southern Nevada last year. That is still a staggering amount of money, and it is also $4.3 billion less than the year before. Both of those things are true at the same time, and that is the whole story.

The numbers came from Jeremy Aguero, a principal at the local research firm Applied Analysis. He presented the findings to the Las Vegas Convention and Visitors Authority Board of Directors this week. His report showed that tourism is still the engine of this valley, and it also showed that the engine ran a little cooler than it did in 2024.

For people who live here, this is not an abstract economics lesson. One in five jobs in this region is directly tied to tourism. If you work a shift on the Strip, wait tables in Henderson, drive a rideshare, clean rooms, or run a small business that serves any of those workers, this report is describing your paycheck. Here is what the numbers say, what they do not say, and how much weight to put on them.

What Happened at the LVCVA Board Meeting

Applied Analysis principal Jeremy Aguero laid out the annual economic impact picture for the LVCVA Board of Directors. The headline figure was total visitor spending in Southern Nevada of $50.8 billion for the year. That covers hotel rooms, food, drinks, shows, gaming, shopping, transportation, and everything else people buy while they are here.

Broken down per person, visitors spent an average of $1,318 per trip. That average includes the couple who flew in for a weekend and the convention attendee who stayed four nights on a corporate card. Divide the $50.8 billion by that $1,318 average and you land in the neighborhood of 38 million trips for the year, which lines up with the scale of this market.

The comparison year is where the story gets more complicated. Total visitor spending in 2024 came in around $55.1 billion. That means last year was down about $4.3 billion. Visitation itself fell by nearly 8 percent. Fewer people came, and the total they spent went down with them.

The jobs figures are the part locals should sit with. Tourism directly supported about 250,630 jobs in Southern Nevada, which works out to roughly one in five jobs in the entire region. When you add indirect impacts, meaning the jobs that exist because tourism workers and tourism businesses spend money here, the total climbs to roughly 382,320 jobs. That is closer to one in three.

The total economic output tied to tourism was about $81 billion, or roughly 40 percent of the regional economy. That is a remarkable concentration. Very few metro areas in the country have a single industry carrying that much of the load. It is a strength when things are going well and a risk when they are not.

It helps to understand the difference between those two big dollar figures. The $50.8 billion is what visitors actually spent. The $81 billion in output is bigger because it counts the ripple. A hotel buys linens and food. A restaurant hires a plumber. A tour operator leases vans. Every one of those transactions is economic activity that would not exist without a visitor starting the chain.

The per person number deserves attention too, because it separates two different questions. If spending falls only because fewer people came, that is a volume problem. If spending falls because each visitor is spending less, that is a value problem, and it is harder to fix with marketing. The $1,318 average is the figure that answers that question year over year, so it is the one to track going forward.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Start with the obvious. A drop in visitor spending shows up first in hours and tips, not in layoffs. When a resort sees fewer guests, the shift schedule gets thinner before anybody loses a job. Servers pick up fewer tables. Bartenders see slower nights. Housekeepers get sent home early. A worker can keep the exact same job title and still take home meaningfully less money.

That matters across the whole valley, not just on Las Vegas Boulevard. Hospitality workers live in Spring Valley, Sunrise Manor, North Las Vegas, Paradise, Winchester, Henderson, and out in Mountain's Edge and Aliante. The paycheck is earned on the Strip and it is spent at the grocery store on Rainbow, the auto shop on Boulder Highway, and the youth sports league in Green Valley. That is exactly what the indirect job number is measuring.

Now the honest part about housing, because this is where people tend to overreach. Local jobs and local incomes are real drivers of the housing market. Lenders qualify buyers on income. Families decide whether to move up or stay put based on how secure they feel at work. So a $4.3 billion drop in visitor spending is genuinely worth watching if you own a home here or plan to buy one.

But a spending dip in one year does not automatically mean home prices fall. Housing in Clark County is also pushed and pulled by mortgage rates, by how many homes are for sale, by builders and their pace of construction, by people moving in from California and other states, and by investor activity. Tourism is one input among several. Anyone who tells you a tourism number predicts your home value is selling you certainty that nobody actually has.

There is one practical wrinkle worth knowing. Households that rely on tip income or variable hours can find the mortgage process harder when their recent earnings dip. Lenders often look at a two year average of income for variable pay. A slower year on the floor can affect what a buyer qualifies for even if their job never changed. That is a real, specific way this report can touch a family's plans.

Renters feel this differently than owners do. A renter whose hours got cut still owes the same amount on the first of the month. Rent does not flex with your shift schedule. For a lot of valley households, a softer tourism year shows up as a tighter rent-to-income ratio long before it shows up in any housing statistic, and that pressure is invisible in a report about billions of dollars.

Small business owners sit in the middle of all this. The dry cleaner near a casino employee parking lot, the family restaurant that fills up after shift change, the nail salon that serves servers and bartenders, all of them depend on tourism money that has already passed through somebody else's paycheck first. When the 382,320 number moves, those businesses feel it in the same week their customers do.

Background and History

Las Vegas has been a tourism town for generations, but the scale of the current numbers is relatively modern. The convention business, the megaresort building boom, and the shift from gambling revenue to entertainment, dining, and event revenue all pushed total visitor spending far past what casino floors alone ever produced. The $1,318 average per trip reflects a visitor who is buying much more than table games.

Locals who lived through 2008 have a reason to be jumpy about numbers like these. In that downturn, tourism and construction fell together, unemployment in the valley climbed to among the worst in the nation, and the housing market collapsed. That memory is why a headline about a $4.3 billion decline lands harder here than a similar headline would land in Phoenix or Denver.

It is worth saying clearly that the current picture is not that picture. In 2008 the local economy lost jobs by the tens of thousands and home values fell by more than half in many neighborhoods. What Aguero described this week is a large industry that shrank from a record level while still supporting hundreds of thousands of jobs and still producing about 40 percent of regional output. Those are different situations.

The recent context also includes a run of very strong years. Las Vegas added the Formula 1 Las Vegas Grand Prix in November 2023 and hosted Super Bowl LVIII in February 2024. Big new attractions and marquee events pulled in high spending crowds and pushed the totals to unusual highs. When a market sets records, the following year has a very tall bar to clear.

Diversifying away from that 40 percent concentration has been a stated goal of local leaders for well over a decade. Professional sports arrived and stuck. Health care, logistics, and warehousing grew. More recently, data centers became part of the conversation about what else this valley could build its tax base on. Progress has been real, and tourism still carries 40 percent of regional output, which tells you how far there is to go.

The report itself measures what happened. It does not settle the argument about why. Locals have plenty of theories about cost, value, resort fees, parking charges, international travel, and the broader economy. Those debates are worth having, but they should be labeled as debates rather than findings.

What Happens Next

The LVCVA does not wait a full year to tell you how things are going. The authority publishes visitor statistics on a monthly basis, including visitor volume, hotel occupancy, average daily room rate, and convention attendance. Those monthly reports are the fastest read on whether last year's decline is leveling off, continuing, or reversing.

The LVCVA Board of Directors also meets regularly, and those meetings are public. Agendas and materials are posted in advance. If you want to see the same slides the board saw rather than a summary of them, that is where to look. Room tax dollars help fund the authority and other public needs in Nevada, so the board's decisions about marketing and convention investment are spending your community's money.

Watch the convention calendar as closely as the tourist numbers. Convention and trade show attendance tends to be steadier than leisure travel, and those visitors book midweek rooms that leisure travelers do not fill. A strong convention year can offset a soft leisure year without the headline visitor count moving much at all.

On the employment side, the Nevada Department of Employment, Training and Rehabilitation publishes monthly labor data for the Las Vegas metro area. Leisure and hospitality employment is broken out in that data. If the tourism slowdown is turning into an actual job problem rather than an hours problem, it will show up there before it shows up in any annual report.

The honest answer about the next twelve months is that nobody knows yet. A decline in one year can be the start of a trend or it can be a return to normal after two record breaking years. Those two scenarios look identical from inside a single data point. The next few months of monthly reports are what will separate them, and anyone claiming certainty right now is guessing with confidence.

Ryan's Take

I think the most useful thing anyone can do with this report is resist the urge to pick a side. The people saying "Vegas is fine, look at $50.8 billion" are right. The people saying "we lost $4.3 billion in one year" are also right. Both numbers came from the same presentation. A story that only tells you one of them is not telling you the truth.

What I actually watch in my business is a little different from the headline. I watch how confident local families feel about their own income. Confidence is what moves people to list a home, to stretch for a bigger house, or to finally buy instead of renewing a lease. Tourism spending feeds that confidence, but it is not the same thing as it. Rates, inventory, and how many people are moving here matter enormously too.

My honest read is that this is a signal worth tracking, not an alarm worth acting on. If you are a homeowner with a stable job and no plans to move, one year of softer visitor spending should not change anything you do. If your household income depends heavily on tips or seasonal hours, this is a good year to be conservative about how much house you take on. That is not a market prediction. That is just planning around the income you actually have.

What You Can Do

Go to the source before you trust a summary, including this one. The Las Vegas Convention and Visitors Authority posts its research and monthly visitor statistics publicly at lvcva.com, and board agendas are published ahead of meetings. If you work in hospitality, reading those monthly numbers yourself is a genuine career advantage. You will see a trend forming before your employer announces anything about it.

Check the labor data too. The Nevada Department of Employment, Training and Rehabilitation publishes monthly employment figures for the Las Vegas area, with leisure and hospitality broken out separately. Comparing those job counts month over month tells you far more about your own security than any single annual headline does.

If your income comes from tips or variable hours, do two practical things this year. First, keep clean records of your earnings, because lenders will average them and good documentation protects you. Second, build a cushion sized to your slowest realistic month, not your best one. That advice is boring, and it is the single thing that separates households that ride out a soft year from households that panic during one.

And if you are weighing a move, get a real read on your specific neighborhood rather than a valley wide average. Summerlin, Henderson, North Las Vegas, and the southwest do not all behave the same way, and a countywide tourism figure will never tell you what your street is doing.
