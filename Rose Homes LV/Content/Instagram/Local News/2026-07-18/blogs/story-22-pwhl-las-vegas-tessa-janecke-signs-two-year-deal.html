PWHL Las Vegas Signs No. 3 Overall Pick Tessa Janecke to Two-Year Deal

PWHL Las Vegas has signed forward Tessa Janecke to a two-year contract, making her the first player ever signed by the city's new pro women's hockey team. Janecke was taken third overall in the June 17 PWHL Draft. She agreed to the deal on Monday, July 13, and the league announced it on Tuesday, July 14.

That makes her the first draft pick in PWHL Las Vegas history. Not just the first forward. Not just the first name on the roster sheet. The first pick, period. Every team gets one moment like this, and Las Vegas just had its own.

Janecke is 22 years old and comes from Orangeville, Illinois, a small town a long way from the Strip. She just finished a career at Penn State that left her as the most decorated player in the program's history. She also won a gold medal with Team USA at the 2026 Winter Olympics in Milan. PWHL Las Vegas will play its home games at T-Mobile Arena.

What Happened

PWHL Las Vegas used the third overall selection in the June 17 PWHL Draft on Tessa Janecke. About four weeks later, the two sides put pen to paper. Janecke signed a two-year contract on Monday, July 13. The Professional Women's Hockey League made the announcement public on Tuesday, July 14.

Janecke is a forward. She spent her college career at Penn State, and she left State College as the most decorated player the program has ever had. Her final numbers there tell the story. She scored 89 goals. She added 112 assists. That adds up to 201 career points. Those are program records, and they were set against Big Ten competition that keeps getting tougher every year.

Her resume does not stop at the college level. Janecke was part of the United States team that won gold at the 2026 Winter Olympics in Milan. She played in seven games during that tournament and recorded five assists. Winning an Olympic gold medal at 22 is rare. Doing it and then immediately going third overall in a pro draft is rarer still.

It helps to slow down and look at those Penn State numbers again. She scored 89 goals. She set up teammates 112 times. Together that is 201 points, which is the most anyone has ever put up in that program. A player does not reach 201 points by having one hot season. That total takes four years of showing up, staying healthy, and producing against the best defenders in the country.

The Olympic line is smaller but just as telling. Five assists in seven games in Milan means she was creating chances in a tournament where every shift is tight and every mistake gets punished. She was not just along for the ride on a gold medal team. She was helping set up goals.

For a brand new franchise, that combination matters. Las Vegas did not draft a project player who needs three seasons to develop. The team drafted someone who has already played in the biggest games women's hockey has to offer and produced in them. A two-year deal locks her in through the early stretch of the franchise, which is exactly the window where a new team needs stability the most.

PWHL Las Vegas will play at T-Mobile Arena, the same building that hosts the Vegas Golden Knights. That is a serious venue for a first-year club. It puts women's pro hockey on the Strip, in a room that Las Vegas fans already know how to fill and how to make loud.

A few things are still open. The team does not have a permanent nickname yet. It is simply PWHL Las Vegas for now. The 2026-27 schedule has not been released either, so nobody can circle a home opener on the calendar just yet. Both of those pieces are coming, and locals are already guessing at names.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

If you have a daughter who plays hockey in Clark County, this is the biggest news of the summer. For years, girls in Las Vegas who fell in love with the sport had to look somewhere else for role models. They watched the Vegas Golden Knights at T-Mobile Arena. They watched the Henderson Silver Knights. Both are great teams, but the players on the ice did not look like them.

That changes now. A local kid can buy a ticket, sit in T-Mobile Arena, and watch a woman who won Olympic gold play in her city. That is a different kind of inspiration. It turns "maybe someday" into "she did it, so I can too." Parents who drive to early morning practices at City National Arena in Summerlin know exactly how much that matters.

There is a practical side too. Pro teams create demand for ice. Demand for ice creates more youth programs, more learn to skate sessions, more clinics, and more camps. Las Vegas has grown its hockey footprint fast over the last decade, and rinks like City National Arena have become real community hubs. A women's pro team gives those rinks another reason to expand girls programming.

It also helps to know where kids in this valley actually skate. City National Arena in Summerlin is the best known option on the west side, and it draws families from all over the northwest. In Henderson, the America First Center and Lee's Family Forum have made the southeast side of the valley its own hockey pocket. Between those buildings, a family can usually find ice within a reasonable drive.

That said, ice time in Las Vegas is still tight. Rinks are limited and the good slots go early. Parents trade stories about 6 a.m. practices for a reason. So when a new pro team raises interest in the sport, where everyone will skate becomes a real question. That kind of pressure is often what pushes a growing market toward more rink space over time.

Then there is the everyday impact on the city. More pro sports means more event nights on the Strip. More event nights mean more jobs, more restaurant traffic, and more hotel bookings on weekends that used to be quiet. Las Vegas has built a real sports economy over the last ten years, and each new team makes that engine a little stronger.

For homeowners, this is part of a much bigger pattern. Buyers moving to Las Vegas from other states keep telling me the same thing. They want a city with things to do year round. Pro hockey, pro football, pro basketball events, and now pro women's hockey all feed that story. It is one more reason people choose Clark County over somewhere else.

Families near the arena corridor and in the master planned communities on the west side tend to feel this first. Summerlin, Spring Valley, and the neighborhoods around City National Arena already have a strong hockey culture. Henderson has its own thanks to the Silver Knights. A new team gives all of those areas one more shared thing to rally around.

Background and History

The Professional Women's Hockey League launched as a serious, well funded home for the best women's hockey players in the world. Before it existed, top players often had to choose between chasing the Olympics and finding a stable place to play for a paycheck. The league changed that math, and it has been adding markets as it grows.

Las Vegas earning a franchise is not an accident. This city has spent the last decade proving it shows up for hockey. The Vegas Golden Knights arrived in 2017 and turned T-Mobile Arena into one of the loudest buildings in the sport. The Henderson Silver Knights gave the valley a second team and a second fan base. Youth programs followed, and rinks got busier.

City National Arena in Summerlin became a big part of that. It serves as a practice facility and a public rink, and it introduced thousands of Las Vegas kids to skating who never would have tried it otherwise. A desert city that once had almost no hockey history now has full youth leagues, travel teams, and adult leagues that run all year.

Tessa Janecke's own path shows how much the sport has changed for young women. She grew up in Orangeville, Illinois, a small town far from any pro spotlight. She built her game at Penn State and turned into the best player that program has produced. Then she made a U.S. Olympic roster and helped win gold in Milan in 2026.

Ten or fifteen years ago, a college career like that often ended with a hard question about what comes next. Today it ends with a pro draft, a two-year contract, and a locker room in a major market. That is real progress, and Las Vegas is now part of it.

What Happens Next

The next steps are pretty clear. PWHL Las Vegas will keep building its roster around Janecke. Signing your first pick early is how a front office sets a tone. It tells the rest of the league, and the rest of the draft class, that this team moves fast and takes care of business.

Two announcements are still outstanding, and both are worth watching. The team does not have a permanent nickname yet. Right now it is just PWHL Las Vegas. Team names in this city tend to spark real debate, and locals will have plenty of opinions once options start circulating. Expect that conversation to get loud.

The 2026-27 schedule has not been released either. Until it drops, nobody knows the date of the first home game at T-Mobile Arena. That first puck drop will be a genuine milestone for the valley, and it will likely sell well. If you want to be in the building for it, keep an eye out for ticket information when the schedule becomes official.

Between now and then, watch for more roster moves, staff announcements, and community events. New franchises usually spend their first months meeting the city. That often means appearances at local rinks, youth clinics, and school visits. Those are the events where Las Vegas kids actually get to meet the players, and they tend to fill up fast.

It is worth being honest about how much is still unknown. There is no permanent nickname. There is no released schedule. There is no announced home opener. Anything you see online claiming otherwise is a guess and not news. The confirmed pieces right now are the venue at T-Mobile Arena, the draft pick, and the signed two-year deal. Everything else is still ahead.

I would also watch how local youth hockey responds. Registration numbers for girls programs are a good early signal. If sign ups jump this fall at valley rinks, that tells you the team is already doing its job off the ice.

Ryan's Take

I have watched Las Vegas change a lot in the years I have been selling homes here. Sports has been one of the biggest drivers. When the Golden Knights arrived, the mood of the city shifted. People who never talked about hockey suddenly had a team. Neighbors met each other at watch parties. That stuff is hard to measure, but it is real, and it shows up in how people talk about where they live.

PWHL Las Vegas adds another layer. This one lands differently because of who it reaches. Every girl in Clark County who straps on skates at City National Arena now has a pro team in her own city to look up to. Signing an Olympic gold medalist as the very first player in franchise history is a strong way to start that relationship.

From a real estate view, I do not tell clients a hockey team will raise their home value on its own. It will not. But quality of life is a real factor in where people choose to buy, and Las Vegas keeps stacking reasons. Buyers relocating here ask about schools, commutes, and what there is to do. Having answers for that last one matters more than people think.

What You Can Do

If you want to follow this team, start with the official sources. The PWHL website has a Las Vegas team page where roster moves, the nickname reveal, and the 2026-27 schedule will be posted first. Bookmark it. The Las Vegas Review-Journal also covers the PWHL beat, so their sports section is a good second stop for local reporting.

Parents, this is a good moment to look at what your kids can actually try. City National Arena in Summerlin runs learn to skate and youth hockey programs, and other valley rinks offer beginner sessions too. You do not need to buy full gear to start. Most programs rent equipment for the first sessions, which keeps the cost down while your child figures out if they like it.

If you want to be at the first home game at T-Mobile Arena, plan ahead. Watch for the schedule release and any presale or season ticket information the team shares. First games for new franchises in this city have a way of selling out quickly, and prices on resale sites tend to climb once the date is public.

You can also just show up and support. Follow the team, share the news with other hockey families, and bring a kid to a game when tickets go on sale. Community support in year one is what makes a franchise stick.
