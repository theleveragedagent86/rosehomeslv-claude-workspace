$12 Million, 24-Court Pickleball Complex Taking Shape at Wayne Bunker Park

City of Las Vegas officials say the new 24-court regional pickleball complex at Wayne Bunker Park is well underway, and the ribbon cutting is targeted for January 2027. The $12 million project sits near Tenaya Way and Alexander Road in northwest Las Vegas, and it will roughly double pickleball capacity across that side of the valley.

Crews have already poured the court slabs and installed light fixtures. The snack shack and restroom building are close to finished. What was an idea on paper two years ago is now concrete, steel, and lights you can actually see from the street.

For families in Centennial Hills, Skye Canyon, Providence, and the northern edge of Summerlin, this is more than a parks story. It is a neighborhood amenity that shows up in listing descriptions, in buyer tours, and in the everyday decision about where people want to live. Public recreation is one of the few things that lifts an entire area at once, and northwest Las Vegas is about to get a big one.

What Happened at Wayne Bunker Park

The City of Las Vegas broke ground on the pickleball complex in January 2026. Eighteen months later, city officials report the work is well past the halfway mark. Crews poured the slabs for all 24 courts. They set and installed the light fixtures that will let players use the courts after sunset. The snack shack and the restroom building are nearly complete.

The total price tag is $12 million. That money does not come from the city's general fund, and it does not come from a new tax. The project is funded through the Southern Nevada Public Land Management Act, a federal law that restricts proceeds from federal land sales in Clark County to specific uses. Parks and recreation projects are one of those approved uses.

That funding detail matters more than most people realize. It means the courts got built without competing against police budgets, road repair, or other city services for dollars. The money was already set aside for exactly this kind of project. When residents ask why the city is spending $12 million on pickleball, the honest answer is that this particular pot of money could not have been spent on much else.

Wayne Bunker Park sits near Tenaya Way and Alexander Road. If you live in the northwest valley, you probably drive past it without thinking much about it. The park has been a neighborhood fixture for years, with open turf, walking paths, and shade. The pickleball complex takes an underused corner of that footprint and turns it into a regional draw.

Twenty-four courts is a serious number. Most public pickleball setups in Clark County are converted tennis courts with taped lines, offering four or six playable courts at best. A purpose-built 24-court facility puts Wayne Bunker Park in a different category entirely. It can host league play, open play, clinics, and tournaments at the same time without anyone waiting an hour for a court.

City officials describe the complex as a regional facility, not just a neighborhood one. That word choice is deliberate. Regional means players will drive in from Summerlin, from North Las Vegas, from Aliante, and from the west side. It also means the city expects the courts to carry tournament traffic and organized play rather than only casual weekend games.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Start with the simplest reason. If you play pickleball in the northwest valley right now, you wait. Courts fill up early. Open play sessions cap out. People show up at 6 a.m. to get a spot, and plenty still go home without playing. Doubling capacity in that part of town takes real pressure off a system that has been overloaded for years.

The demand is not a fad anymore. Pickleball has become one of the fastest-growing recreational activities in the country, and Las Vegas has an unusually good fit for it. The sport works for retirees, for families with kids, and for people in their thirties who want exercise without a knee replacement. Southern Nevada has all three groups in large numbers, plus the weather to play outdoors most of the year.

For homeowners, the effect shows up differently. Public amenities near a home change how that home feels to a buyer. Nobody buys a house because of a pickleball court. But a buyer touring two similar homes in Centennial Hills and Skye Canyon will notice that one of them sits five minutes from a brand new 24-court regional complex with lights and restrooms. That kind of detail helps a listing stand out in a market where inventory has loosened up.

Appraisers do not put a line item on a nearby park, but buyers price it in their heads anyway. National buyer surveys have shown for years that green space, walkability, and nearby recreation rank high on the list of neighborhood features people say they want. In practice that shows up as more weekend showing traffic and a shorter list of objections. Homes near well-kept public amenities tend to hold buyer interest when the market slows, because the reason to live there does not change when rates move.

There is a second effect that matters in Las Vegas specifically. A lot of buyers here are moving from out of state and cannot judge a neighborhood from memory. They rely on what they can see and drive to in a single weekend. A large, new, clearly funded public facility signals that the city is still investing in that side of town. That reads as stability to someone deciding between three areas they had never visited before.

There is also the quality-of-life piece that does not show up in numbers. Northwest Las Vegas grew fast, and rooftops came before recreation in a lot of neighborhoods. Providence, Skye Canyon, and the newer stretches of Centennial Hills filled in with homes long before the parks caught up. A project like this closes some of that gap and gives families a reason to stay local on a Saturday morning instead of driving across the valley.

Retirees and active adults may feel the biggest change. Northwest Las Vegas has a large population of people over 55 who moved here from California, the Pacific Northwest, and the Midwest. Pickleball is often how they meet neighbors after a move. A complex this size becomes a social hub, not just an athletic one, and that has real value for people who relocated without a built-in network.

Background and History

The Southern Nevada Public Land Management Act, usually called SNPLMA, passed in 1998. It set up a system where the federal government sells surplus public land in Clark County and directs the proceeds into specific local uses. Parks, trails, conservation, and recreation projects all qualify. Over the years, SNPLMA money has funded a long list of Clark County improvements that residents use without knowing where the dollars came from.

Because Clark County is surrounded by federal land, this arrangement gave Southern Nevada a funding stream that most metro areas simply do not have. When the valley expanded outward, land sales generated money, and some of that money came back as parks. The Wayne Bunker Park complex is a direct product of that system.

SNPLMA also explains why parks money in Clark County moves on its own schedule. Projects get nominated, reviewed, and approved in rounds, so a park can sit in the pipeline for years before a shovel hits dirt. The tradeoff is worth it. Once a project clears, the funding is dedicated and does not get pulled back into a general fund during a tough budget year. That is why residents sometimes see a new park break ground while other public spending sits on hold.

The pickleball surge has also changed how cities across the country budget for recreation. Parks departments spent decades building ball fields, tennis courts, and pools. Pickleball arrived fast, appealed to a wide age range, and needed far less space per player than most other sports. One tennis footprint can hold several pickleball courts, which makes the sport unusually efficient to add. Clark County and its cities responded the way most places did, first by striping existing courts and then by building dedicated facilities once the temporary fix stopped keeping up.

Pickleball demand in Clark County has been outrunning supply for close to a decade. Local clubs and informal groups spent years lobbying cities and the county for dedicated courts instead of shared tennis space. Tennis players and pickleball players competing for the same surfaces created friction at parks across the valley. Purpose-built facilities solve that problem for both sides.

The city broke ground in January 2026 after the planning, design, and funding approvals worked their way through. Construction on a project this size moves in stages. Grading and utilities come first, then slabs, then lighting and fencing, then the support buildings and surfacing. Reaching the point where slabs are poured and lights are up means the heaviest and least visible work is behind them.

What Happens Next

The target for the ribbon cutting is January 2027. That gives crews roughly six months to finish surfacing, install nets and fencing, wrap up the snack shack and restroom building, and complete the landscaping and parking work around the courts.

Anyone who has watched a public project knows that target dates can move. Weather, material delivery, and inspections all have a say. But a January 2027 goal with slabs already poured and lighting already installed is a realistic timeline, not a hopeful one. The riskiest phases of construction are done.

For residents planning around it, a January opening has a practical upside. Winter is prime outdoor season in Southern Nevada. Courts that open in January give players several comfortable months before summer heat pushes play to early mornings and evenings. It also lands when part-time residents and winter visitors are in town, so the complex will likely be busy from the first week.

Homeowners have a timing question too. A finished, open amenity carries more weight with buyers than a construction fence and a promise. If you are weighing a spring 2027 listing in a nearby neighborhood, the complex should be open and in use by then, which is a much better story to tell on a showing.

Between now and then, watch for a few things. The city will need to decide how court time gets managed, whether that means reservations, open play blocks, league contracts, or some mix. A 24-court regional facility usually needs a scheduling system on day one, or the same crowding problem shows up in a nicer building. Programming details often get announced in the final months before opening.

Also watch the surrounding area. New regional amenities tend to pull activity toward them. Nearby businesses along Tenaya Way and Alexander Road could see more traffic on weekends. Neighborhoods within a short drive, including parts of Centennial Hills, Providence, and Skye Canyon, get a talking point that did not exist before. Real estate marketing in the northwest valley will start mentioning this complex the moment it opens.

Ryan's Take

I pay attention to amenity projects because they are one of the few things that lift a whole area rather than one street. A new grocery store, a new park, a new school, and now a regional pickleball complex all do the same job. They make a neighborhood easier to sell without any individual homeowner spending a dollar.

Northwest Las Vegas has been the fastest-growing part of the valley for years, and the criticism has always been the same. Too many homes, not enough to do. Skye Canyon and Providence built beautiful communities, but families still drove to Summerlin or downtown for a lot of their weekend activity. Projects like this one chip away at that complaint, and buyers notice.

The funding source is worth repeating too. This is SNPLMA money, restricted by federal law to parks and recreation. It was not pulled away from something else. When I talk with buyers who are new to Las Vegas and skeptical about how the city spends money, this is a clean example of a project built the right way with the right dollars. That is not something I get to say often.

What You Can Do

If you play, start planning now. Local pickleball clubs and Facebook groups in Centennial Hills, Skye Canyon, and Summerlin will be the first to hear about opening details, league sign-ups, and open play schedules. Getting into one of those groups before the complex opens is the easiest way to avoid the January rush.

If you want to follow the construction or ask questions about programming, the City of Las Vegas parks and recreation department is the place to go. City council meetings and ward newsletters cover project updates, and public comment is open to residents. If you have an opinion about how court time should be scheduled, the months before opening are when that input actually gets heard.

If you own a home in the northwest valley, keep this project in your back pocket. When you eventually sell, a brand new 24-court regional complex a few minutes away is a legitimate selling point, especially for buyers relocating from out of state who are looking for an active community. And if you are shopping for a home in Centennial Hills, Skye Canyon, Providence, or northern Summerlin right now, it is worth knowing which neighborhoods sit closest to it.
