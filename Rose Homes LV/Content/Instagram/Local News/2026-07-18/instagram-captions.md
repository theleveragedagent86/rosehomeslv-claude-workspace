## Story 1: Lake Mead Is About To Set A Record Nobody Wants

Lake Mead is on pace to fall to the lowest level ever recorded, and it could happen before this month is over.

The Bureau of Reclamation projects the lake will drop to about 1,040 feet in July 2026.

The all-time record low is 1,040.58 feet, set back in July 2022.

As of July 12, 2026, the lake sat at roughly 1,042.77 feet. That is just over two feet above the record.

Forecasters are not calling this the bottom, either. They expect another 30-foot drop through May 2028.

There is a second number in that report that got far less attention. Hoover Dam hydropower capacity could face up to a 40 percent additional reduction as early as this fall.

This is not really a story about a lake. It is a story about your tap and your power bill.

Nearly all of Southern Nevada's drinking water comes out of Lake Mead. When the lake drops, the rules about how we use water get tighter.

We have already done a lot here. Decorative grass has been coming out for years and the valley recycles the water we use indoors.

That is the frustrating part for a lot of homeowners. You replaced your lawn, you fixed your sprinklers, and the lake still keeps falling.

The power side is the piece almost nobody is talking about. Less water behind the dam means less electricity out of it.

When that generation drops, power has to come from somewhere else, and that cost tends to land on customers.

For anyone buying a home here, this belongs in the conversation. Ask about the landscaping rules in the HOA. Ask what the summer power bill actually runs.

None of this means Las Vegas is running dry tomorrow. It does mean the cheap and easy conservation wins are mostly behind us.

The next round of rules is going to be harder, and somebody is going to be unhappy about it.

## Story 5: The County Just Listed 75 Fixes For School Traffic. Now Who Pays

Four kids died walking or riding to school last year in Clark County. This week the county finally put a list on paper.

The countywide School Traffic Safety Working Group released a formal report with 75 proposals.

They are sorted into four buckets. Education, enforcement, engineering and planning, and policy.

The timeline runs from the next few months out to the next five years.

The report follows a school year in which 427 young people age 18 and under were struck by vehicles going to and from school. Four of them died.

That is a jump of more than 350 percent over the prior year.

Some of it starts right away. Extra police presence around schools for the first two weeks of class. Replacing missing and damaged signs. Repairs to pavement markings and landscaping. Hiring more crossing guards.

Now here is the sentence that turns good news into a question mark.

Many of the bigger items depend on agencies finding funding, and that funding is not secured.

A list of 75 recommendations is 75 things somebody has to pay for.

The extra patrols and the sign replacements are the cheap ones. Those will happen.

The expensive fixes are the ones that actually change outcomes. Rebuilt crossings. Better lighting. Redesigned intersections near campuses.

Those are the items that quietly slide into next year's budget conversation, and then the year after that.

Parents in Centennial Hills, Green Valley, and North Las Vegas have been naming their worst intersections for months. They are not guessing.

The report is real progress. Putting numbers and deadlines on paper makes agencies accountable in a way that one more meeting never does.

But paper does not slow down a driver at 7:15 in the morning.

School starts August 10. That gives everyone about three weeks to show they meant it.
