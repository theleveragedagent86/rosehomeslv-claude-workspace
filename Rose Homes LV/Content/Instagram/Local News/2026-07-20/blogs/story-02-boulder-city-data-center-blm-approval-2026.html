A Federal Shortcut Approved a Boulder City Data Center and Shut the Public Out

In late June 2026, the Bureau of Land Management approved a data center on federal land near Boulder City without holding a public comment period. Federal officials did it by amending an old solar farm permit instead of starting a brand new review, which meant residents never got a chance to speak.

The land belongs to the federal government. The company is Townsite Solar 2, which is owned by a Texas-based hedge fund. Back in 2023, that company got a permit to build a solar farm on the site. This June, the BLM changed the permit so the company can build a data center there instead.

Two numbers made people angry. Boulder City estimates it will lose about $2.3 million a year in revenue it would have earned by leasing city land for a project like this. Meanwhile, the developer's last publicly disclosed rent payment to the BLM for that plot, back in 2023, was $1,207.67.

Federal officials say the approval follows the law. They point to a 2025 executive order that told agencies to speed up permits for data centers. They also said the environmental study done for the solar project was good enough to cover the data center.

Boulder City strongly disagrees. On Tuesday, July 14, 2026, the City Council voted unanimously to appeal the decision. Residents packed that meeting and rallied outside beforehand. Here is what happened, why it matters far beyond Boulder City, and what comes next.

What Happened

In 2023, the Bureau of Land Management gave Townsite Solar 2 a permit to build a solar farm on a plot of federal land. That kind of permit is called a right-of-way. It lets a private company use public land for a specific project and pay rent to the federal government for it. The company's last publicly disclosed rent payment for the site in 2023 was $1,207.67.

In late June 2026, the BLM approved an amendment to that permit. The amendment lets the company build a data center on the same land instead of a solar farm. A data center is a big warehouse full of computer servers. It is a very different building from rows of solar panels, and it uses a very different amount of power.

Here is the part that upset people. When a federal agency approves a big project, a law called NEPA usually kicks in. NEPA stands for the National Environmental Policy Act. In plain terms, it says the government has to study how a project will affect the land, air, water, and people around it before it says yes. That study process is also where the public normally gets to send in comments.

Instead of doing a new study, the BLM used something called a "determination of NEPA adequacy." That is a fancy way of saying the agency decided the old environmental review, the one done for the solar farm, already covered everything it needed to know about the data center. If the old review is good enough, no new review is needed. And if no new review is needed, there is no new public comment period either.

That is the whole story in one sentence. The shortcut was legal in the eyes of the officials who used it, and it also closed the only door residents had.

Boulder City found out after the fact. City Attorney Brittany Walker said federal land managers never worked with the city in any real way. "We were not involved in a collaborative way in the process of looking at the application at all," Walker said.

Then there is the money. Boulder City owns a lot of land of its own, and the city leases land to developers for projects like solar and data centers. Those leases pay real money into the city budget. City officials estimate Boulder City is out roughly $2.3 million a year because this project landed on federal ground instead of city ground. That gap between $2.3 million and $1,207.67 is why this story spread so fast.

To be fair to everyone involved, no one has been accused of breaking the law. The BLM says its process complies with a 2025 executive order aimed at speeding up data center permitting. The disagreement is about process, notice, and who gets a say.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

You may not live in Boulder City. This still matters to you, and here is why.

Most of the land in Nevada is owned by the federal government. Drive fifteen minutes in almost any direction from your neighborhood and you will hit federal land. That means the question raised here is not a Boulder City question. It is a Clark County question. If a federal agency can swap the use of a permit without new public review, that same move could happen next to Henderson, next to North Las Vegas, or next to a subdivision in the southwest valley.

For homeowners, the practical worry is simple. When land near your house gets developed, you usually get notice. A sign goes up. A hearing gets scheduled. You can show up and speak, or you can write in. Local zoning gives you that. Federal land does not run on local zoning, so when a project moves onto federal ground the local process you are used to may not apply at all.

That does not mean a data center is automatically bad for your property value. Data centers bring construction jobs and tax base. But buyers care about what is next door, and they care about the unknown. A neighbor who cannot get a straight answer about what is going up across the road is a nervous neighbor. Uncertainty is what actually softens a street, not the building itself.

Appraisers deal in comparable sales, and there simply are not many sales yet of homes near a brand new Nevada data center. That means nobody can hand you a clean number for what it does to value. What we do know is that big industrial neighbors tend to matter most to the closest streets and matter less the farther out you go.

There is also the city budget angle, and this one hits renters and homeowners the same way. Lease revenue pays for city services. Parks. Road repairs. Police and fire. When a city expects $2.3 million a year and does not get it, that money has to come from somewhere else or the service has to shrink. Boulder City is a small city, so $2.3 million a year is a meaningful piece of its budget.

Then there is water and power, which every household here thinks about. Data centers use a lot of electricity, and cooling systems can use water. Southern Nevada is already having hard conversations about both. Adding large power users to the valley is a real policy question, and it deserves to be debated in the open rather than settled by an amendment to a three-year-old permit.

Finally, there is the precedent. Henderson is scheduled to vote on July 21, 2026, on a 180-day pause for new data center permits inside city limits. Cities across the valley are trying to get their arms around this land use. What happened in Boulder City is a reminder that city rules only reach so far. On federal land, the city may be a spectator.

Background and History

Data centers moved to the top of Southern Nevada's land use fights very quickly. Cheap land, lots of sunshine for power generation, and no state income tax made Nevada attractive. Projects that were pitched as solar or renewable energy a few years ago are now being pitched as computing infrastructure.

That is exactly the shift you see here. Townsite Solar 2 came in with a solar plan in 2023 and got approval. Solar farms are low buildings, mostly panels and posts, and they generate power. A data center consumes power instead. The 2023 environmental review was written with the solar version in mind.

It helps to understand what NEPA was built to do. Congress passed it decades ago because federal agencies were approving big projects without looking hard at the consequences. NEPA forced agencies to slow down, study the effects, and write it down. Over time, the public comment step became one of the few reliable ways ordinary people could push back on a federal decision.

A "determination of NEPA adequacy" is a real, established tool. It exists so agencies do not have to redo an identical study for a nearly identical project. Used the way it was intended, it saves everyone time and money. The fight in Boulder City is about whether a data center is close enough to a solar farm for that tool to apply.

The 2025 executive order on data center permitting sits on top of all of this. It directed federal agencies to move faster on approvals for this specific kind of project. Officials involved in the Boulder City approval say their decision complies with that order. Supporters see that as government finally moving at the speed of business. Critics see speed being purchased with public input.

Boulder City also has its own history worth knowing. It is one of the few places in Nevada with strict growth controls written into how the city operates, and residents there have a long habit of turning out for land use fights. That background explains the size of the reaction. This is a town that pays attention to what gets built.

What Happens Next

Boulder City is not letting this go. On Tuesday, July 14, 2026, the City Council voted unanimously to appeal the BLM approval to the Interior Department's Board of Land Appeals. That is the administrative body inside the Interior Department that reviews decisions made by agencies like the BLM.

The city also asked for something called a stay. A stay is a pause. If it is granted, construction would stop while an administrative law judge reviews the case. If it is denied, the developer can keep moving forward even while the appeal is pending. Watch for that ruling first, because it decides whether anything gets built in the meantime.

Appeals like this are not fast. There is no published deadline for a final decision, so plan on months rather than weeks. The core question the judge will look at is whether the BLM was right to lean on the 2023 solar review instead of running a fresh one for a data center.

Two other things are worth tracking. First, the Henderson City Council vote on July 21, 2026, on a 180-day pause for new data center permits. That vote is about city land, not federal land, but it tells you where the valley's politics are heading. Second, watch whether other Clark County jurisdictions start asking for formal consultation agreements with the BLM so they are not surprised again.

If you own property near federal land anywhere in the valley, the practical takeaway is to stop assuming you will get a notice in the mail. Federal projects do not always come with the local warning system you are used to.

Ryan's Take

I sell homes in this valley, so let me be direct about the part that affects you. This is not really a story about whether data centers are good or bad. Reasonable people land in different places on that. This is a story about whether the people who live next to a piece of land get to know what is coming.

Buyers ask me constantly what is going up on that empty dirt down the street. Most of the time I can find the answer, because most of the time it runs through a county or city process with public records and hearings I can pull. When the land is federal, that trail can go cold fast. That is a real gap, and this case put it in front of everyone.

I also want to be fair to the other side. The BLM says it followed the law and an executive order that told it to move faster. That is a defensible position. My honest read is that the tool was probably legal and the outcome still feels wrong to the people who live there, and both of those things can be true at once.

What I would watch is the stay request. If construction is paused while this gets reviewed, Boulder City has real leverage. If it is not, the appeal may end up deciding a question about a building that is already going up. Either way, this is the fight that sets the pattern for the next ten of these in Clark County.

What You Can Do

If you live in Boulder City, the fastest way to stay useful is to follow your City Council agendas. Council meetings are where the appeal will be discussed and funded, and public comment there is still wide open even though the federal comment period was not. Showing up is not symbolic in a town this size.

If you live anywhere else in Clark County, check what is next to you before you assume it is safe. Look up your parcel and the parcels around it through the Clark County Assessor. If a nearby parcel shows as federal land, know that a project there may follow federal rules rather than your city's zoning rules.

You can also sign up for notices directly. The BLM posts proposed projects and public comment periods on its Nevada state office pages, and many comment windows are short. Signing up before you have a problem is much easier than trying to catch up after a decision is made.

If you are buying near open desert land, ask your agent to check both the local zoning and whether the surrounding parcels are private or federal. It is a five minute question that can save you a very unpleasant surprise two years into owning the home. If you are selling near a proposed project, get ahead of it and have the facts ready, because buyers will ask.
