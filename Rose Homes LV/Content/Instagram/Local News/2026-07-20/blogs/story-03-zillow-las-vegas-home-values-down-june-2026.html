National Home Values Went Up. Zillow Says Las Vegas Values Went Down 3.1 Percent

Zillow's June 2026 market report says the typical home in the United States is now worth $372,057, up 1.1 percent from a year ago. The typical home in the Las Vegas metro is worth $427,825, down 3.1 percent over that exact same year. National real estate news is not local real estate news, and June is one of the cleanest examples of that we have seen all year.

Here is where it gets confusing for anyone who has been following the local numbers. Las Vegas Realtors reported that the median price of an existing single-family home in Southern Nevada was $490,000 in June. That matched the all-time record set in May. It was also up about 1 percent from June 2025.

So one report says Las Vegas home values dropped. Another report says Las Vegas home prices hit a record. Both were published within days of each other. Both come from real data.

Neither number is wrong. They are answering two completely different questions. One is a computer model that estimates what every home in the valley is worth, whether or not anyone tried to sell it. The other is the middle price of the homes that actually changed hands last month. Once you understand that difference, the whole story makes sense. And once you understand it, you will never look at your Zestimate the same way again.

What Happened

Zillow publishes a monthly market report built on something called the Zillow Home Value Index, or ZHVI. The June 2026 edition came out July 7. It put the typical U.S. home value at $372,057. That was up 0.7 percent from May and up 1.1 percent from June 2025. Nationally, the number moved in the direction most people expect. Slowly and upward.

The Las Vegas metro row in that same report tells a different story. The typical Las Vegas area home value came in at $427,825. That was down 0.1 percent from May and down 3.1 percent from a year earlier. A 3.1 percent drop on a home valued near $427,825 works out to roughly $13,000 in a single year.

The month-to-month piece deserves less attention than the yearly piece. A 0.1 percent move on a $427,825 value is about $430. That is noise. It is not a trend, and nobody should make a decision based on it. The 3.1 percent yearly decline is the number that matters, and it is the number that will show up in the group chats.

One detail gets lost when people share the headline. Even after that decline, the typical Las Vegas home is still worth about $55,000 more than the typical American home. Vegas sits roughly 15 percent above the national figure. We are not a cheap market that got cheaper. We are an expensive market that gave a little back.

Now the local side. Las Vegas Realtors, which runs the local Multiple Listing Service, released its June numbers around the same time. The median price of an existing single-family home in Southern Nevada was $490,000. That tied the all-time high from May. It was up about 1 percent from June 2025. Local sales also climbed in June compared to the month before.

The condo and townhome side did not look the same. The local median for existing condos and townhomes was $292,000 in June, down 4.3 percent from June 2025. That is a real decline in a real segment of our market, and it is one of the clues that helps explain the gap between the two reports.

One more local number worth writing down. In June, 78.5 percent of existing local homes sold within 60 days. A year earlier, that figure was 82.8 percent. Homes are still selling. They are just taking a bit longer to get there than they did last summer.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Start with the homeowner who opened Zillow this month and saw a smaller number than last year. That feeling is real, and the reaction is usually one of two things. Either panic that the market is falling apart, or anger that the site got it wrong. Neither reaction is the right one.

Your Zestimate is a model output. It is an educated guess produced by software that has never walked through your house. It does not know you redid the kitchen. It does not know your neighbor's identical floor plan backs to a busy street and yours backs to a park. It is not an appraisal and it is not an offer. When the metro index falls 3.1 percent, your specific home did not automatically lose 3.1 percent.

Now think about a seller. If you are listing this fall, the $490,000 record median can be dangerous if you read it wrong. That number does not mean your home is worth $490,000. It means half the existing single-family homes that sold in Southern Nevada in June went for more than that and half went for less. Your price comes from your street, your square footage, your condition and your recent comparable sales. Not from a countywide midpoint.

Buyers should read this differently again. A market where the broad value index is flat to slightly down, and where fewer homes are selling inside 60 days, is a market with more room to negotiate than we had a year ago. That does not mean sellers are desperate. It means the days of writing an offer in eleven minutes with no questions asked have cooled off in a lot of price ranges.

If you own a condo or townhome, pay closer attention than most. A 4.3 percent yearly decline in that median is meaningfully different from what single-family owners are seeing. Rising HOA costs and insurance pressure have been real headwinds for attached housing across the country, and Southern Nevada is not immune.

There is also a practical money angle. If you are planning to refinance, pull equity, or drop mortgage insurance, the lender is going to order an appraisal or an automated valuation. Neither of those is your Zestimate. Going in with a realistic number saves you time, money and disappointment.

The last thing worth saying is that a metro number covers a lot of ground. The Las Vegas metro in this report includes Henderson, North Las Vegas, Summerlin, Boulder City and everything in between. Those submarkets do not move together. A guard-gated pocket in the southwest and a starter neighborhood off Boulder Highway can go in opposite directions in the same quarter. One valley-wide percentage cannot describe both.

Background and History

The Zillow Home Value Index is not a list of sale prices. It is a statistical model that estimates the value of every home in a defined area, then reports a typical value for the middle of the market. It includes the house on your block that has not sold since 2014. It includes homes that were never listed. It is meant to describe the whole housing stock, not the small slice of it that traded last month.

The Las Vegas Realtors median works the opposite way. It takes every existing single-family home that closed through the local MLS in June, lines those sale prices up from lowest to highest, and reports the one in the middle. It only counts homes that actually sold. It is a snapshot of transactions, not of the whole valley.

That is why the two can move in opposite directions at the same time without either being wrong. Imagine a month where fewer entry-level homes sell because higher rates squeezed first-time buyers out, while sales in Summerlin and Henderson hold steady. The middle of the sold pile shifts upward. The median rises. Meanwhile the model that values all 700,000-plus homes in the metro sees soft demand and slightly longer marketing times, and it eases values down a little across the board. Median up, index down, same month, same city.

The condo number supports that idea. Attached homes are a big share of our lower-priced inventory. When that segment weakens by 4.3 percent, it pulls down a broad index that counts every condo in the valley. But if fewer of those condos are closing, they have less influence on a single-family median that excludes them entirely.

There is one more wrinkle. Monthly index models like this one get revised as more sales close and more data works its way through the system. The figure you read in July is a best estimate of June, not a final carved-in-stone result. That is normal, and it is another reason to treat any single month as one data point rather than a verdict.

This is not a new problem, and it is not unique to Las Vegas. Every market in the country has multiple price measures that disagree at the margins. Reporters usually quote whichever one makes the better headline. Homeowners are left to sort it out.

What Happens Next

The first thing to watch is whether these two measures start moving back toward each other. If the Zillow index keeps sliding while the local median keeps setting records, that gap becomes the story of the second half of 2026. If the index flattens out over the next two or three months, the 3.1 percent drop looks more like a correction from a peak than the start of a slide.

The second thing to watch is time on market. That 78.5 percent of homes selling within 60 days, down from 82.8 percent, is a small change that points in a clear direction. If that number keeps drifting lower through August and September, sellers will feel it before any index tells them. Price reductions usually show up in the data before medians do.

The third thing is the condo and townhome segment. A 4.3 percent yearly decline in that median is the sharpest move in either report. If attached housing keeps softening while detached housing holds, Las Vegas ends up with a genuinely two-speed market, and the metro-wide averages get less useful every month.

Watch what new-home builders do this fall as well. When builders lean hard on rate buydowns and closing cost incentives, resale sellers in the same price range have to answer that offer. A buyer comparing a brand-new home with a discounted rate against a fifteen-year-old resale down the street is doing simple math. That pressure shows up in resale prices before it shows up in any national index.

Keep the calendar in mind too. Summer in Las Vegas is our slow season for showings, and the fall usually brings a second wave of activity before the holidays. July and August numbers tend to look weaker than they really are. Judging the market off a single midsummer report is a mistake in both directions.

Finally, expect this exact confusion to repeat next month. Zillow will publish another report. Las Vegas Realtors will publish another one. The headlines will not match. That is not a scandal and it is not a cover-up. It is just two organizations counting two different things on two different schedules.

Ryan's Take

I get some version of this question almost every week, and it usually starts the same way. Someone sends me a screenshot of their Zestimate and asks whether it is right. The honest answer is that it is a decent starting point for a conversation and a terrible basis for a decision.

Here is what I actually do when a client asks what their home is worth. I pull the sales in their subdivision from the last three to six months. I look at square footage, lot, condition, upgrades, whether it backs to a wall or a street, and what the buyer pool for that specific floor plan looks like right now. Sometimes that lands above the Zestimate. Sometimes it lands well below it. Twice this year it came in more than $40,000 apart from the online number, in opposite directions.

What I do not do is tell people the market is fine when it is not, or tell them the sky is falling to scare them into listing. Right now the truth is somewhere in the middle. Las Vegas values gave back a few percent over the last year. Sold prices at the top of the existing single-family market are still at record levels. Homes are taking a little longer to sell. All three of those things are true at once, and a homeowner deserves to hear all three.

What You Can Do

If you are curious about your value, do not stop at the Zestimate. Ask for recent comparable sales in your specific neighborhood, ideally within a half mile and within the last six months. If a home two streets over sold last month with your same floor plan, that single sale tells you more than any metro-wide index ever will.

If you plan to sell in the next six to twelve months, start now instead of the week you list. Price is only part of it. Condition, timing and how your home shows against the other listings in your community all move the final number. In a market where fewer homes are clearing inside 60 days, being the best-prepared listing on your street matters more than it did last year.

If you are buying, use this report as permission to slow down and do your homework. Ask how long a home has been on the market, whether the price has been reduced, and what similar homes nearby actually closed at. If you own a condo or townhome and you are watching that 4.3 percent decline with concern, get a real read on your specific building rather than the countywide number, because HOA health and unit mix vary enormously from one complex to the next.

And if you are not buying or selling at all, the useful move is simply to stop reacting to headlines about your own house. Save this one thought instead. A national number tells you about the country. A metro index tells you about a model. Only a comparable sale on your street tells you about your home. Everything else is context, and context is worth reading, but it is not worth losing sleep over.
