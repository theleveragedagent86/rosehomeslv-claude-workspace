Las Vegas Rents Rose 17 Percent Since 2019. Los Angeles Rents Actually Fell

Las Vegas one-bedroom rents climbed about 17 percent between June 2019 and June 2026. Los Angeles one-bedroom rents dropped 1.3 percent over those same seven years. That comes from a new Zumper analysis covered by the Las Vegas Review-Journal, and it matters because it changes the story Clark County renters have been told for a long time.

The old pitch was simple. Move to Las Vegas and pay far less than you would in Southern California. That is still true today. But the gap is not as wide as it used to be, and it has been closing fast.

Here are the actual numbers. The median one-bedroom rent in the Las Vegas Valley went from about $1,053 in June 2019 to $1,235 in June 2026. In Los Angeles, the same type of unit went from $2,230 to $2,200. Vegas renters now pay roughly $182 more per month than they did seven years ago. Los Angeles renters pay about $30 less.

Now the part headlines tend to skip. Los Angeles is still much more expensive. A one-bedroom there costs about $965 more every single month than the same unit here. Over a year that is close to $11,580. Las Vegas did not become as expensive as Los Angeles. Las Vegas stopped being the bargain it was in 2019.

That difference matters if you are deciding whether to renew a lease or start looking at homes. Below is what the data actually says, why supply sits at the center of it, and how to think about the rent versus buy math without anyone selling you an answer.

What Happened

Zumper, a national rental listing platform, compared median one-bedroom rents in June 2019 with median one-bedroom rents in June 2026 across major metros. The Las Vegas Valley came in at about $1,053 seven years ago and $1,235 today. That is a 17 percent increase.

Los Angeles moved the other way. A median one-bedroom there went from $2,230 to $2,200, a drop of 1.3 percent. Put those two together and the growth gap between the two metros is more than 18 percentage points. The Review-Journal framed it as Vegas rents rising almost 20 percent faster than Los Angeles rents since 2019.

Zumper researcher Crystal Chen pointed to one main reason for the split: supply. Cities that permitted and delivered a lot of new units saw rents flatten or drift back toward 2019 levels. She named Denver and Salt Lake City as examples. Fast-growing markets that did not add enough units saw the opposite. She put Las Vegas, Austin and Miami in that second group.

That is a simple idea with real weight behind it. When more households want to rent than there are units available, landlords hold the leverage. When a wave of new buildings opens at once, those same landlords have to compete. Competition shows up as flat renewals, a free month, or waived fees.

It is worth saying plainly what this study does not say. It does not say Las Vegas rent is now close to Los Angeles rent. In dollars, the two markets are not close at all. The $1,235 versus $2,200 gap is huge, and it is still one of the biggest reasons people move here. What changed is the direction. In 2019, a Los Angeles one-bedroom cost about 2.1 times a Las Vegas one-bedroom. In 2026 it costs about 1.8 times as much. The gap narrowed. It did not close.

There is also a difference between a median and your actual rent. A median is the middle of the market. Half of the one-bedrooms rent for less than that, half rent for more. Your building, your ZIP code and your renewal date matter far more to your budget than a metro-wide number does.

The comparison also leans on asking rents for listed units, not on what every household in the valley currently pays. Renters who have stayed in the same apartment for years often pay well under the median. People signing a new lease this month are the ones facing today's asking price. That is part of why the same statistic can feel wrong to two neighbors in the same complex.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

For a Clark County renter, the practical takeaway is that the cushion is thinner than it used to be. That $182 a month works out to about $2,184 a year that used to go somewhere else. On a typical valley household budget, that is not a rounding error.

It also changes the rent versus buy conversation, and this is where I want to be careful. Rising rent does not automatically mean buying is the better move. It means the comparison is finally worth running with real numbers instead of assumptions.

Here is what actually goes into that comparison. On the buy side you have a down payment, closing costs, principal and interest, property taxes, homeowners insurance, and any HOA dues. You also have maintenance, which most first-time buyers underestimate. A water heater, an air conditioning system in a valley that runs it hard for five months a year, and eventually a roof all become your problem once you own.

On the rent side you have a payment that can rise at renewal, a security deposit you may or may not get back, and almost no exposure to repair costs. You also keep flexibility. If your job changes or your family grows, you give notice instead of listing a house and paying commission and closing costs on the way out.

The single biggest variable is how long you plan to stay. Buying and selling both cost money up front and on the back end. If you are likely to leave the valley in two or three years, the math often favors renting, even with rents climbing. If you expect to be here seven years or longer, the math shifts, because you spread those one-time costs over a much longer stretch and you build equity along the way.

The second biggest variable is your down payment and your cash reserves. Buying with nothing left in the bank is how a good decision turns into a rough year. A lender will approve you for a payment that leaves you stretched thin. Whether you should accept that payment is a completely separate question, and it is yours to answer, not theirs.

One more local wrinkle. Rent increases here have not been spread evenly. A renter in a 2022-built complex in the southwest and a renter in an older building near Boulder Highway have lived through very different seven years. Metro-wide medians hide that. Compare your own renewal notice to what similar units within a mile are asking right now.

None of this is a pitch. Some households in Clark County should buy this year. Others should rent two more years and keep saving. The value of a story like this one is that it makes you run your own numbers instead of guessing at the answer.

Background and History

So why did Las Vegas rents climb while Los Angeles rents did not? Start with who moved here.

The valley added people quickly after 2019, and a large share of them came from Southern California. That demand has not slowed down. Realtor.com data covered by the Review-Journal showed that Los Angeles and San Jose together generated close to one third of all out-of-market home searches for the Las Vegas Valley in the first quarter of 2026. Southern California remains the largest source of buyer demand flowing into Clark County.

People who search for homes also rent first. A household that arrives in the spring usually signs a lease before it ever signs a purchase contract. That puts steady pressure on the rental market on top of the pressure coming from people who already live here.

Supply did not keep up. Building takes years. Land near job centers is limited. Construction costs rose sharply during the same window. So the valley absorbed a lot of new households without adding anywhere near a matching number of new units. That is exactly the gap Chen was describing.

Los Angeles had a different problem, and it produced a different result. Rents there were already extremely high in 2019, population growth stalled, and remote work pulled some renters out of the most expensive submarkets. Flat rent in Los Angeles is not a sign that Los Angeles became affordable. It is a sign that a very expensive market ran out of room to keep climbing.

It also helps to remember where Las Vegas started. In 2019, a $1,053 median one-bedroom was one of the cheapest big-metro rents in the West. A market that starts low has more room to rise in percentage terms. A 17 percent jump on $1,053 is $182. The same 17 percent on $2,230 would have been $379. Percentages can make a smaller dollar move look bigger than it feels in a checking account.

What Happens Next

The supply argument cuts both ways, and there are early signs it is starting to work in favor of renters here.

Roughly 4,200 new multifamily units are forecast to deliver across the Las Vegas metro in 2026. That is real inventory arriving in a market that has been short on it for years. New buildings do not push rent down overnight, but they change the negotiation. A leasing office with empty units behaves differently than one with a waitlist.

Renters are already seeing a little of that shift. A Las Vegas Sun report found that a $1,500 budget bought more apartment square footage in the valley in 2026 than it did in 2025. Spring Valley led the metro at 954 square feet for $1,500, up 19 square feet year over year. Paradise added 25 square feet to reach 940 square feet, which ranked as the third-largest year-over-year gain in the entire country.

Those are small gains. Nobody is throwing a party over 19 extra square feet. But direction matters, and the direction flipped. The same dollar is buying slightly more space instead of slightly less.

Here is what to watch over the next year. Track how many of those 4,200 units actually deliver on schedule. Watch for concessions like a free month or waived application fees showing up in listings. Keep an eye on whether Southern California demand stays as strong as it was in the first quarter. If deliveries land and demand cools even a little, renewal increases should soften. If deliveries slip and demand holds, the 2019 to 2026 trend likely keeps going.

One caution on that optimism. New apartment supply in the valley skews toward newer, higher-end product. That relieves the top of the market first. Relief usually reaches older and cheaper buildings later, as renters who can afford a new unit move up and free up what they leave behind.

Ryan's Take

Here is what I keep seeing in real conversations. Someone moves here from Southern California, looks at a $1,235 one-bedroom, and feels like they won. Then they renew twice, watch it climb toward $1,350, and start asking a different question. That question is usually some version of "am I just paying someone else's mortgage?"

My honest answer is that it depends. Sometimes yes, sometimes no, and it turns on your numbers rather than on a headline. I have told people to buy this year. I have also told people to keep renting and save for another eighteen months. Both were the right call for those specific households, and neither one paid me anything in the moment.

What I would push back on is the idea that this study means Las Vegas is now expensive like Los Angeles. It is not. A one-bedroom here still runs roughly $965 a month less. That is a genuine advantage and a big reason people keep arriving. The honest read is narrower than the headline: the discount is shrinking, and the reason is that we did not build enough housing for how many people showed up.

If you rent in this valley, the useful response is not panic. It is to run your own numbers one time, with real quotes and your real timeline, so you know exactly where you stand. Most renters I talk to have never actually done that. They have only guessed, usually based on something a coworker said. An hour with honest math beats a year of wondering.

What You Can Do

If you rent, start with your renewal notice. Look up what similar units in your complex and within a mile of it are asking today. If a newer building nearby is offering a free month, you have something concrete to point to. Landlords sitting on vacancy do negotiate, especially outside the peak summer moving months.

If you are weighing a purchase, do three things before you decide anything. Get a real payment quote instead of an online estimate, so you can see principal, interest, taxes, insurance and any HOA dues together in one number. Add your own maintenance line, because owning here means an air conditioning system that works hard. Then compare that full total to your current rent plus the increase you realistically expect over the next few years.

Also check whether you qualify for help. Nevada's Home Is Possible program has been offering a 5.50 percent rate on FHA, VA and USDA loans along with $20,000 in down payment assistance for qualifying essential workers. Requirements include a 640 credit score, six months of Nevada residency, and a purchase price under $832,750. You do not have to be a first-time buyer. As of July 20, 2026 the state's rate sheet showed only 59 reservations left, so verify the count is still live before you build a plan around it.

And if the honest answer is that renting still fits your life better right now, that is a legitimate answer. Save the difference, watch the new supply come online, and revisit the question next year with better numbers.
