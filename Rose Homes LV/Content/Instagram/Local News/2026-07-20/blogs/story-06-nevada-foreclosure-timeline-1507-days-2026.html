Foreclosure Takes 563 Days Nationally. In Nevada It Takes Over Four Years

Banks across the country are finishing foreclosures faster than they have in more than a decade. Homes foreclosed on nationwide in the second quarter of 2026 had been in the foreclosure process for an average of 563 days. In Nevada, the same average was 1,507 days, or roughly 4.1 years.

Those two numbers come from the same report, released by the property data company ATTOM in mid-July 2026. Nevada's average is about 2.7 times the national average. Only four states run longer, and one nearby state, Texas, averaged just 155 days.

This is a good example of why national real estate news is not local real estate news. If you read a national headline that says foreclosure is speeding up, that headline is accurate. It is just not describing what happens here. The process in Nevada still measures in years, not months.

That matters most to one specific group of people. If you are a Clark County homeowner who has fallen behind or is worried about falling behind, the practical takeaway is that you likely have time to work the problem. Time is the resource that gives someone options.

One important caution up front. An average is not a promise. It does not mean you can ignore a missed payment, and it does not mean your own situation will take four years. Read the rest of this article as information, not as advice about your specific loan.

What Happened

ATTOM released its Mid-Year 2026 U.S. Foreclosure Market Report on July 16, 2026. The report tracks foreclosure activity across more than 3,000 counties. One section of it looks at speed, meaning how long the process takes from the first filing to the day it is finished.

Nationally, homes foreclosed in the second quarter of 2026 had been in the process an average of 563 days. That is the lowest figure since 2013. It was down 2 percent from the previous quarter and down 13 percent from a year earlier. ATTOM noted the national average has now fallen for seven quarters in a row.

Then comes the state list. The five states with the longest average foreclosure timelines in the second quarter of 2026 were Louisiana at 3,491 days, Hawaii at 2,293 days, New York at 2,007 days, Connecticut at 1,626 days, and Nevada at 1,507 days. Nevada ranked fifth longest in the country.

The other end of the list looks nothing like that. The shortest averages were Texas at 155 days, New Hampshire at 157 days, Wyoming at 173 days, West Virginia at 196 days, and Alaska at 199 days. A homeowner in Texas and a homeowner in Nevada are living under two very different clocks.

Put those two side by side and the size of the gap is easier to feel. Nevada's average is nearly ten times the Texas average. It is also longer than the national average by 944 days, which works out to more than two and a half extra years. These are state averages and not rules, so no single homeowner should expect to land exactly on the number. Still, a gap that wide tells you the two states handle this very differently.

Rob Barber, the CEO at ATTOM, described the national picture as a market slowly returning to normal. He pointed to shorter timelines as one sign of that, while also noting that some homeowners appear to be under more financial strain than they were a year ago. Both things can be true at once.

There is one detail in how this number is built that is easy to miss. ATTOM measures homes that actually completed the foreclosure process during the quarter. It is a look backward at finished cases, not a forecast for cases that just started. That distinction matters a lot, and we come back to it below.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

For most people in the valley, this number changes nothing about daily life. Distressed property is a small slice of the local market right now. Short sales and foreclosures combined made up just 1.4 percent of all existing local property sales in June 2026, according to Las Vegas Realtors data reported by FOX5 Vegas. That is up from 0.8 percent a year earlier, but it is still a very small share.

For a homeowner who is behind, though, the number is worth understanding. A long average timeline in Nevada suggests that the process here rarely moves quickly. In practice, that usually means a homeowner who falls behind has room to make phone calls, gather documents, and look at real options before anything final happens.

Here is the part that needs to be said plainly. A long average does not mean you should wait. It does not mean you should stop opening mail from your loan servicer, and it does not mean you should skip a payment because you assume nothing will happen for years. The average includes cases that were contested in court, cases where a loan modification was reviewed and reworked more than once, cases delayed by bankruptcy filings, and cases that stalled for reasons that had nothing to do with the homeowner. Those long, tangled files pull the average up. An individual timeline can be far shorter than 1,507 days.

It also helps to be concrete about what that time is actually for. Time lets you gather your paperwork before you need it, including recent pay stubs, bank statements, a written budget, and a short explanation of what changed. Time lets you compare more than one option instead of grabbing the first one offered. Most of all, time keeps you out of the worst version of this, which is making a permanent decision about your home in a panic during one bad week.

One more note for anyone comparing neighborhoods. This is a statewide figure. ATTOM reports the timeline at the state level, so Henderson, North Las Vegas, Summerlin and the rest of Clark County are all folded into the same 1,507 day average. There is no separate published timeline for each city in the valley. If someone tells you the process runs faster or slower in one part of town than another, ask where that number came from.

The honest way to think about it is this. In Nevada, a homeowner who falls behind usually has time to work the problem, and that time is only useful if you use it. Options that are generally worth asking about include calling your loan servicer early, asking whether forbearance or a loan modification is available, sitting down with a HUD-approved housing counselor, and, for an owner with equity, selling before the process moves further along.

Equity is the piece that has changed most in Southern Nevada. The median price of an existing single-family home here was $490,000 in June 2026, matching the record set in May. An owner who bought several years ago and is now struggling may have real equity in the home. That does not fix a hard month, but it does mean selling is a live option for some people rather than a last resort. It is one option among several, and it is not automatically the right one.

Background and History

A foreclosure timeline is simply a measure of elapsed time. The clock starts when the first foreclosure document is filed against a property and stops when the process is completed. Everything that happens in between, including pauses, is counted.

ATTOM groups foreclosure activity into three phases, and that framework is a useful way to picture the whole thing. The first phase is default, which is the opening notice that a loan is seriously behind. The second is auction, which is a scheduled sale notice. The third is real estate owned, often shortened to REO, which is when a lender takes the property back. A case can stop at any point along the way, and plenty of them do.

That last point deserves its own sentence. A foreclosure process that starts does not always finish. Some loans get brought current. Some get modified. Some homes get sold before a sale date ever arrives. So a completed foreclosure average should not be read as the expected path for every household that misses a payment. It is the timeline for the cases that ran all the way to the end.

States differ for a lot of reasons. Some states route foreclosures through the court system and some do not. Some have more required steps before a sale can move forward. Beyond the rules themselves, the biggest driver is often what the homeowner does. A case where someone responds, applies for help, or disputes something will take longer than a case where no one responds at all. ATTOM's report reports the numbers and does not explain why any single state runs long or short.

Nevada also carries history that other states do not. Southern Nevada was one of the hardest hit housing markets in the country during the crash that began in 2008. A wave that large leaves a long tail, in the form of rules written afterward, older files that took years to clear, and lenders and servicers who learned to move carefully here.

There is one more thing worth knowing about a number like 1,507 days. It is a rear-view mirror. A case that finished in the spring of 2026 after roughly 4.1 years would have started somewhere around early 2022. So Nevada's average is describing loans that entered the pipeline years ago, not loans that ran into trouble this year. Nobody should read it as a prediction of what a case opened in 2026 will look like.

What Happens Next

The first thing to watch is whether the national trend keeps going. Seven straight quarters of shorter timelines is a real pattern, not noise. If that continues, the gap between the national average and Nevada's average could keep getting wider, which would make the contrast even sharper the next time this report comes out.

The second thing to watch is Nevada's own number. It moves quarter to quarter, and a single reading is not a trend. ATTOM updates this data with each quarterly and mid-year foreclosure report, so the next update will show whether 1,507 days was a step down, a step up, or roughly flat. Anyone reading a future headline about this should check which direction the state figure actually moved before drawing a conclusion.

There is also a question worth keeping in the back of your mind. Nevada's average is being held up in part by cases that have been open for a very long time. As those older files finally clear out of the system, the state number can fall on its own, without anything about the process actually changing. That means a lower Nevada figure next quarter would not automatically prove homeowners here have less time. It would just mean the backlog got smaller. Numbers like this need a second look before anyone draws a conclusion from them.

Locally, the number I pay closer attention to is the share of sales that are distressed. Las Vegas Realtors publishes it every month. It sat at 1.4 percent of existing local property sales in June 2026, up from 0.8 percent a year earlier. That is a small number that roughly doubled, which is worth tracking without being worth panicking about. If that share keeps climbing month after month, it will tell us more about the health of the local market than any national timeline average ever will.

Ryan's Take

The headline gap here is wide enough that it sounds made up, and I understand why people share it. But I would rather people take the useful part away than the shocking part. The useful part is that in Nevada, falling behind on a mortgage is usually not a cliff. It is a slow process with steps, and steps mean chances to act.

What You Can Do

If you have missed a payment or you can see one coming, call your loan servicer before the next due date. Servicers have programs that most homeowners never hear about, and many of them are easier to qualify for early than late. Write down who you spoke with and when. Keep every letter you get, even the ones you do not want to open.

Before you make that call, it helps to have a few things in front of you. Have your loan number, a rough monthly budget, your most recent income documents, and a short honest summary of what happened and whether you expect it to be temporary or ongoing. Servicers ask different questions depending on which programs you might fit into. Having the basics ready turns a frustrating call into a productive one, and it usually shortens how long the whole review takes.

Talk to a HUD-approved housing counselor. This service is free or low cost, and the counselors are independent, meaning they do not work for your lender and are not trying to sell you anything. You can find one through HUD's counselor search or the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, or by calling HUD's housing counseling line at 800-569-4287. For questions about your legal rights and deadlines in Nevada, talk to a licensed Nevada attorney.

Be careful with anyone who contacts you first and asks for money up front to save your home. Real counseling does not work that way. And if you want to understand where you stand on equity, that is a straightforward question with a real answer, and it is worth knowing before you decide anything. Knowing your number does not commit you to selling. It just means you are choosing between options instead of guessing.
