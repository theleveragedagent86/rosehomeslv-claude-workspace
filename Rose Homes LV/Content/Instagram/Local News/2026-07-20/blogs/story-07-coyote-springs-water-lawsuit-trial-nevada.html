Developers Want $1.5 Billion From Nevada Over a Failed City, and the Trial Just Started

A trial that opened this month in Las Vegas could decide whether Nevada taxpayers owe a group of California developers more than $1.5 billion. The case is about groundwater, and the result could shape how much new housing Clark County is allowed to build for years to come.

Opening statements began on Wednesday, July 8, 2026, at the Regional Justice Center in downtown Las Vegas. District Judge Mark Denton is presiding. On one side are the Seeno brothers, a California-based building family behind a stalled project called Coyote Springs. On the other side is the State of Nevada.

The Seenos say the state destroyed the value of their land. They planned a large new community about 50 miles north of Las Vegas. To build it, they needed to pump a lot of groundwater. The state engineer determined that pumping would conflict with older water rights held by other users, and the project never got off the ground. Court filings show the developers are seeking at least $1.5 billion, plus interest and attorneys' fees.

Nevada disagrees. State water regulators have won the key rulings so far. In January 2024, the Nevada Supreme Court backed the state engineer's authority to treat six connected basins as a single groundwater source.

Nothing has been decided. This is an active trial that is expected to run for months. Both sides still get to put on their evidence, and the judge has not ruled on anything.

If you own a home in Southern Nevada, this matters more than the average courthouse headline. Water is the ceiling on how many homes this valley can build. This case asks a hard question about who pays when that ceiling comes down on a landowner.

What Happened

The Coyote Springs project sits in Coyote Springs Valley, spread across parts of Clark and Lincoln counties. It was pitched as a full city with homes, schools, shops and golf. A city that size needs a lot of water, and in that part of Nevada the only real source is groundwater pumped from underground.

That is where the trouble started. Nevada water law follows a simple order of priority. Older claims get served first. That idea is called senior water rights. If you filed your claim in 1900 and your neighbor filed in 1990, you are senior and your neighbor is junior. In a shortage, the senior right gets water before the junior one does. It is close to a line at the store, and the people who got there first do not have to move.

The state engineer, who is Nevada's top water regulator, looked at the pumping Coyote Springs would need. The finding was that the pumping would conflict with senior rights in the larger Muddy River watershed. Groundwater in that area feeds springs and river flow that other users, and protected species, already depend on. Once that conflict was found, the water the project counted on was no longer available at the scale it needed.

The second piece is the basins. For a long time, Nevada managed groundwater basin by basin, almost as if each one were its own bucket. Scientists and regulators concluded that several of these basins are actually connected underground. Pumping hard in one place can pull the water table down somewhere else miles away. So the state engineer began managing six connected basins as one shared source, a system known as the Lower White River Flow System.

The developers challenged that. A lower court initially sided with them. Then, on January 25, 2024, the Nevada Supreme Court reversed and held that the state engineer did have the authority to manage those connected basins together in order to protect senior rights and the public interest.

That brings us to this trial, filed in 2020 and finally being heard now. The developers argue that the state's actions took away nearly all the value of their private property, so the state should pay for it. In plain terms, this is the shape of a regulatory takings argument. The general idea is that when a government rule strips so much value out of private property that the owner is left with almost nothing, the owner should be compensated. The state's position, reflected in the rulings so far, is that it was managing a shared public resource under the law and protecting people who were in line first.

Both of those arguments are claims. A judge has not decided which one wins.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Nevada is the driest state in the country. That single fact drives more of our housing market than most people realize. Land is not the only limit on growth here. Water is.

Every new subdivision in Clark County has to be served by a water system. That system has to have supply behind it. When supply is tight, approvals get harder, projects shrink, and the homes that do get built cost more. That is a big part of why Las Vegas home prices and rents have climbed the way they have. Demand keeps arriving and supply has a hard ceiling.

The state engineer is the person who enforces that ceiling. That job involves saying no to people who want to pump more than the system can give. It is not a popular job, and it was never designed to be.

Here is the honest stake for local homeowners. If a landowner can win a very large damage award because a water ruling went against them, then every future ruling carries a price tag. A regulator who has to weigh a possible billion-dollar bill may decide differently than one who does not. That could cut in either direction over time. It could make the state more cautious about restricting pumping, which would open the door to more building. It could also make the state more cautious about issuing new water rights in the first place, which would slow building down. Nobody honestly knows which way it lands.

There is also a straightforward taxpayer question. Nevada does not have a state income tax. If the state ends up owing a very large judgment, that money comes from somewhere, and Nevadans are the ones who fund the state budget. That is why the case has drawn attention well beyond people who follow water policy.

None of this happens in a vacuum. Southern Nevada is already working hard to hold onto the water it has. This month the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation agreed to pay up to $65 million to leave as much as 200,000 acre-feet of Colorado River water in Lake Mead, which is the reservoir that supplies almost every faucet in this valley. That deal was reported by the Las Vegas Review-Journal on July 17, 2026. When the region is paying millions of dollars to keep water in storage, a case about the right to pump billions of dollars worth of groundwater is not a side issue.

Background and History

The story of this land goes back almost 40 years. In 1988, Congress approved the Nevada-Florida Land Exchange Authorization Act. That law traded federal land in Coyote Springs Valley and Mineral County to Aerojet in exchange for environmentally sensitive private land in the Florida Everglades. Aerojet wanted to test rocket motors out there. Military contracts were cut back, and the test site was never built.

In 1998, Reno lobbyist Harvey Whittemore bought the land for about $23 million. His plan was much bigger than a rocket range. Early versions called for tens of thousands of homes and 10 golf courses. Over time the pitch grew into a full city with well over 100,000 homes, complete with schools, shopping and parks. Whittemore brought in partners, including Pardee Homes and the Seeno family.

Some of it actually got built. A golf course designed by Jack Nicklaus broke ground in 2005 and opened in 2008. Roads, flood control work and utility lines went in. Court filings and reporting by the Las Vegas Review-Journal describe more than $200 million in spending on that infrastructure. What never got built was the housing. Not one home.

The water fight ran alongside all of it. The project was counting on roughly 30,000 acre-feet of groundwater per year drawn from several basins. As the state engineer's understanding of the connected basins developed, the amount the project could count on was cut sharply. Former State Engineer Jason King ultimately determined the pumping could not go forward because of conflicts with senior rights in the Muddy River watershed.

Coyote Springs Investment LLC and the Seeno family sued the state in 2020. Six years later, that case finally reached opening statements.

What Happens Next

The immediate answer is patience. The trial is expected to last months, not weeks. Water cases are slow because they turn on technical evidence about geology, pumping models and decades of paperwork. Expect a long parade of expert witnesses.

After the presentation of evidence, the court will have to answer two separate questions. The first is whether the developers had a protected property interest that the state took. The second, only if the answer to the first is yes, is what that interest was actually worth. The $1.5 billion figure is what the developers are asking for. It is a demand, not a ruling, and damage awards in cases like this frequently land somewhere different from the number in the complaint.

Whatever the trial court decides, an appeal is very likely. The losing side in a case of this size almost always asks a higher court to take another look. That means the final word could still be years away, and the Nevada Supreme Court has already weighed in once on the underlying water question.

There is a policy track too. Nevada's Legislature meets in odd-numbered years, so the next regular session is in 2027. If this case creates a large financial exposure for the state, lawmakers would have a chance to respond then. That could take the form of changes to water law, to how the state engineer's decisions are reviewed, or to nothing at all. I am not going to guess.

For now, the practical takeaway is that nothing changes today. Water rules in Clark County are the same this week as they were last week. Your water bill, your service, and your ability to buy or sell a home are not affected by an ongoing trial.

Ryan's Take

I sell homes in a desert. That is not a poetic line, it is the actual business. Every conversation about where Las Vegas grows next eventually runs into the same wall, and that wall is water.

What makes this case worth watching is not the dollar figure, as attention grabbing as it is. It is the precedent question underneath it. Nevada has spent decades building a system where one office gets to say how much water comes out of the ground. That system is why our supply has held up better than a lot of people predicted. If that office starts having to price the risk of a lawsuit into every decision, the job changes.

I also try to be fair to the other side of this. The developers bought land, spent real money on roads and utilities, and were told by the state that they could not use the water they had planned around. Whether that adds up to a legal taking is exactly what the court is there to sort out. Reasonable people land in different places on that, and I am not the one who gets to decide it.

What I tell clients is simple. Do not read a trial headline as a market signal. This will not move prices in Summerlin or Henderson this year. But if you are thinking about land, raw acreage, or a long-horizon build somewhere on the edge of the valley, water availability is the first question to ask, not the last.

What You Can Do

Most homeowners do not need to do anything about this case. It is worth understanding, not worth worrying about. Still, there are a few practical moves if water and growth affect your plans.

If you are buying land or raw acreage anywhere outside an established service area, confirm the water situation in writing before you go under contract. Ask who the water purveyor is, whether the parcel already has committed service, and what it would cost to connect. A lot cannot be a homesite without water, no matter how good the price looks.

If you are buying a normal home inside the valley, you are on the Southern Nevada Water Authority system and this trial does not touch your service. You can still check the Southern Nevada Water Authority website for current conservation rules, especially landscape rules, because those do change and they affect what you can plant.

If you follow local growth debates, keep an eye on the county and city meetings where new projects get approved. Water availability comes up constantly in those hearings, and they are open to the public. That is where policy actually shows up in your neighborhood.

And if you own in an area where new development has been proposed nearby, watch the approval calendar rather than the courthouse. Zoning votes will change your street long before an appellate ruling does.
