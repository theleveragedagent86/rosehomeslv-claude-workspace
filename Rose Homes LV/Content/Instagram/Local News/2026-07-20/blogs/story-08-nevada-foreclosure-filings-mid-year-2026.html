Foreclosures Jumped 21 Percent Nationally. Nevada's Went Up Just 6 Percent

Foreclosure filings across the United States jumped 21.26 percent in the first half of 2026. In Nevada, they went up just 6.38 percent. That gap is the whole story here, and it is a good reminder that a national real estate headline is not a local real estate report.

Here are the two numbers side by side. ATTOM, a national property data company, counted 227,548 U.S. properties with a foreclosure filing between January and June 2026. Nevada had 2,935 filings over those same six months. The national count is up 21.26 percent from a year ago and up 28.25 percent from the first half of 2024. Nevada's increase was one of the four smallest of any state in the country.

Now here is the part that a lot of quick coverage will skip. Nevada's foreclosure rate is still worse than the national rate. Nationally, 0.16 percent of all housing units had a filing, which works out to one in every 632 homes. In Nevada it was 0.22 percent, or one in every 452 housing units. Both of those facts are true at the same time.

So the honest read is this. The trend line in Nevada is much flatter than the alarming national headline suggests. But Nevada started from a higher base, and it is still sitting on that higher base today. If you only read the first half of that sentence, you get a comfortable story that is not accurate. If you only read the second half, you get a frightening story that is not accurate either. This article is going to give you both halves.

What Happened

ATTOM released its Mid-Year 2026 U.S. Foreclosure Market Report on July 16, 2026. The report counts properties, not people and not events. If a single home got three different notices in six months, it still counts once.

A "foreclosure filing" is a broad category. It covers three different things. The first is a default notice, which is the early paperwork a lender files when a borrower falls behind. The second is a scheduled auction. The third is an actual bank repossession, where the lender takes the home back. A default notice is the first step, not the last one, and many of them never end in a lost home.

That matters because the headline number mixes early warnings in with finished foreclosures. A rising filing count can mean more people are in trouble. It can also mean lenders are simply processing paperwork faster than they were a year ago. Usually it is some of both.

Nationally, the totals were 227,548 properties in the first six months of 2026. That is 21.26 percent higher than the first half of 2025 and 28.25 percent higher than the first half of 2024. So this is not a one year blip. The national number has been climbing for two straight years.

Nevada's number moved much less. The state logged 2,935 filings, up 6.38 percent from a year ago. To put that in plain terms, Nevada's growth rate was roughly a third of the national growth rate. Nevada also accounted for about 1.3 percent of all U.S. foreclosure filings in the period, out of a country with 50 states.

The contrast gets sharper when you look at the states on the other end. Idaho, Colorado, Georgia and North Carolina all posted increases between 47 and 59 percent. Those are markets that saw huge price run-ups and heavy in-migration during the last few years. Nevada, which people love to describe as a boom and bust market, was one of the calmest states in the country on this particular measure.

But the rate tells a different story than the growth. Nevada's foreclosure rate of one in every 452 housing units is meaningfully worse than the national rate of one in every 632. Put another way, a Nevada home was roughly 40 percent more likely to have a foreclosure filing in the first half of 2026 than the average American home. The state did not have a bad six months. It has a higher baseline than most of the country, and that baseline did not go away.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Most people who read a national foreclosure headline are really asking one question. Is my neighborhood about to fill up with distressed sales? The best local answer we have right now says no.

In June 2026, short sales and foreclosures combined made up only 1.4 percent of all existing local property sales in Southern Nevada. That is about one in every 71 sales. A year earlier the figure was 0.8 percent. So yes, the share roughly doubled. But it doubled from a very small number to a still very small number.

Compare that to the crash years, when distressed property was not a rounding error but the entire market. At 1.4 percent, distressed sales are not setting prices in Summerlin, Henderson, North Las Vegas or anywhere else in the valley. They are a footnote. If you are worried that foreclosure activity is about to drag your home value down, the local sales data does not support that fear right now.

Another local number worth watching stayed almost perfectly flat. Cash buyers made up 23.2 percent of local sales in June 2026, compared to 23.1 percent a year earlier. Cash share tends to spike when investors smell distress and start hunting bargains. A move of one tenth of one percentage point is not a spike. It is a market doing the same thing it did last year.

There is a second reason this matters, and it has nothing to do with data. It has to do with how people make decisions. When a homeowner reads "foreclosures up 21 percent," they sometimes panic and list a house they did not need to sell. When a buyer reads it, they sometimes wait for a wave of cheap inventory that never shows up here. Both of those are expensive mistakes built on a number that describes 49 other states more than it describes this one.

At the same time, I am not going to pretend the higher Nevada rate does not exist. One in 452 is a real number attached to real households. Roughly 2,935 Nevada properties got some kind of foreclosure paperwork in six months. Behind a lot of those filings is a family dealing with a job loss, a medical bill, a divorce, or an insurance and tax payment that outran the budget. The market being stable does not mean everyone in it is fine.

Background and History

To understand why Nevada's higher base is not a surprise, you have to go back about eighteen years. Las Vegas was one of the hardest hit housing markets in the country during the 2008 crash. Nevada led the nation in foreclosure rate for several years running.

The reasons were specific to this valley. Prices had run up fast in the mid 2000s. A large share of loans were adjustable or low documentation. The local economy leaned heavily on tourism, construction and gaming, and all three fell apart at once. When jobs disappeared, mortgage payments disappeared right behind them.

That experience left two marks that still show up in the data. The first is cultural. Nevada lawmakers added homeowner protections after the crash, and the state's foreclosure process picked up more steps than most states have. The second is structural. Nevada still has a service based economy that moves harder in both directions than the national average.

When the country does well, Las Vegas often does better. When the country slows, Las Vegas often feels it first and feels it more. That is why a slightly elevated foreclosure rate here, even in calm years, is fairly normal. It is a feature of the local economy, not a signal that something new is breaking.

It is also worth remembering how different the loans are now. The mortgages written between roughly 2012 and 2026 were underwritten to far stricter standards than the mortgages written between 2004 and 2007. Most homeowners in Clark County today also have real equity, because prices have climbed for years. A homeowner with equity who gets in trouble usually has an option the 2008 homeowner did not have, which is to sell the house and walk away with money instead of losing it at auction.

What Happens Next

ATTOM publishes this report twice a year, plus monthly updates, so the next real checkpoint on these figures comes with the year-end 2026 release. The number to watch in Nevada is not the total. It is the direction of the growth rate.

If Nevada's year-over-year increase stays in the single digits while the national number keeps climbing, that tells us the gap is structural and steady. If Nevada's growth rate starts catching up to the national rate, that is a genuine change worth paying attention to. Right now, one report showing 6.38 percent is a data point, not a trend you can bank on.

The local numbers to watch are simpler and they come out monthly from Las Vegas Realtors. Keep an eye on the distressed share of sales. As long as short sales and foreclosures stay somewhere near that 1.4 percent mark, the valley is fine. If that share climbs past 3 or 4 percent and keeps going, that would be the early sign of a real shift.

Cash buyer share is the second local tell. Investors move before headlines do. If cash share jumps well above the 23 percent range it has held for a year, that usually means somebody with money thinks bargains are coming.

I would also watch local jobs more than I watch mortgage rates. Foreclosure filings do not spike because rates move. They spike because paychecks stop. Southern Nevada's job market, especially in hospitality and construction, is the single best leading indicator of what our foreclosure numbers will look like a year from now.

One more thing to expect. You are going to keep seeing national foreclosure headlines this year, and they are going to keep sounding worse than what is happening on your street. That is not the reporters being dishonest. It is just what happens when one number gets averaged across a country with 50 very different housing markets.

Ryan's Take

I have been asked some version of "is Vegas about to crash again" almost every week for years. This report is a good example of why the answer keeps being no, and also why I refuse to say everything is perfect.

Nevada growing at 6.38 percent while the country grows at 21.26 percent is genuinely good news. That is not spin. Four states in the entire country had a smaller increase than we did. For a market with our reputation, that is a quietly impressive result.

But I am not going to hide the other number, because you would find it eventually and then you would rightly stop trusting me. One in 452 Nevada homes is worse than one in 632 nationally. We are still carrying a higher foreclosure rate than most of the country. Anybody who tells you Nevada is fine and everyone else is in trouble is only reading half the report.

The way I explain it to clients is with a car analogy. Our speed is lower than everyone else's right now, which is great. But we are driving on a rougher road than most states, and we always have been. Slower on a rough road is still better than fast on a rough road. It is just not the same as being on a smooth road.

Practically, if you own a home in Clark County and you are current on your mortgage, this report changes nothing about your life. If you are behind, or you can see that you are about to be behind, this report is not your problem. Your loan servicer is your first call, and you should make it early.

What You Can Do

If you are actually behind on a mortgage payment, or you know you are about to be, the single most useful thing you can do is contact your loan servicer early. Servicers have far more options when you call before you miss payments than after you have missed several. That is true whether you are in Las Vegas, Henderson, North Las Vegas or Boulder City.

You can also get free help. A HUD-approved housing counselor can review your situation and explain your options at no cost to you. These are nonprofit counselors, not sales people, and using one does not commit you to anything. If somebody calls you offering to stop a foreclosure for an upfront fee, that is a warning sign, and the free counselor is the safer first stop.

I want to be clear about my own role here. I am a real estate agent. I am not an attorney, a lender, or a HUD housing counselor, and nothing in this article is legal or financial advice. If you are facing a foreclosure, you should talk to people who are licensed to help with that specifically.

Where I can help is on the real estate side of the question. If you have equity and selling is on the table, I can tell you what your home is realistically worth in today's market and how fast it would likely sell. Sometimes that changes the whole conversation, because a homeowner who thought they were stuck finds out they actually have room to move.

And if you are a buyer hoping this report means cheap foreclosures are coming to Summerlin, I would gently reset that expectation. At 1.4 percent of local sales, distressed inventory is not where the opportunity is in this valley right now.
