UFC Legend Randy Couture Lists His Southern Highlands Home for $1.1 Million

Randy Couture, the six-time UFC champion and UFC Hall of Famer, has put his Las Vegas home on the market for $1.1 million. He is moving full time to his private ranch in Arizona, according to the Las Vegas Review-Journal.

The house sits in The Foothills, a guard-gated pocket inside the Southern Highlands master plan in the southwest valley. It has four bedrooms and four bathrooms across 3,743 square feet. The lot is 0.38 acres, a little more than a third of an acre.

The listing is held by Ariel Ives of The Barton Group at Keller Williams Las Vegas. Anyone who wants to tour the home or write an offer on it should go through her office. This article is news coverage and market commentary. It is not a pitch on another agent's listing.

Here is the part people are sharing. The home sold new in 2003 for $456,355. Twenty three years later the asking price is $1.1 million. That looks like a jackpot, and it is a real gain, but the plain math is smaller than the headline makes it feel.

That gap between the story and the math is why this listing is worth a closer look. Most homeowners in the valley have their own version of this same question. You bought years ago, you watched the news say prices hit a record, and you want to know what your house is actually worth today. This one property gives us a clean way to walk through it.

What Happened

The Review-Journal reported the listing on July 10, 2026. Couture is selling the Southern Highlands house and moving to his ranch in Arizona full time. The paper reported the asking price at $1.1 million.

The home was custom built in 2003. It measures 3,743 square feet with four bedrooms and four bathrooms. At the asking price, that works out to roughly $294 a square foot.

The features listed in the coverage tell you where the money went over the years. The roof carries 52 solar panels, and the reporting notes a $12,000 credit with NV Energy. The backyard has a heated pool with a new Pebbletek finish. The house also has smart home integration.

One detail makes this listing different from a normal sale. Ariel Ives, the listing agent, said some of Couture's memorabilia will convey through a separate bill of sale. That means the fight items are not part of the real estate contract. They would trade as personal property in a side agreement.

Now the math everyone wants. The original 2003 sale price was $456,355. The current ask is $1,100,000. Divide $1,100,000 by $456,355 and you get about 2.41. So the asking price is about 2.41 times the original price.

Spread that over 23 years and it comes out to roughly 3.9 percent compound growth per year. That is a solid long-run return on a house. It is not the lottery ticket the raw numbers suggest.

Two honest caveats belong right next to that number. First, $1.1 million is an asking price, not a sale price. What the home actually closes for may be higher or lower, and we will not know until it closes.

Second, the 2003 figure was the original purchase price of a custom home. Over 23 years the owner added things. The solar array and the new pool finish are two examples named in the coverage. Those improvements cost money, and that money is part of the difference between $456,355 and $1.1 million. This is not a clean apples-to-apples appreciation figure.

None of this takes anything away from the home. A well-kept custom house on a third of an acre behind a guard gate is a genuinely good property. It just means the price history is a story about a specific house, not a scoreboard for the whole valley.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

A single listing does not move the market. What it does is give us a real, public number to measure against. Most of us never see what a neighbor paid or asked. This one is in the paper.

Start with the baseline. Las Vegas Realtors reported a June 2026 median price of $490,000 for existing single-family homes. That matched the record set in May and was up about 1 percent from a year earlier, as reported by FOX5 Vegas.

Put the two numbers side by side. At $1.1 million, this home is asking about 2.2 times the valley median. That places it in the upper tier of the local market. It does not place it in the true luxury bracket, where prices in Las Vegas run well past $2 million.

That middle spot is exactly why the listing is useful. It is the price band a lot of move-up buyers here actually shop. Doctors, business owners, executives and long-time homeowners cashing out equity all end up looking between $900,000 and $1.4 million.

There is one more piece of context. A Redfin report ranked the Las Vegas Valley third in the nation for how fast luxury home prices are climbing, behind two Florida metros, as covered by the Review-Journal. Demand at the top of our market has been strong.

Buyers in this band tend to shop from a pretty consistent list. They want a primary bedroom on the ground floor, a three-car garage, and a backyard big enough for a real pool plus some shade. They want a gate, and they want a short drive to the freeway.

That list is why homes like this one hold their value. You cannot add a third of an acre to a lot after the fact. You cannot move a house closer to the 215. Those things are baked in, and they are what separates a 2003 custom home from newer product on a smaller footprint.

So how should a regular homeowner read all of this? Carefully. Headline "then versus now" numbers almost always flatter the market, because they leave out three things.

The first is improvements. Every dollar of pool work, solar, flooring and roofing that an owner spends shows up in the sale price later. That is not appreciation. That is money the owner already paid.

The second is carrying costs. Over 23 years an owner pays property taxes, insurance, HOA dues, mortgage interest and repairs. Those are real dollars, and they never show up in a "bought for X, listed for Y" headline.

The third is inflation. A dollar in 2003 does not buy what a dollar buys in 2026. Some of that 2.41 multiple is just the currency changing, not the house getting more valuable.

Take all three out and the honest takeaway is this. Long-run Las Vegas appreciation has been solid but not magical. Owning here for two decades has generally worked. It has not been free money.

Background and History

Southern Highlands sits in the far southwest valley, near the I-15 and the 215 Beltway. It is a master-planned community that filled in mostly through the late 1990s and the 2000s. It is known locally for its golf course, its country club and its collection of gated neighborhoods.

The Foothills is one of the guard-gated enclaves inside that master plan. Guard-gated means a staffed gate, not just a keypad and a rolling arm. That staffing is paid for through association dues, and it is one of the main reasons buyers pay more per square foot in these pockets.

The lot size matters here too. At 0.38 acres, this property has roughly 16,500 square feet of land. A lot of newer tract lots in the valley come in well under half that. Land is one of the things you are actually buying in a 2003-era custom neighborhood, and it is hard to replace in newer construction.

The 2003 build date fits the era. That was the middle of a massive building run in Clark County, when the southwest valley was going up fast. Homes from that period tend to have larger lots, taller ceilings and floor plans built around a backyard.

Location is a big part of why Southern Highlands still draws move-up buyers. You are close to the I-15 for a straight shot toward the airport and the Strip, and close to the 215 for a run across the south valley toward Henderson. For people who work on the Strip or travel a lot, that access is worth real money.

The trade-off is that you are on the edge of town. Some daily errands mean a longer drive than they would from a more central neighborhood. Buyers who value quiet and space usually decide that trade is fine. Buyers who want to walk to everything usually shop somewhere else.

On the owner's side, Couture is one of the most recognizable names in the sport. He won UFC titles at both heavyweight and light heavyweight and is in the UFC Hall of Fame. Las Vegas has long been a home base for the sport, so fighters living in valley neighborhoods is not unusual here.

Beyond the reported fact that he is relocating to his Arizona ranch, there is nothing more to read into the move. People sell houses for all kinds of ordinary reasons. A change of home base is the reason given, and that is where the story ends.

What Happens Next

From here the listing does what every listing does. It gets showings, it gets feedback, and it either finds a buyer near the asking price or the price gets adjusted. The closed number is the one worth waiting for.

When it does close, that sale becomes a comparable sale for The Foothills. Appraisers and agents will use it. Neighbors thinking about selling will use it. That is the real value of this listing for people who live nearby.

Buyers in this band should expect a few specific hurdles. A loan on a $1.1 million purchase is large enough that many buyers land in jumbo territory rather than a standard conforming loan. Jumbo underwriting tends to ask for more reserves and stricter documentation, so getting fully pre-approved before touring matters more at this price than it does at $450,000.

Solar is the other thing to pin down. A 52-panel array is a real asset if the system is owned free and clear. If a system is leased or financed, the buyer usually has to qualify to take over the agreement, and that can slow a closing down. The public reporting does not say which arrangement applies here, so any buyer would need to confirm that directly with the listing agent. The memorabilia is a separate track entirely. Because it is handled through a bill of sale, it does not become part of the appraised value of the real estate. A lender will not finance it. A buyer who wants those items negotiates them as personal property, on their own terms and with their own money.

For everyone else in the valley, the thing to watch is not this one house. It is whether the upper tier keeps holding up. Las Vegas Realtors publishes a market report every month, and the June figure of $490,000 is the number to compare each new release against.

Ryan's Take

The number that will travel on social media is "$456,355 to $1.1 million." I understand why. It is a great hook and it is technically true.

But the honest read is roughly 3.9 percent a year over 23 years. That is a good, steady return. It is not the kind of number that should make anyone quit their job and start flipping houses.

I also want to be clear about the improvements. Fifty two solar panels and a fresh Pebbletek pool finish did not appear on their own. An owner wrote checks for those. When you subtract owner investment, the pure appreciation piece gets smaller again.

And an asking price is a hope, not a fact. I have seen homes list high and close under. I have seen homes list conservatively and close over. Until it records, $1.1 million is a number on a flyer.

The last thing I will say is about the celebrity angle. A famous owner gets a listing more eyeballs. It does not add value to an appraisal. What sells this house is the same thing that sells any house in The Foothills. Good square footage, a big lot, a guard gate and a backyard people want to spend time in.

What You Can Do

Do the honest math on your own home. Take your purchase price, add up what you have spent on real improvements, and compare that to a current value estimate. That number is far more useful than any headline about someone else's house.

If you own in Southern Highlands or a similar guard-gated pocket, put this listing on your watch list. Do not react to the asking price. Wait for the closed price, because that is the figure that will show up in your own appraisal later.

If you are shopping in the $900,000 to $1.4 million range, get your financing settled first. Talk to a lender about jumbo options before you fall in love with a floor plan. Knowing your real ceiling saves you from wasted weekends.

If you are looking at any home with solar panels, ask one question early. Is the system owned, leased or financed? The answer changes your monthly cost and it can change your closing timeline.

And if you see a home you like with another agent's sign in the yard, remember that agent works for the seller. Having your own representation costs you nothing on most purchases and keeps someone in your corner during negotiation and inspection.
