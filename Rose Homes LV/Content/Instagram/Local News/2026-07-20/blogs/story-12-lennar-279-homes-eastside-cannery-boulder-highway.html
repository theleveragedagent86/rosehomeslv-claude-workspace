279 Homes Approved for the Old Eastside Cannery Site on Boulder Highway

The Clark County Planning Commission approved Lennar Corp.'s plan for a 279-lot subdivision on the old Eastside Cannery site along Boulder Highway. It matters because a shuttered casino property would become one of the densest new single-family neighborhoods in the east valley, and because county staff had asked the commission to say no.

The site sits on about 29.5 acres at Boulder Highway and Harmon Avenue, in unincorporated Clark County. Lennar wants to build two-story single-family homes there. The plan calls for compact lots with small front and back yards, plus two park areas and a trail.

County staff recommended denial. Their main concern was the driveways. The plan includes 5-foot driveways, which staff said create unsafe backing conditions. Staff also flagged limited street parking across the subdivision.

The Planning Commission approved it anyway. That vote is not the last word. The project now goes to the Clark County Commission for final action at the August 5, 2026 meeting. An earlier vote on the project had already been postponed once.

So nothing is settled. As of today, this is an approved plan at one level of government waiting on a second level. If you live near Boulder Highway, or you are watching for new construction in the east valley, this is the date to circle.

Below, I walk through what the commission actually approved, what the density numbers really mean, why staff raised the objections they did, and what happens between now and August 5.

What Happened

Lennar Corp. is one of the largest homebuilders in the country. It brought forward a plan to build 279 two-story single-family homes on the former Eastside Cannery property. The land is roughly 29.5 acres along Boulder Highway at Harmon Avenue.

Here is the math that drives this whole story. Divide 279 lots by 29.5 acres and you get about 9.5 lots per acre. That is dense for detached single-family homes in Clark County. Most newer tract neighborhoods in the valley are platted at lower densities than that.

Now translate that into square feet. One acre is 43,560 square feet. So 29.5 acres is about 1.29 million square feet. Spread across 279 homes, that works out to roughly 4,600 square feet of land per home before you take anything out.

But you do have to take things out. Streets, sidewalks, the two park areas and the trail all come off the top. Once you subtract those, the actual private lots land meaningfully below 4,600 square feet each. My rough estimate puts them somewhere in the low 3,000s [NOT VERIFIED]. Lennar has not published lot dimensions publicly, so treat that as arithmetic, not a reported figure.

That is what "compact lots with small front and back yards" means in plain English. The homes go up rather than out. Two stories on a small footprint is how you fit 279 units on 29.5 acres.

County staff looked at that layout and recommended denial. The driveway issue was the headline. A 5-foot driveway is the distance from the garage door to the sidewalk or street. Five feet is not long enough to park a car.

Think about your own vehicle. A midsize sedan runs about 16 feet long. A full-size pickup can be more than 19 feet. Either one parked in a 5-foot driveway hangs well out over the sidewalk and into the street. Staff said that setup creates unsafe backing conditions, meaning drivers reversing out have less room and worse sightlines.

Staff also raised limited street parking. Those two concerns are connected. When driveways are too short to hold a car, the extra cars go to the curb. If the curb space is already tight, the street fills up fast.

None of that is a small technical quibble. It is a real design critique from the people whose job is to review these plans. The Planning Commission heard it and approved the project anyway, which is something the commission is allowed to do.

What the Planning Commission did is a recommendation and an approval at its level. The Clark County Commission holds the final vote on August 5, 2026.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Start with supply. Las Vegas builders pulled 2,918 new home permits through April 2026, down 24 percent from the same stretch a year earlier. In all of 2025 they pulled 9,734 permits, the lowest annual total since 2016. New construction here has slowed down a lot.

Against that backdrop, 279 homes is not nothing. It is one subdivision, and it will not move the valley's price picture by itself. But when permits are falling, every approved project carries more weight than it would in a busy year.

Second, location matters. This is infill. The land is already disturbed, already served by roads and utilities, and already sitting on a major corridor. Building here does not push the valley's edge further into open desert.

That connects to a bigger argument happening right now. A new analysis by RCG Economics says the widely quoted figure of nearly 80,000 vacant or underutilized acres in Clark County overstates what can actually be built on. The report found the underlying study counted parking lots, steep slopes and mountaintops.

If that is right, the valley has less usable land than the headline number suggests. And that makes sites like this one more consequential, not less. A 29.5-acre parcel with existing infrastructure on a major road is a genuinely useful piece of land.

Third, Boulder Highway itself. It is a major east valley corridor that has seen very little new single-family construction. Most of what has gone up in this part of town for years has been apartments, commercial space, or nothing at all.

Putting 279 owner-occupied households on that corridor changes the mix. More rooftops usually means more demand for nearby retail and services. It also puts more residents near a transit line, which is part of why planners generally favor housing on corridors like this.

Now the other side, because it is real. Staff's objections were not trivial. Five-foot driveways will not fit most vehicles, so cars end up on the street. That changes how a neighborhood feels to live in and to drive through.

Dense street parking makes it harder for emergency vehicles to get through. It makes backing out of a driveway riskier, especially with kids around. And it tends to spill over into whatever neighborhood sits next door.

Neighbors have fair reasons to raise concerns about density here. Nearly 9.5 units per acre next to lower-density blocks is a visible change. Traffic on Boulder Highway is already heavy, and 279 households add trips.

There is also a buyer angle worth naming. Smaller lots mean less land cost per home, and land is a big share of what a new house costs here. That is often how builders reach a lower price point on a detached home.

So the density is not just a planning decision. It is usually a price decision. Buyers who want a detached house with a yard and a garage, at a payment they can actually make, frequently end up choosing a smaller lot to get there.

Both things can be true. The valley needs housing, and this specific design has issues worth arguing about. That is exactly the tension the county commission has to sort out on August 5.

Background and History

The Eastside Cannery opened as a hotel-casino on Boulder Highway and served the east valley for years. It is one of a handful of locals casinos that anchored that stretch of road. Boyd Gaming owned it.

Boyd closed the property and eventually decided not to reopen it as a casino. The company imploded the tower in March 2026. Before that, Boyd said it planned to sell the property for residential use.

That is an unusual thing to watch happen in Las Vegas. Implosions here are part of local history, and most of them have been on or near the Strip. This one was a neighborhood casino on a commuter corridor coming down to make room for houses.

It also tells you something about where the money is going. A casino site being converted to for-sale housing means the owner concluded homes were worth more than gaming on that parcel. That is a signal about both the local casino market and the housing shortage.

Boulder Highway has been slowly changing for a while. The corridor runs from near downtown Las Vegas southeast toward Henderson. It has carried a lot of older motels, aging commercial strips, and casinos built for locals rather than tourists.

Very little new single-family construction has gone up along it. Builders in this valley have historically pushed toward the outer edges where large tracts of raw land were available and cheaper to develop. Boulder Highway was mostly left to redevelopment, which is slower and harder.

That is what makes this project notable regardless of how you feel about the density. It is an attempt to do the harder kind of building, on a site that already had infrastructure, in a part of the valley that has been skipped over.

An earlier vote on the Lennar project was postponed before the Planning Commission acted. Postponements are common when a project needs revisions or when there are unresolved concerns. The staff recommendation for denial was still in place when the commission approved it.

What Happens Next

The next real date is August 5, 2026. That is when the Clark County Commission takes final action. Until that vote happens, this project is not approved in a final sense.

The county commission is not required to follow the Planning Commission. It can approve the plan as presented. It can approve it with conditions attached. It can send it back for changes, or it can deny it outright.

Conditions are the outcome to watch for. If commissioners share staff's concerns but still want the housing, the natural move is to approve with changes to the driveway depth, the parking count, or the lot layout. That is a very common way these things get resolved.

Watch the driveway number specifically. If a revised plan comes back with deeper driveways, something else has to give. Deeper driveways on the same acreage usually means fewer lots, smaller homes, or a reworked street grid.

That trade-off is the whole story in one sentence. You can have more homes or more parking on a fixed 29.5 acres, but making room for one takes room from the other. Everything else is a detail of how that gets balanced.

If the commission approves it, the project still has a long road. Final maps, permits, grading, and utility work all come before anyone builds a house. Then Lennar has to decide when to actually start construction based on market conditions.

No construction timeline has been reported for this project. No home prices, floor plans, or square footages have been announced either. Anyone telling you what these homes will cost right now is guessing.

If it gets denied or sent back, the site stays vacant for longer. Lennar could revise and return, or Boyd could look for a different buyer. Neither of those happens quickly.

The vote is also worth watching beyond this one parcel. Clark County has more aging commercial and casino sites on corridors like Boulder Highway. How commissioners handle compact infill here gives builders a read on what the county will accept somewhere else.

Ryan's Take

I think the county commission approves this, probably with conditions. The valley needs housing, this is infill on a site that is already cleared, and turning down 279 homes on a major corridor is a hard vote to explain. But I would not be surprised to see the driveway standard get changed first.

I also think staff got the substance right. A 5-foot driveway is not a driveway. It is an apron. Whatever you call it, the second car in a two-car household is going to live at the curb, and so is the third.

That is not a dealbreaker for me. Dense housing works in plenty of places. But it works better when the parking plan is honest about how many cars actually show up, and when the streets are designed for that reality from day one.

Here is what I would tell a buyer looking at a neighborhood like this. Go walk a comparable Lennar community at a similar density on a weeknight around 7 p.m. Look at the curb. That tells you more than any site plan will.

For east valley homeowners, my read is that this project is a net positive for the corridor. New owner-occupied housing tends to bring investment with it. The near-term construction disruption is real, and so is the added traffic, but the long-run direction looks better than a vacant casino pad.

What You Can Do

If you live near Boulder Highway and Harmon, put August 5, 2026 on your calendar. Clark County Commission meetings include public comment. Showing up, or submitting a written comment, is the most direct way to be heard on this.

Look up the agenda a few days ahead on the Clark County website. Agendas post before meetings and will tell you where this item sits on the schedule. That saves you from sitting through hours of unrelated business.

If you plan to speak, be specific. General complaints about density carry less weight than a concrete point about the driveway depth, the parking count, or a particular intersection you drive every day. Commissioners respond to details they can act on.

If you are a buyer interested in new construction in the east valley, do not wait on this project. There is no timeline, no pricing, and no guarantee it gets built as proposed. Keep looking at what is actually available now.

If you own a home nearby and you are wondering what this does to your value, the honest answer is that it depends on execution. New housing on a corridor is usually good for surrounding values over time. I am happy to look at your specific street and give you a straight read.
