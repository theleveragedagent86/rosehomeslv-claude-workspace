A New Study Says Clark County's 80,000 Buildable Acres Includes Parking Lots and Mountaintops

A new analysis says the number everyone quotes about how much land is left in Clark County is badly overstated. The Las Vegas firm RCG Economics reviewed a widely cited inventory of nearly 80,000 vacant or underused acres. It found the inventory counted private parking lots at churches, schools and even a Costco, plus land on steep slopes and mountaintops.

That matters because this one number sits at the center of almost every argument about housing supply and home prices in Southern Nevada.

Here is something you should know right up front. The study was paid for by the Southern Nevada chapter of NAIOP, the Commercial Real Estate Development Association. That is a trade group for commercial real estate developers. Developers have an obvious interest in arguing that buildable land is scarce, because that argument supports releasing more federally controlled land around the valley. That does not automatically make the findings wrong. It does mean you should read the report knowing who paid for it.

The other side answered fast. Andrew Kjellman, Deputy CEO of the Regional Transportation Commission of Southern Nevada, said the 78,285-acre figure in the RTC inventory was never meant to be read as land you can build on today. He also pointed out that the RTC's own study gave a low development ranking to about 68,132 of those acres, or 87 percent of the total.

So both sides have a real point. The RTC never claimed every acre was shovel ready. But the number kept getting quoted in public without that context. This post walks through what the study found, what the RTC says back, and what the whole fight means for anyone buying, selling or owning a home here.

What Happened

The Regional Transportation Commission of Southern Nevada runs a planning effort called Southern Nevada Strong. As part of that work, the RTC built an inventory of vacant and underutilized land across Clark County. The inventory used Clark County Assessor data to flag parcels that looked empty or lightly used.

That inventory has been quoted in headlines, at public meetings and in policy fights ever since. The general shorthand became simple. Clark County has around 80,000 acres sitting there, so why do we need more land?

NAIOP Southern Nevada hired RCG Economics to look at the actual parcels behind the number. RCG's finding was that a large share of the flagged land cannot realistically be built on. Some of it is private parking. The Review-Journal reported that the inventory swept in parking lots belonging to churches, schools and a Costco. Those are working parking lots for buildings that are already in use.

Other parcels sit on steep slopes or on mountaintops. Land like that can be technically vacant and still be impossible or wildly expensive to build homes on. RCG also flagged entitlement limits, ownership problems and infrastructure capacity as reasons a parcel might be counted as available but never actually turn into housing.

Kjellman's response was that the RTC agrees on the basic point. He said the 78,285-acre figure should not be treated as land currently available for development. He noted the RTC's own study already ranked most of that land as poorly suited for building.

That last part is worth doing the math on. If 68,132 of 78,285 acres landed in the low ranking, that leaves about 10,153 acres in the higher-ranked tiers. That works out to roughly 13 percent of the total. In other words, even by the RTC's own scoring, the strong candidates for development are a small slice of the headline number.

One important caution on that math. A low ranking is not the same as impossible. The RTC scored parcels on things like nearby population, access to transit and social services, and whether the site sat inside an existing redevelopment area. A parcel can score low on those measures and still get built. So treat the 13 percent as an illustration of how the ranking breaks down, not as a hard count of how many acres are truly buildable.

There is also no single agreed-upon number here, which is part of why this argument keeps going.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Start with the numbers themselves, because they do not agree. The RTC study cites 78,285 acres. The RTC's own website carries a headline about more than 82,000 acres of potential. Southern Nevada Strong has promoted the figure as 84,000 acres. RCG Economics now says all of those overstate what can actually be built.

That is a spread of nearly 6,000 acres between the low and high public figures, before anyone even argues about buildability. When people quote "80,000 acres" in a meeting, they are usually rounding one of these. It is fair to say nobody has settled on one clean number.

Now here is why any of this touches your house. Clark County is nearly surrounded by federal land. Clark County's own government pages report roughly 4.45 million of the county's 5.15 million acres are federally owned. That is about 86 percent by that math, and the county describes the federal share as approximately 90 percent depending on how you count it.

Private land in the valley is a limited pool. When a builder wants to put up 200 homes, they have to buy from that pool. If the pool is small and demand is strong, the price of a raw acre goes up. Land cost is baked into the price of every new house that gets built on it.

We just watched that happen in real time. On July 20, the Review-Journal reported that Howard Hughes Holdings sold 73 acres in Summerlin to three homebuilders for $126 million. Pulte paid $52.57 million for 30.4 acres. KB Home paid $40 million for 23.1 acres. Tri Pointe paid $33.5 million for 19.6 acres. Prices topped out near $1.75 million per acre, among the highest the developer has ever gotten for tract housing land.

Builders do not pay $1.75 million an acre when good dirt is easy to find. That sale is concrete market evidence that developable land in the desirable parts of the valley is tight. Whatever you think of who paid for the RCG study, the land market is voting with real money.

Supply on the building side is also thin. Las Vegas builders pulled 2,918 permits through April 2026, down 24 percent from the same stretch a year earlier. They pulled 9,734 in all of 2025, the lowest annual total since 2016. Fewer new homes coming online puts more pressure on the resale market, which is where most buyers here are shopping anyway.

So the acreage fight is not an abstract planning argument. It feeds directly into how much a builder pays for dirt, how many homes get built, and what you end up paying for a house in Henderson, North Las Vegas or the southwest.

Background and History

Nevada has been arguing about federal land for a very long time. The federal government controls the large majority of the state, and Clark County is one of the most federally dominated counties in the country. The valley did not sprawl outward by accident. It grew into whatever private land was available and then hit a wall.

Congress created a workaround for Southern Nevada. There is a defined area around Las Vegas, often called the disposal boundary, where the Bureau of Land Management is pre-authorized to sell public land. Land outside that boundary generally needs new action from Congress before it can be sold.

That boundary is not large. Nevada Policy, a Las Vegas think tank, reports the disposal boundary covers about 6.3 percent of Clark County. Everything else is either already private or effectively off the table without a new law.

Against that backdrop, the Southern Nevada Strong underutilized lands inventory landed with real weight. It was released in 2025 and it gave people a counterargument. Instead of releasing more federal land at the edges, the inventory pointed at land already inside the developed valley. Southern Nevada Strong highlighted the Historic Westside, downtown Las Vegas and downtown Henderson as places where infill could happen.

That is the debate in a sentence. Should Southern Nevada grow out, by opening more federal land at the edges, or grow up, by building more densely on land already inside the valley?

Both sides use the acreage number for their own case. Infill supporters use it to argue there is plenty of room inside the valley already. Development groups use studies like RCG's to argue the number is inflated and the edges have to open up. NAIOP made that theme explicit at a May 21, 2026 breakfast event titled "Land: Southern Nevada's Ultimate Economic Constraint," reported by Nevada Business Magazine.

What Happens Next

The most likely near-term outcome is a fight over methodology, not a settled answer. RCG has raised specific objections about which parcels got counted. The RTC has said the inventory was a screening tool rather than a list of build-ready sites. Those two positions can both stand, and neither side has to back down.

Watch for a follow-up or a revision to the underutilized lands inventory. If the RTC or Southern Nevada Strong publishes an updated version that strips out active parking lots and steep terrain, that would be the clearest sign the criticism landed. A revised number would immediately change how the figure gets quoted in public meetings.

Also watch the local approvals. Every infill project that actually gets built is a data point for the "grow up" side. Every one that dies at a planning commission is a data point for the other side. The Clark County Commission takes final action August 5 on Lennar's plan for 279 homes on the old Eastside Cannery site along Boulder Highway. That is exactly the kind of infill redevelopment the inventory was pointing toward.

On the federal side, efforts to open more BLM land around the valley come up regularly in Congress and in state politics. I am not going to tell you which way that should go, and I am not going to name any specific pending bill here, because the status of legislation changes fast and I would rather give you nothing than give you something stale. If that is your issue, follow it through your own representatives and through local reporting.

Finally, watch land prices. They are the least political indicator in this whole story. If per-acre prices in Summerlin, the southwest and Henderson keep climbing, the scarcity argument gets stronger no matter who commissioned which study. If prices flatten out, that tells you something too.

Ryan's Take

I try to be careful with studies that come with a sponsor attached, and this one comes with a big one. NAIOP represents commercial developers. A finding that says "there is less usable land than you think" is exactly the finding that helps their members. I would say the same thing about a tenant group publishing a study that concluded rents are too high.

That said, I think the underlying criticism is fair. Counting an active Costco parking lot as underutilized land is the kind of thing that makes a number look bigger than the reality on the ground. Anybody who has tried to find a decent buildable parcel in this valley knows the good dirt is not sitting around waiting.

I also think Kjellman's response is fair. The RTC did rank 87 percent of that land low. The problem was never really the study. The problem was the shorthand, where a screening inventory turned into "we have 80,000 acres" in every housing conversation in town.

What I keep coming back to is the Summerlin sale. Three national builders just paid up to $1.75 million an acre. Those companies have land teams, market data and every reason to pay as little as possible. They paid that anyway. That tells me more about real land scarcity in the good parts of this valley than any of the competing acreage totals do.

My honest read is that both numbers are being used as weapons in a policy fight, and the truth sits somewhere in the middle. There is real infill capacity here. There is also real scarcity. Those two things are not in conflict.

What You Can Do

If you own a home here, understand that land scarcity is one of the reasons your value has held up the way it has. Limited buildable land plus steady demand is a real support under prices in Clark County. It is not the only factor, but it is a big one, and it is not going away quickly.

If you are buying new construction, ask the sales agent when the builder acquired the land for that community. Older land buys were made at lower prices. Newer land buys at today's per-acre numbers show up in the base price and in how much the builder is willing to negotiate.

If you are watching a specific neighborhood, look at what is actually entitled and approved nearby, not just what looks empty on a map. An empty lot next door might be a church parking lot, a drainage channel or a slope nobody can build on. Clark County and city planning departments post agendas and case files online.

If you care about the policy side, show up. Planning commission and city council meetings are where infill projects live or die, and public comment genuinely gets counted. The Henderson data center vote and the August 5 Clark County vote on the Boulder Highway homes are both examples of decisions made in a room you can sit in.

And if you just want to know what all this means for your own home value or your buying timeline, ask somebody who watches this market every week rather than guessing from a headline.
