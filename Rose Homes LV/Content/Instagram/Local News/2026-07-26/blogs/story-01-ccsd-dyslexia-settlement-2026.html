CCSD Will Now Screen Every Struggling Reader for Dyslexia After a $1.5 Million Settlement

The Clark County School District just agreed to a major change that touches every school in the valley. Under a new $1.5 million settlement with the Legal Aid Center of Southern Nevada, CCSD will now screen any student who scores below reading benchmarks for signs of dyslexia, no matter their age, and it will strengthen support for students living in foster care. The deal was announced on Wednesday, July 22, 2026, and it closes out more than 20 individual complaints that said the district failed to give some struggling readers the instruction they were owed.

Here is why this matters to so many families. Reading is the skill that almost everything else in school is built on. When a child cannot read at grade level, math, science, and history all get harder, and confidence takes a hit. This settlement promises earlier help for kids who have been slipping through the cracks, and it puts extra focus on foster youth, one of the most vulnerable groups in any school system. For parents across Summerlin, Henderson, Spring Valley, and North Las Vegas, it is a signal that the district is changing how it spots and treats reading struggles. Below we break down exactly what CCSD agreed to, who benefits, and what you can do if you think your own child needs help.

What Happened

On Wednesday morning, the Legal Aid Center of Southern Nevada and CCSD announced a settlement that does two big things at once. First, it expands dyslexia and reading support for every student in the district. Second, it strengthens services for students in foster care. Barbara Buckley, the Executive Director of the Legal Aid Center, said the two sides share a goal of changing outcomes for the most vulnerable kids so they can read and build a future.

A separate pot of money, totaling $1.5 million, will be spread over four years to 23 students. These students, in grades 3 through 12, were named in individual due process complaints that Legal Aid filed on their behalf. The money will pay for educational, social and emotional, and behavioral services, including intensive academic programs. Many of these students are in foster care, and Buckley pointed out that only about half of CCSD's foster youth currently graduate from high school.

The settlement also sets new rules for how the district handles reading across every campus. Here are the literacy pieces of the agreement:

  • Any student who scores below the set benchmarks on the district's interim reading tests will get extra screening, including checks for signs of dyslexia, no matter how old they are.
  • CCSD will launch a districtwide structured literacy program through a system called 95 RAP, and it will roll the program out first at schools with the most foster care students.
  • Students with disabilities who struggle with basic reading will get reading help based on need, even if their special education category does not obviously call for it, and their progress will be tracked and shared with families.
  • The district will update its Special Education Procedures Manual and retrain IEP teams so they consistently ask whether a student might have dyslexia and connect that child with the right support.

On the foster care side, CCSD agreed to move existing staff into its Foster Care Department and Student Services Division, finish a full audit of foster students' academic plans before the 2026-2027 school year, and stop trying to waive the three-year special education re-evaluations that these students are owed. Together, these steps turn a handful of individual complaints into policy that reaches the whole district.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

For Las Vegas families, this is not just a legal story. It is about whether your kid, or your neighbor's kid, gets caught early when reading is hard. Dyslexia is common. Many experts estimate it affects up to one in five people to some degree. In a district this size, that means tens of thousands of students could benefit from better screening. The old approach often missed older kids, because some screening tools focus only on the early grades. The new rule says age does not matter. A struggling seventh grader gets checked, not just a first grader.

There is a money angle too, and it hits close to home for anyone who owns a house here. School quality is one of the biggest things buyers look at when they pick a neighborhood. Families pay a premium to live near schools with strong reading scores and strong support programs. When a district invests in literacy and closes gaps, it can lift the reputation of schools across the valley over time, from Centennial Hills to Green Valley. Strong schools help hold up home values, and weak ones can drag them down.

Think about how buyers actually shop here. A family moving to Las Vegas from out of state will spend hours comparing schools before they ever tour a single home. They look at ratings, they read parent reviews, and they ask about special programs. A district that screens every struggling reader and backs it up with a real structured literacy plan gives those buyers one more reason to feel good about planting roots. Neighborhoods like Skye Canyon, Mountains Edge, and Inspirada have grown fast because young families keep betting on them, and those families care deeply about how local schools treat kids who need extra help.

This settlement also shines a light on foster youth, a group many people never think about. Foster students move homes and schools more often than their peers. They lose records, they repeat lessons, and they fall behind. A graduation rate near 50 percent means about half of these kids leave school without a diploma. That affects the whole community. Adults who cannot read well tend to earn less, struggle to find stable housing, and are more likely to need public help. Fixing this early is cheaper and kinder than paying for it later.

For renters and parents who move a lot, the foster care pieces matter beyond foster families. The district is promising better coordination and record keeping for students who change schools. That is good news for any family that has bounced between apartments in Spring Valley or moved across town for a new job. When schools track a child's needs and share them with the next campus, kids do not have to start over each time they walk into a new classroom.

Background and History

To understand this settlement, it helps to know how we got here. For years, families and advocates argued that some students in CCSD were not getting the reading help the law requires. Legal Aid Center of Southern Nevada, a nonprofit that provides free legal help, filed a series of due process complaints on behalf of individual students. A due process complaint is a formal challenge parents can file when they believe a school district is not meeting a child's special education needs. More than 20 of these complaints piled up, many involving children in foster care.

Dyslexia sits at the center of the fight. Dyslexia is a common learning difference that makes it hard to connect letters with sounds, which slows down reading. It has nothing to do with how smart a child is. With the right instruction, often a method called structured literacy, kids with dyslexia can learn to read well. The catch is that they have to be identified first, and for years many were not, especially older students who had already fallen behind their classmates.

Nevada has taken steps on this issue before. The state has passed laws meant to push schools to spot dyslexia earlier and to give parents more say in the process. Even so, advocates said the rules were not always followed on the ground, and that foster students in particular kept falling through the gaps. Foster youth are covered by extra protections because they move so often, but those protections only work if someone is actually tracking each child's plan.

It also helps to remember just how big CCSD is. It is one of the largest school districts in the entire country, with hundreds of thousands of students spread across Las Vegas, Henderson, North Las Vegas, Boulder City, and the unincorporated county. A policy change here does not affect one campus or one town. It reaches classrooms from the far northwest valley all the way to the southeast. That scale is exactly why advocates pushed for a districtwide fix instead of settling each case one at a time and leaving the next family to start the fight over again.

This settlement is the result of both sides deciding to fix the pattern rather than fight case by case. Instead of just paying the 23 named students and moving on, CCSD agreed to change its practices for everyone. That is what makes this bigger than a normal legal settlement. It turns a small group of individual complaints into districtwide policy, which is why parents far beyond the original 23 families are paying attention.

What Happens Next

The clock is already ticking. CCSD agreed to finish its audit of foster students' academic plans before the 2026-2027 school year, which starts in August. That is a tight window, and it is one of the first things parents can watch. If your child is in foster care and has an academic plan or an IEP, you should expect the district to review it soon.

The structured literacy rollout will happen in stages. Schools with the highest number of foster care students go first, and then the program spreads across the district. Because this is a multi-year plan, families will not see every change at once. The $1.5 million for the 23 named students is spread over four years, so those supports will unfold over time as well.

It is also worth watching which schools are named in the first wave. Because the rollout starts at campuses with the most foster care students, those neighborhoods will see the new structured literacy program before others. Parents at other schools can ask their principals where their campus falls in the schedule. Keeping a simple timeline in mind helps you hold the district accountable, because a promise on paper only helps your child once it reaches the actual classroom.

Parents should also watch how the district trains its staff. The settlement calls for updated IEP team training and a revised Special Education Procedures Manual. Those changes happen behind the scenes, but they shape what happens in the room when your child is evaluated. If you have a meeting scheduled about your child's reading, it is fair to ask whether the team has been trained on the new dyslexia screening rules.

Finally, keep an eye on Project RISE. This is a separate reading program CCSD says it invested in on its own, outside the settlement. RISE stands for Reading Intervention for Student Empowerment, and it targets kids who struggle to learn to read, including those who may show signs of dyslexia. Together with the settlement, it signals that reading is becoming a bigger priority for district leaders.

What You Can Do

If you think your child may have dyslexia or is struggling to read, you do not have to wait. Start by asking your child's school for a copy of their most recent interim reading assessment. Under the new settlement, students who score below benchmark are supposed to get extra screening, so ask directly whether your child qualifies and request that screening in writing.

If your child already has an IEP or a 504 plan, ask the team whether dyslexia has been considered and point to the district's updated procedures. Parents of foster youth should ask about the academic plan audit and make sure their student's plan is current before the new school year begins. You can also reach out to the Legal Aid Center of Southern Nevada, which brought these complaints and works with families on education issues at no cost.

Stay involved beyond your own household too. CCSD Board of Trustees meetings are open to the public, and reading and special education are regular topics. Showing up, sending a comment, or simply following the district's updates keeps pressure on leaders to follow through. Strong schools are a shared project, and they help everyone, whether you have kids in the system or you just want your neighborhood to thrive.
