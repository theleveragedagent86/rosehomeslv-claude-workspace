Mark Stone's Contract Question Looms Over the Golden Knights This Season

Mark Stone, the only captain in Vegas Golden Knights history, is entering the final year of his contract, and the team now has a tricky call to make about his future. Stone is heading into the last season of the eight-year, $76 million deal he signed back in 2019, and once these 84 regular season games are finished, he will need a new contract. That one question, whether and how the Golden Knights should re-sign their captain, is shaping up to be the biggest running story of the 2026-27 season for local hockey fans.

Nothing has been signed. To be clear, there is no new deal, no extension, and no announcement. This is an open situation, and both sides look to be at the very start of a long conversation. Stone is 34 years old, he has fought through a stack of injuries, and the salary cap math is not simple. At the same time, he is one of the most important players in franchise history and the face of the only major pro sports champion that Las Vegas has ever produced. SinBin.vegas broke down the numbers on July 24, and the short version is this: keeping Stone is complicated, and letting him go would be a shock.

What the Contract Situation Actually Is

Here are the facts as they stand today. A little more than seven years ago, then Golden Knights general manager George McPhee traded for right wing Mark Stone and signed him to an eight-year contract worth $76 million. That deal carried a cap hit of $9.5 million per season, and at the time it made Stone the 12th highest paid player in the entire NHL. It is now 2026, that contract is winding down, and the final year is all that is left.

Stone has more than earned his keep. In 400 games with Vegas, he ranks seventh in franchise games played, third in points with 396, fourth in goals with 136, and second in assists with 260. He sits at plus 112, which is third all time for the team, and he has 27 game winning goals, second in club history. He wears number 61, he was named the franchise's first and only captain, and he has led the team to seven postseason appearances, two Stanley Cup Final runs, and a championship. Those are cornerstone numbers, not role player numbers.

Stone's game is also about far more than the box score. He is one of the best defensive forwards of his era, a player who kills penalties, wins puck battles along the boards, and disrupts the other team as well as anyone in the league. He tends to raise his level in the playoffs, when checking gets tighter and every shift carries more weight. That two way value is a big reason the front office has never regretted the original contract, and it is part of what makes replacing him so hard to imagine. You do not simply go out and find another captain who does all of that.

The catch is age and health. Stone will turn 35 during the coming season, and he is about to start his 15th NHL year. His body has taken a beating since he passed 30. He has spent three lengthy stints on long term injured reserve, and he has been forced to miss stretches of games in each of the past six seasons, including playoff games in each of the last two years. That injury history is the single biggest reason a new contract is hard to price.

So what is he worth now? Based on the current market, an argument can be made that Stone's value still lands north of $10 million per season. This week, future Hall of Fame winger Patrick Kane re-signed with the Chicago Blackhawks for two years and $16 million, and Kane is four years older than Stone with a bigger drop in production. Other recent deals set the bar even higher. Trevor Zegras signed for $9.125 million, Nico Hischier for $11.7 million, and Connor Bedard for $15 million. Former teammate Pavel Dorofeyev just inked a deal worth $11 million a year. With the salary cap climbing, it is hard to imagine Stone's fair number sitting below eight figures, even with the injury risk baked in.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

You might wonder why a hockey contract belongs on a Las Vegas real estate blog. The honest answer is that the Golden Knights are part of what turned this valley into a real big league city, and that identity matters to the people who live here and to the people who keep moving here. Before 2017, Las Vegas had no major pro sports team. Now it has a Stanley Cup banner, a packed arena on the Strip, and a fan base that stretches from Summerlin to Henderson to North Las Vegas. Mark Stone is stitched into that story.

The team also drives real economic activity. Home games at T-Mobile Arena pull tens of thousands of people into the entertainment corridor on the south end of the Strip, and that traffic supports restaurants, bars, hotels, and jobs across the valley. Watch parties fill sports bars in Green Valley and Centennial Hills. Local businesses run Golden Knights promotions. When the team is winning and stable, that energy ripples out into the community in a way that is easy to feel and hard to measure.

For families thinking about putting down roots here, pro sports is part of the lifestyle pitch. A buyer weighing Las Vegas against another city is not just buying a house. They are buying access to a downtown, an airport, a schools system, weather, and yes, a hockey team they can take their kids to see. A steady franchise with a recognizable captain adds to the sense that this is a serious place to build a life, not just a place to visit.

The arena district itself is a good example of how a sports anchor shapes a city. The south Strip corridor around T-Mobile Arena has become a year round destination, and the steady flow of games, concerts, and events supports the kind of dining, retail, and nightlife that buyers ask me about when they picture living close to the action. Newcomers from traditional hockey markets in the Midwest and Canada often tell me the Golden Knights were one of the first things that made Las Vegas feel like home. That emotional pull is part of the region's appeal, even if it never shows up on a single comparable sale.

There is a note of caution worth stating plainly, though. A star player's contract does not move home prices. Your property value in Skye Canyon or Inspirada is driven by mortgage rates, local inventory, jobs, and interest from buyers, not by whether the captain gets an extension. What the team does add is quality of life and civic pride, and those things quietly help keep Las Vegas on the map for people shopping for a new home. That is the real link between a hockey contract and the housing market here.

Background and History

To understand why this decision carries so much weight, it helps to rewind. The Golden Knights entered the NHL as an expansion team in 2017 and stunned everyone by reaching the Stanley Cup Final in their very first season. That instant success set the tone for a franchise that has never been shy about spending to win. Owner Bill Foley, known around town as The Creator, built a culture that expects deep playoff runs every single year.

That win now culture has always come with hard choices. To stay near the top of the standings, Vegas has repeatedly reshaped its roster, moving on from popular players and swallowing tough contracts when a deal did not pan out. Fans have watched the team make cold, cap driven decisions before, even with players they loved. It is a big reason the Stone question feels so charged. Everyone in the fan base knows the front office is willing to make the unsentimental move if the math says so, and that knowledge sits underneath every conversation about the captain's future.

Stone arrived in February 2019, when McPhee sent a package to Ottawa to land him and immediately locked him up long term. Naming him the first captain in team history was more than a formality. It made him the on ice voice of the organization and the player fans pointed to when they described what the team stood for. He then helped deliver the ultimate prize, lifting the Stanley Cup as Las Vegas claimed its first major sports championship.

That history is exactly why the coming negotiation is loaded. The obvious comparison, and it is one fans will bring up all season, is Jonathan Marchessault. Marchessault was a beloved original Golden Knight and a playoff hero, and the front office still let him walk in free agency rather than pay a long term deal into his mid 30s. Vegas has proven, more than once, that loyalty and past performance do not decide contracts. Business does. That track record is what makes some fans nervous about how the Stone talks could go.

What Happens Next

The clock is ticking, because training camp is right around the corner. If the front office and its captain have not already started talking, they likely will soon, with any extension covering the 2027-28 season and beyond. New head coach Ryan Craig would almost certainly prefer to have this settled before the regular season starts, and most fans feel the same way. An unresolved contract can hang over a locker room and turn into a season long distraction.

The money picture frames the whole thing. As of now, the Golden Knights have roughly $80 million already tied up in salaries for the 2027-28 campaign, so any Stone extension has to fit inside a cap that is rising but still crowded. That leads to the central tension. Does the captain take a discount so Vegas can keep loading up around him? Or does he push for every last dollar, since at his age this is very likely his final multi year contract? Both choices are reasonable, and both come with real trade offs.

Expect the debate to get loud either way. Around town and online, some fans want the captain locked up now out of loyalty and leadership, while others point to his age and injury history and argue the team cannot afford another contract that ends up parked on injured reserve. Both sides have a real case, and neither is going to back down quickly. That back and forth is exactly why this will be a season long story, the kind of thing people argue about at watch parties in Henderson and in the T-Mobile Arena concourse between periods.

There is also a real chance the team simply waits. Given the injury history, the front office may want to see how Stone looks in camp and early in the season before committing big dollars and term. That patient approach protects the team from another expensive deal that could end up stuck on long term injured reserve. The risk is that waiting lets the questions linger, and lingering questions tend to grow louder as the season rolls on. Keep an eye on camp updates and the first month of games for the earliest signals.

Ryan's Take

I look at this both as a lifelong hockey fan and as someone who sells the Las Vegas lifestyle for a living, and Mark Stone is a big part of that lifestyle. When I talk to buyers moving here from other markets, the Golden Knights come up all the time. Having a captain who helped win a Cup is the kind of civic asset that makes a growing city feel established. You cannot put that on a listing sheet, but it is real, and it is part of why people feel good about planting roots here.

If I had a vote, I would find a way to keep him, but I would keep it smart. A shorter term deal, maybe two or three years, with a fair cap number and some protection built in for the injury risk, feels like the responsible middle ground. Let him finish his career as a Golden Knight and eventually get his number lifted to the rafters, without saddling the team with a long contract that could go sideways. The Marchessault lesson cuts both ways. Business matters, but so does the value of a franchise face that the whole valley recognizes.

What You Can Do

If you want to follow this story as it unfolds, start with training camp. That is where the first real hints about Stone's health and the tone of any talks will show up. From there, watch the opening month of the season, since a quiet stretch with no news often says as much as an announcement. Golden Knights home games at T-Mobile Arena are the best place to take the temperature of the fan base, and this will be a hot topic in the stands all year.

There are also easy ways to stay close to the team beyond game nights. You can catch open practices at City National Arena in Summerlin, or follow the pipeline by watching the Henderson Silver Knights at Lee's Family Forum. Those events are family friendly and affordable, and they are a fun way to plug into the local hockey community whether you are a longtime resident or brand new to the valley. If you are new here, they are also a great, low key way to meet neighbors who love the same team.

It also helps to follow trusted local coverage. Outlets that live and breathe this team will track every camp update and contract whisper, so you are not leaning on national rumors that miss the local context. Bookmark a couple of reliable Golden Knights sources now, because the Stone story will very likely move in small steps rather than one big headline. Little clues, a comment here or a lineup note there, will tell the story long before any deal is announced.
