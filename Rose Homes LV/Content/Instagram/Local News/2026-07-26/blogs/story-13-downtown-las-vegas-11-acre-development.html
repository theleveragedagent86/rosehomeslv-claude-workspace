Downtown Las Vegas Puts an 11-Acre Site Near Fremont Street Up for Grabs

The City of Las Vegas is looking for a developer to build on about 11 acres in the heart of downtown, one of the largest open parcels the city has offered in a generation. City leaders put out a formal request for proposals in July 2026, and they want big ideas for the land near Fremont Street. Proposals are due by 5 p.m. on December 16, 2026.

The site sits close to some of the most visited spots downtown. It is within walking distance of the Fremont Street Experience, the Mob Museum, the Downtown Container Park, and the bars and restaurants of East Fremont. Symphony Park and the 18b Arts District are a short distance away. The city says the land is a rare chance to shape what it calls an ongoing renaissance for Downtown Las Vegas.

This is not a small corner lot. The city wants a mixed-use plan that could include hotels, entertainment, shops, offices, homes, green space, and even pro sports. A project like that could bring new jobs, new housing, and new energy to a part of town that has been climbing back for years. For anyone who lives, works, or owns property near downtown, this is a story worth watching closely, because the choices made here will shape the area for a long time.

What the City of Las Vegas Actually Announced

The City of Las Vegas issued a request for proposals, often called an RFP, for a downtown property of roughly 11 acres. An RFP is a public invitation. The city is asking private developers to send in their best plans and to pitch how they would build on the land. The city shared the news on its social media accounts on July 20, 2026, and local station FOX5 reported the details the next day.

The parcel sits in a prime downtown location. Based on the city's description, the land is bounded by Las Vegas Boulevard, Stewart Avenue, and the US 95 and I-11 freeway corridor. That puts it near the eastern edge of the Fremont Street Experience and close to the Fremont East entertainment district. Few blocks in the entire valley pack this much foot traffic, history, and freeway access into one place.

City officials laid out a long wish list for the site. They want a mixed-use project that fits the neighborhood while creating a new destination. The list includes hospitality, which means hotels and resorts. It includes entertainment and retail. It includes office space and housing. It even names professional sports, green space, and civic and public safety uses. In plain terms, the city wants a little bit of everything on this land, not just another single-use building that sits quiet at night.

The timeline is clear. Proposals must be turned in by 5 p.m. on December 16, 2026. Developers are asked to submit their plans electronically to a city contact named Tracy Reich at an official lasvegasnevada.gov email address. The city has also posted more details on its website under a listing it calls the downtown assemblage request for proposals. An assemblage is simply a group of smaller land pieces that have been combined into one larger site, which is why this parcel is so unusually big for the urban core.

There is no chosen builder yet, and no price tag has been announced. This is the very first step in a long process. The city is casting a wide net to see who is interested and what they would bring to the table. That is good news for residents, because it means the public still has time to weigh in before any single plan gets locked in. Right now, the land is close to a blank page, and the city is asking the development world to help fill it.

Why This Matters for Las Vegas Homeowners and Renters

A project this size can change the feel of a whole area, and downtown touches far more people than just tourists. Tens of thousands of residents live within a few miles of Fremont Street, in neighborhoods like the John S. Park Historic District, the Arts District, Huntridge, and the newer condo towers near Symphony Park. What rises on these 11 acres could shape their commutes, their home values, and their daily life for decades to come.

Start with housing. The city's wish list includes residential use, which means new homes could be part of the final plan. Downtown has added apartments and condos over the past decade, but demand has stayed strong. More housing near jobs and entertainment gives buyers and renters more choices in a market that often feels tight. It can also take a little pressure off the wider valley, where prices and rents remain high for working families.

Then there are jobs. A mixed-use project with hotels, shops, offices, and possibly a sports venue would need workers to build it and workers to run it. Construction jobs come first, followed by long-term roles in hospitality, retail, and services. Those paychecks tend to stay local, and they help support the other small businesses that already call downtown home.

Home values are part of the story too. When a city invests in an area and private money follows, nearby property often becomes more desirable. Owners of homes and small buildings near downtown could see stronger interest and higher values over time. That is welcome news if you own. It can be harder if you rent, because rising demand can push rents up as well. This is exactly why the details of the plan matter so much to regular people, not just to investors.

Traffic and daily life will feel the change as well. The site sits right by the US 95 and I-11 corridor, so how the project handles parking, streets, and walkability will affect how easy it is to get around. Green space is on the wish list, and a well-designed park or plaza could give families a new place to gather. Public safety uses are listed too, which could mean added services in the area. For the average Las Vegas family, the takeaway is simple. This is a large piece of the urban core that could add homes, jobs, and gathering spots close to real neighborhoods, so it pays to understand what is on the table.

How Downtown Las Vegas Got to This Point

Downtown is the oldest part of Las Vegas. The city was founded in 1905 when land near the railroad tracks was auctioned off, and Fremont Street became the first center of hotels, casinos, and city life. For many decades, though, the bright lights of the Strip pulled visitors and money south, and downtown lost some of its shine and its crowds.

The comeback started in the 1990s. In 1995, the Fremont Street Experience opened its famous canopy of lights over five blocks, giving downtown a signature attraction that still draws millions. The bigger turning point came in 2012, a year city leaders called the Year of Downtown. That year brought the Smith Center for the Performing Arts, the Mob Museum, and a new City Hall, and the Discovery Children's Museum followed close behind.

Private money arrived too. The online retailer Zappos moved its headquarters into the old City Hall, and the company's late leader, Tony Hsieh, poured about $350 million of his own wealth into a broad revitalization effort known as the Downtown Project. New bars, restaurants, shops, and the Downtown Container Park followed. The Arts District, also called 18b, grew into a hub of galleries, murals, and small businesses that now draws crowds on First Friday each month.

The city has also spent years assembling and planning land near the core. Symphony Park, built on 61 acres the city bought from the Union Pacific Railroad in 1995, added the Smith Center and a Cleveland Clinic brain health center. Piece by piece, downtown has shifted from a faded gaming district into a mixed neighborhood of homes, culture, and business. The new 11-acre RFP is the next chapter in that long, steady effort, and it may be one of the biggest single pieces the city has offered yet.

What Happens Next With the 11-Acre Site

The clock is now running toward the December 16, 2026 deadline. Between now and then, development teams will study the land, sketch out concepts, and put together detailed proposals. Big projects like this often draw national and even international bidders, so the city could receive several very different visions for the same piece of ground. Some may lean on hotels and entertainment, while others may push housing or a sports venue.

After the deadline, city staff will review the proposals. They will weigh factors like design, financing, experience, and how well each plan matches the city's goals for hotels, homes, entertainment, offices, green space, and public uses. This review takes time. City leaders will likely hold public meetings, and the City Council will have the final say on any deal. Nothing gets built overnight, and the first plans the public sees may change more than once.

It is also worth setting expectations on timing. From selection to groundbreaking to ribbon cutting, a mixed-use project of this scale can take years. Plans can change, partners can shift, and market conditions can move. Residents should watch for a few key moments, such as when the city names finalists, when a chosen developer is announced, and when any zoning or design details go before the Council for a vote. Each of those steps is a chance to learn more and to speak up.

For now, the smart move is to stay informed and keep an open mind. The land is one of the largest downtown parcels available in a generation, and the city has made clear it wants something special. What actually rises there will depend on who bids, what they promise, and how the public responds along the way.

What You Can Do as a Las Vegas Resident

You do not have to be a developer to have a voice in this. The City of Las Vegas posted the full details online under its downtown assemblage request for proposals, so you can read exactly what the city is asking for. Reviewing that document is the best way to understand the real goals for the site instead of relying on rumors or social media guesses.

Next, stay connected to City Hall. City Council and Planning Commission meetings are open to the public, and big downtown projects usually come up for discussion and votes. You can attend in person, watch online, or send comments to your ward's council member. If a park, a housing plan, or a traffic design matters to you, that is where the real decisions get made.

It also helps to follow trusted local news and to talk with neighbors. Business groups, neighborhood associations, and the Arts District community all keep a close eye on downtown growth. Getting involved with one of them is a simple way to stay in the loop and to add your voice to the conversation.

Finally, think about your own real estate plans. If you own near downtown, this is a good time to understand your home's value and how the area is trending. If you are thinking about buying, it helps to know which blocks are poised to benefit from a project like this. Knowledge is your best tool, and a little homework now can pay off later.
