CCSD Seeks $4,000 More Per Student Under a New Nevada Funding Formula

Clark County School District Superintendent Ebert says a new state funding formula is now in place, and she is working to get about $4,000 more for every child in the district. She shared that goal at a July 15 Mesquite Chamber of Commerce luncheon, where she laid out her plans for CCSD and its roughly 280,000 students.

That number matters to almost every family in Southern Nevada. The dollars that follow each student help decide class sizes, teacher pay, school safety, and the programs your kids can take. When the person running the fifth-largest school district in the country says she wants thousands more per child, parents, homeowners, and future buyers should pay attention.

Ebert framed the funding push as part of a bigger plan to lift the whole district, including the small rural communities that often feel left behind. She told the crowd that the new formula will make a real difference, especially for towns like Mesquite. She also spelled out what she cares about most, from valuing teachers to bringing back trust with the community.

This is a bread-and-butter school story, not a flashy one. But school funding shapes neighborhoods, and neighborhoods shape home values. Here is what Superintendent Ebert actually said, why it matters for Las Vegas and Henderson families, and what you can watch for as the money question moves forward.

What Happened at the Mesquite Chamber Luncheon

Superintendent Ebert spoke to Mesquite Chamber of Commerce members on July 15 at the 1880's Grille. The event was a lunch meeting, and she used it to introduce herself and share her vision for the district. This is her first year as superintendent, though she is not new to CCSD. She has spent 25 years with the district.

The headline number was money. Ebert said there are 280,000 children in CCSD, and she is trying to get $4,000 more per child for the district. She tied that push to a new state funding formula that she said is now in place and will help, especially in rural areas. Mesquite has four schools in its rural community, and she said the new formula should make a difference there.

Ebert also laid out her priorities in plain terms. "I believe in valuing the educators, ensuring school safety, bringing back trust and community relations, and number one is academic excellence," she said. That is a short list, but it covers the things parents ask about most: good teachers, safe buildings, honest leadership, and strong learning.

She pointed to a large survey her office ran to find out what families want. The survey reached 50,000 people across the Nevada district, including 30,000 students. The clear message was that students want more real-life experiences while they are still in high school. Ebert sees local businesses as a big part of the answer.

"This is where business owners can come into play with internships for students," she said. "Students can get a feel for what they are thinking is their chosen profession. Being in a real-life situation can and does sometimes make a difference in a student's thinking." In other words, she wants more internships, job shadowing, and hands-on learning that connect classrooms to real careers.

A group of Virgin Valley High School FFA students attended the luncheon. One of them, student writer and FFA member Anexie Martinez, asked a sharp question. She noted the big differences between her rural school and the many schools in Las Vegas, and she asked how the district would adjust rules and funding to fit rural students. Ebert answered that Mesquite's rural schools already receive more funding than many other central schools. She explained that the principal of each school decides how the money is spent, and she offered to work with the local principal on it. She added that students should speak up too, and that student advisory committees should be advocates for how the funds get spent.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Here is why this reaches far beyond one lunch in Mesquite. Per-pupil funding is the engine behind almost everything you see in a school. It pays for teachers, aides, counselors, books, computers, and building repairs. When the money per student goes up, class sizes can shrink and programs can grow. When it stays flat, schools stretch every dollar and families feel it.

CCSD is huge. With about 280,000 students, it is the fifth-largest school district in the United States. That means a change of $4,000 per child is not a rounding error. Across a district this size, that kind of increase would be a major shift in the classroom experience for hundreds of thousands of kids in Las Vegas, Henderson, North Las Vegas, and every corner of the valley.

For parents, the link is simple. More funding can mean more teachers, which can mean smaller classes and more attention for each child. It can mean updated technology, safer buildings, and more electives like art, music, and career training. Ebert's push for internships and real-life learning could give high schoolers a head start on jobs and college. Those are the things families talk about at the kitchen table.

For homeowners, the stakes are just as real, even if you do not have kids in school. School quality is one of the biggest drivers of home values in any market. Buyers pay close attention to schools, and strong schools help hold and lift prices in a neighborhood. When a district invests more per student and programs improve, nearby homes often benefit. When schools struggle, buyers notice that too.

Class size is where many families feel funding first. Nevada schools have long carried some of the largest class sizes in the country, and crowded rooms make it harder for teachers to reach every student. More money per pupil is one of the few levers that can bring those numbers down. For a fast-growing area like Summerlin, Skye Canyon, or the southwest valley, where new rooftops keep filling up local schools, steady funding is what helps schools keep pace with all those new students.

Renters feel it in a different way. Strong schools make a neighborhood more desirable, which can support rents and keep demand steady. Families often pick where to rent based on the local school, so the health of CCSD ripples through the whole housing market, not just the for-sale side.

There is also a fairness angle that hits home for many locals. Ebert's focus on rural communities like Mesquite is a reminder that not every school in Clark County starts from the same place. A funding formula that sends more help where it is needed can narrow the gap between a small town school and a large valley campus. That matters to families in far-flung parts of the county who sometimes feel forgotten. It also speaks to trust, and Ebert said plainly that she wants to bring back trust and community relations.

Background and History

To understand the $4,000 request, it helps to know how Nevada got here. For decades, the state paid for schools using an old system known as the Nevada Plan, which dated back to the 1960s. Critics said it was outdated and did not send enough money to the students who needed the most help.

In 2019, state lawmakers passed a major overhaul called the Pupil-Centered Funding Plan. The idea was to fund students in a more direct way, with a base amount for every child plus extra weighted dollars for certain groups, such as English learners, students from low-income homes, gifted students, and students in special education. The new formula began phasing in during the following state budget cycles. When Superintendent Ebert says a new funding formula is in place, this is the shift she is talking about.

The change matters because Nevada has long ranked near the bottom nationally for how much it spends per student. For years, teachers, parents, and administrators have argued that the state needs to close that gap. That is the backdrop for Ebert's push to get about $4,000 more per child. She is not inventing a new idea; she is joining a long-running fight over school money in a fast-growing state.

Rural communities have their own chapter in this story. Small towns like Mesquite have fewer schools and fewer students, which can make funding tricky. Ebert's point that Mesquite's rural schools already receive more funding than many central schools reflects how the formula tries to account for the higher cost of running small, remote campuses. It also shows why a single statewide formula has to be flexible enough to fit very different communities, from a downtown Las Vegas high school to a four-school town near the Arizona line.

What Happens Next

The big decisions on school money in Nevada run through the state. Lawmakers set education funding during the legislative session, which happens every two years. The next regular session is in 2027, and school funding is always one of the largest and most debated pieces of the state budget. That is the arena where a request for more per-pupil money will live or die.

Between now and then, expect plenty of study and debate. Nevada has a group that reviews the funding formula and makes recommendations on the base per-pupil amount and the weighted dollars for high-need students. Districts like CCSD, along with teachers, parents, and business groups, will push their case for more money. Ebert's $4,000 goal gives Clark County a clear number to rally around.

That review group is the state's Commission on School Funding, created when lawmakers passed the new formula. It studies the base per-pupil amount and the weighted dollars for high-need students, then recommends changes to lawmakers. Its reports often become the starting point for budget talks. Parents who want to follow the money closely can track the commission's meetings and recommendations, since they help shape what districts like CCSD can realistically ask for.

Closer to home, watch how CCSD spends the money it already has. Ebert made a point of saying that principals decide how funds are used at each school, and that students should speak up through advisory committees. That means some of the most important choices happen at your child's campus, not just in Carson City. School organizational team meetings and school board meetings are where those local calls get made.

Also keep an eye on the internship and real-life learning push. Ebert wants local businesses to offer internships and hands-on experiences for high schoolers. If that idea grows, you may see new partnerships between CCSD high schools and Las Vegas and Henderson employers. Parents of teens should ask their schools what career and work-based programs are available for the coming year. And watch the rural piece, since how the new formula plays out for those four Mesquite schools will be an early test of whether the promise of extra help for rural areas holds up.

Ryan's Take

As a Las Vegas real estate agent, I watch school news closely, because schools and home values move together more than people think. When buyers call me, one of the first questions is almost always about the schools. A district that is fighting to add thousands of dollars per student, and doing it with a plan for rural areas too, is the kind of story that can shape where families choose to plant roots.

I like that Superintendent Ebert is talking about real-life learning and internships. Career training and hands-on programs are a real draw for families with teens, and they help kids who are not headed straight to a four-year college. Neighborhoods near schools with strong career programs, good results, and safe campuses tend to hold value well, even when the broader market cools. The trust piece is smart too. Vegas has grown fast, and a lot of families feel unsure about their schools. If CCSD can show that more money actually reaches the classroom and lifts results, that confidence can ripple into the housing market, because buyers pay a premium for a school they believe in.

My honest take is that this is a story to watch, not a done deal. The $4,000 is a goal, not a guarantee, and the real fight is still ahead in the next legislative session. But the direction matters. If you are buying or selling in the next year or two, keep an eye on how your local schools are funded and how they are performing. It is one of the quiet forces behind your home's value.

What You Can Do

You do not have to wait for the next legislative session to get involved. Start local. Every CCSD school has a School Organizational Team, made up of parents, staff, and community members, that helps decide how the school spends its budget. Showing up to those meetings is one of the most direct ways to shape how funding is used at your child's campus.

You can also follow the CCSD Board of Trustees. The board meets regularly, and its agendas cover budgets, programs, and policies that touch every school in the valley. Public comment is open to residents, so if you have an opinion on funding or programs, this is a place to be heard. Meeting dates and materials are posted on the district website at ccsd.net.

If you want to help with the internship push, business owners can reach out to local high schools about hosting students. Ebert made a direct ask for exactly this kind of partnership. Even a few internship or job-shadow spots can make a difference for a teenager trying to picture a future career. For the state-level fight, watch the Nevada Legislature as the 2027 session approaches, and let your state lawmakers know how you feel about per-pupil funding. And if you are weighing a move, do your homework on the schools that serve any neighborhood you are considering, because school quality is one of the biggest factors in both your family's daily life and your home's long-term value. A good starting point is the state's public school performance reports and the district's own school profiles, which let you compare nearby campuses on results, class sizes, and programs before you commit to an address.
