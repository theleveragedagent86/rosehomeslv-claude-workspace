## Story 1: CCSD Just Paid $1.5 Million Because It Failed Struggling Readers

CCSD Just Paid $1.5 Million Because It Failed Struggling Readers

For years, some Clark County families begged the schools to help teach their kids to read. This week, the district agreed to pay for dropping the ball.

CCSD and the Legal Aid Center of Southern Nevada settled more than 20 due process complaints. The complaints said the district failed to give required reading instruction to students with dyslexia.

Foster kids rarely have someone in their corner at a school meeting. That is exactly why this settlement matters.

The new screening rules could help thousands of readers who were quietly falling behind. That sounds great on paper. The real test is whether the district actually follows through.

## Story 3: NV Energy Is Changing How You Pay for Power Starting in 2027

NV Energy Is Changing How You Pay for Power Starting in 2027

A new charge is coming to your power bill, and it has nothing to do with how much energy you use all month. It is about your worst 15 minutes.

NV Energy just detailed a new daily demand charge that starts January 1, 2027.

Here is how it works. The charge is based on your highest 15-minute burst of power use each day, billed at about 14 cents per kWh.

The utility estimates it will add about $15 to $20 a month for a typical household.

To balance it out, NV Energy says it is lowering the basic service charge and the per kWh volumetric rate.

The company also flagged about $63 million in refunds for past overcharges to multifamily customers.

This is a real shift in how power gets billed in Southern Nevada.

Most of us think about our power bill as one big monthly number. This new charge changes that.

Now the moment you run the AC, the dryer, the oven, and the pool pump all at once could cost you more.

Families who spread out their heavy power use might save money. Families who do everything at 6 p.m. might pay more.

The company says the lower base rates offset the new charge for the average home. That sounds fine, but averages hide winners and losers.

The $63 million in refunds is a reminder that billing mistakes do happen, and they add up over time.

Bottom line, it pays to understand your own habits before this kicks in.

Experts point to record homeowner equity as a firewall. When people owe far less than their home is worth, they rarely lose it to foreclosure.

If money gets tight, most owners today can sell and walk away with cash instead of facing foreclosure.

That is the opposite of what happened in 2008, when millions owed more than their homes were worth.

So when you see a scary foreclosure headline about Nevada, remember the real rate. National starts of 26,217 and a local rate near 1 in 2,500 tell very different stories.
