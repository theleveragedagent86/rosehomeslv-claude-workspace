CCSD Police Plan a School-Zone Crackdown on E-Bikes and E-Scooters Ahead of the New Year

Clark County School District police are launching a school-zone crackdown on e-bikes and e-scooters after a huge jump in kids getting hit by vehicles. Officials say 432 students were struck by cars last school year, up from 120 the year before, and about half of those cases involved an e-bike or an e-scooter.

That is not a small increase. That is nearly four times as many kids hurt in a single year. With classes starting soon, CCSD police say they are done waiting and are stepping up patrols around campuses across the valley.

What Happened

On July 28, 2026, CCSD leaders laid out their plan for the new school year. Along with a push to fix broken air conditioning and updates to the K-9 program, the district said school-zone safety is a top priority. The reason is simple. The number of students hit by vehicles exploded last year.

The district reported that 432 students were struck by cars during the last school year. The year before that, the number was 120. That is a jump of more than 250 percent. Officials say roughly half of the new cases involved a student on an e-bike or an e-scooter, either the student riding one or a rider losing control near traffic.

To respond, CCSD police plan to increase their presence in school zones when the new year begins. That means more officers near crosswalks, more attention on drop-off and pick-up times, and a closer look at how kids are getting to and from campus. The focus is on the busy windows in the morning and afternoon when foot traffic, bikes, scooters, and cars all mix together on the same streets.

The crackdown is not just about writing tickets. It is about slowing everyone down. E-bikes and e-scooters can move much faster than a regular bicycle. Some models hit 20 miles per hour or more. When a device moving that fast meets a car backing out of a school lot or a driver rushing to work, the results can be serious.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

If you have a kid in a Clark County school, this hits home right away. E-bikes and e-scooters have taken over as the way a lot of middle schoolers and high schoolers get around. They are cheaper than a car, they beat waiting for a bus, and they give teens freedom. But that freedom now comes with real risk, and parents are caught in the middle.

This is also a driver issue. Every adult who commutes through a neighborhood with a school knows how chaotic those streets get at 7 a.m. and 3 p.m. A crackdown means more enforcement, which likely means more stops and more tickets, both for drivers and for young riders. If you drive through a school zone in Summerlin, Green Valley, Centennial Hills, or anywhere else in the valley, expect more eyes on the road this fall.

There is a bigger picture too. Families often pick a home based on the schools nearby and how safe the walk or ride to campus feels. When a neighborhood earns a reputation for dangerous school-zone traffic, that reputation can follow it. Safe streets and calm school routes are part of what makes an area feel livable, and buyers notice.

For renters and homeowners alike, this story is really about one thing. Kids need to get to school in one piece. A jump from 120 to 432 injuries is the kind of number that makes every parent stop and think about how their own child is getting to class.

Background and History

E-bikes and e-scooters were not a big part of the school commute a few years ago. They took off fast. Prices dropped, models got faster, and suddenly a lot of teens had a way to zip across town without a license or a car. Cities all over the country have struggled to keep up with the rules, and Clark County is no different.

Part of the problem is that many of these devices sit in a gray area. Some e-bikes are treated like bicycles under the law. Others are powerful enough to act more like small motorcycles. Riders often do not know which rules apply to them, and neither do the drivers sharing the road. That confusion adds to the danger.

CCSD has dealt with school-zone safety before, but usually through crossing guards, painted crosswalks, and reduced speed limits. What changed is the sheer volume of injuries. When the count nearly quadrupled in one year, the old tools were clearly not enough. That is why the district is leaning on its own police force to step in directly this time.

This crackdown lands during a busy stretch for CCSD. The district is also hiring about 400 new teachers, watching enrollment shift, and working to fix aging buildings. Safety around campuses is one more heavy item on a long list as the district prepares to welcome students back.

What Happens Next

Expect the extra enforcement to begin as soon as students return to class. CCSD police will focus on school zones during the morning and afternoon rush, when the risk is highest. Parents should plan for slower drop-off lines and more officers directing traffic in the first weeks of the year.

Watch for how the district measures results. The real test is whether the injury count drops from that alarming 432 figure. If the numbers fall, the crackdown works and likely continues. If they stay high, expect the conversation to shift toward tougher rules on where and how fast these devices can be ridden near schools.

Parents may also see new guidance from individual schools about e-bike and e-scooter use. Some campuses could add parking areas, set speed expectations, or send reminders home about safe riding. It is worth keeping an eye on messages from your child's school in the first month.

Ryan's Take

As someone who works with families moving all over the valley, I can tell you that school safety is one of the first things parents ask about. It is not just test scores. It is the walk to class, the crosswalk on the corner, and whether the streets feel calm during pickup. This crackdown is a sign that CCSD is taking a real problem seriously, and that matters to buyers who care about where their kids grow up.

My honest read is that this is a shared responsibility. Drivers need to slow down and stay alert in school zones. Parents need to talk with their kids about riding safely and knowing the rules for their device. And the district needs to keep the pressure on until that injury number comes back down. Safe neighborhoods are good for kids and good for home values, and those two things go hand in hand.

What You Can Do

Start with a simple talk at home. If your child rides an e-bike or an e-scooter to school, go over the basics together. Wear a helmet, slow way down near crosswalks, stay off the phone, and never assume a driver sees you. Know the top speed of the device your child rides and whether it counts as a bike or something faster under local rules.

If you drive, treat every school zone like kids could dart out at any second, because they can. Put the phone down, obey the posted speed, and give bikes and scooters plenty of room. A few extra seconds of patience can prevent a life-changing accident.

You can also get involved beyond your own household. Reach out to your child's school about crossing guards and safe routes. Show up to community meetings when school-zone safety is on the agenda. And if a crosswalk or intersection near a campus feels dangerous, report it so the district and the county can look at fixes. Safer streets happen when neighbors speak up.
