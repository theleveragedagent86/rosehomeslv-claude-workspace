Clark County Petitions the BLM for 20 Acres to Build Affordable Housing

Clark County is asking the federal government for about 20 acres of public land at a steep discount so it can build affordable homes for seniors and families. The move uses a rarely tapped program called SNPLMA 7(b), and it comes as Southern Nevada faces a shortage of roughly 80,000 to 90,000 housing units.

If you rent in the valley, own a home here, or have kids trying to move out on their own, this matters to you. Cheap land is the single biggest lever local government has to lower the cost of new housing, and this petition is a test of whether that lever still works in a region where the feds control most of the ground.

What Happened

Clark County is moving to acquire about 20 acres of federal land through a program known as SNPLMA 7(b). SNPLMA stands for the Southern Nevada Public Land Management Act, a 1998 law that lets the Bureau of Land Management sell public land around Las Vegas for development. The 7(b) piece of that law is the part that allows the county to buy land at a discount, rather than paying full market price, when the land will be used for a public purpose like affordable housing.

The plan calls for the land to be used for affordable senior housing and affordable family housing. That is a key detail. This is not luxury product or market-rate rentals. It is aimed at the residents who feel the squeeze the most, including older adults on fixed incomes and working families who keep getting priced out.

The reason this made news is simple. Southern Nevada is short an estimated 80,000 to 90,000 housing units, and the gap keeps getting wider. FOX5 Las Vegas reported on the petition and pointed out that this federal land tool, while powerful, is rarely used. In a region where the federal government owns roughly 85% of the land, a program that turns cheap public ground into homes should be a go-to option. Instead it sits mostly on the shelf.

Getting the land is only step one. After the county secures the acreage, it still has to line up a developer, funding, and approvals to actually build. That is why the acquisition matters so much. Land is the foundation, and if the county can get it cheap, everything built on top of it can be more affordable too.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Here is the plain math. When land is expensive, everything built on it has to charge more just to break even. When land is cheap, a developer can build the same apartment and still keep rent lower. That is the whole point of the 7(b) discount. It attacks the cost problem at the very bottom of the stack, before a single wall goes up.

For renters, a shortage of 80,000 to 90,000 units is why rents keep climbing even when the national headlines say the market is cooling. There simply are not enough places to live for the number of people who want to live here. Every new affordable unit takes a little pressure off the whole system. It gives someone a place to go, which frees up the unit they were fighting over.

Seniors feel this in a specific way. Older adults on Social Security or a fixed pension cannot absorb a rent hike the way a dual-income household might. Affordable senior housing keeps longtime Las Vegas and Henderson residents in the community they built, instead of forcing them to move away from family and doctors just to afford a roof.

Working families feel it too. Teachers, nurses, hospitality workers, and first responders are the backbone of this valley, and many of them earn too much to qualify for the deepest aid but not enough to comfortably rent or buy at today's prices. Dedicated affordable family housing is built for exactly that gap.

Background and History

To understand this petition, you have to understand how unusual Nevada's land situation is. The federal government owns roughly 85% of the state. Around Las Vegas, the Bureau of Land Management controls huge stretches of desert on every edge of the valley. Private developers cannot just buy that ground. The land has to be released through federal channels first.

That is why Congress passed SNPLMA back in 1998. The law drew a boundary around the Las Vegas Valley and allowed the BLM to sell public land inside it for development. Money from those sales gets split up. A share goes to Nevada's education fund, a share goes to the Southern Nevada Water Authority, and the largest share funds conservation and public projects across the state. It is the engine that has fed a lot of the valley's growth for more than two decades.

The 7(b) provision is a quieter corner of that same law. Instead of selling to the highest bidder, it lets local governments and certain groups get land at a discount for public uses. On paper, it is a perfect fit for the affordable housing crisis. In practice, it has been used far less than you would expect, which is exactly the point FOX5 raised in its reporting. The tool exists. The land exists. The need is enormous. Yet the gap keeps growing.

Part of the reason is that securing the land is slow and complicated, and building affordable housing has its own maze of financing and approvals. None of it happens overnight. That history is why housing advocates see this 20-acre petition as more than one project. They see it as a test of whether Clark County will lean on this tool more often going forward.

What Happens Next

The next milestone is the land acquisition itself. The county has to work through the federal process to secure the 20 acres under the 7(b) discount. That step involves the BLM, and public land transfers of this kind include review periods and paperwork that take time. This is not a decision that closes in a week.

After the land is secured, watch for the county to name a development partner and a funding plan. Affordable housing usually stacks several sources of money, including low-income housing tax credits and local funds. The specific mix, the number of units, and the exact location within the parcel are the details that will tell you how big an impact this project can really have.

The bigger thing to watch is whether this becomes a pattern. One 20-acre project will not close an 80,000-unit gap. But if the county proves the 7(b) process can work and repeats it, the cumulative effect over several years could be real. Keep an eye on Clark County Commission meetings and agendas for future land requests tied to housing.

Ryan's Take

I have watched this valley grow my whole career, and I can tell you land is the whole ballgame here. We are boxed in by federal land on every side, and that scarcity is a huge reason prices stay stubborn even when supply ticks up. Any tool that puts cheap, buildable ground into the housing pipeline deserves attention, and 7(b) is one of the few that does exactly that.

My honest read is that this is a good move that needs to become a habit, not a headline. Twenty acres is meaningful, but it is a drop against a shortage this size. The real win would be Clark County using this program again and again, project after project. If you are renting and waiting for relief, do not expect this one petition to change your rent next month. But it is the right kind of step, and I would rather see the county reaching for this tool than letting it collect dust.

What You Can Do

If this issue matters to you, the most useful thing you can do is pay attention to your local government. Clark County Commission meetings are open to the public, and agendas are posted ahead of time on the county website. Land requests, housing projects, and funding decisions all move through that body. Showing up or submitting public comment is how residents shape which projects get greenlit.

If you or a family member could benefit from affordable senior or family housing, start learning the landscape now. The Southern Nevada Regional Housing Authority and local nonprofits keep waitlists and eligibility information. These programs often have limited slots, so knowing the process early puts you ahead when new units come online.

And if you are simply trying to figure out your own next move in this market, whether that is renting smarter, buying, or timing a sale, that is exactly what I do every day. The valley's housing story is complicated, and you do not have to sort it out alone.
