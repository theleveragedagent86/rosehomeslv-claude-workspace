CCSD Board of Trustees Candidates Lay Out Their Visions Ahead of November

The people who want to help run the fifth-largest school district in the country just told voters what they plan to do. CCSD Board of Trustees candidates laid out their visions ahead of the November 3 general election, and several seats are contested. That means Clark County families get a real choice about who steers the schools their kids attend.

This matters to every household in the valley, not just parents. School board decisions touch home values, property taxes, safety near campuses, and how a neighborhood feels to a buyer. When the board changes direction, the ripple reaches your street. Here is what the candidates are saying and why you should care.

What Happened

Las Vegas Weekly reported on July 30, 2026 that candidates for the Clark County School District Board of Trustees shared their platforms ahead of the fall election. The general election is set for November 3. Three seats on the board are contested, which sets up the kind of race that draws passionate parents, teachers, and voters to the polls.

The Board of Trustees is the elected body that oversees CCSD. The district serves roughly 300,000 students across more than 350 schools. The board sets policy, approves the budget, and hires and evaluates the superintendent. In plain terms, these seats carry real power over how billions of dollars get spent and how classrooms run.

Candidates across the contested races spoke to familiar themes. Student safety came up again and again, especially with the district heading into a school year that started August 10. Academic results, teacher pay and retention, school funding, and transparency with families were all on the table. Several candidates stressed the need to rebuild trust between the district and the community after years of budget fights and leadership turnover.

The candidates also differed on approach. Some leaned on their backgrounds as educators and argued that classroom experience should guide board decisions. Others pointed to business or community organizing experience and said the district needs stronger financial discipline and better communication. That contrast gives voters a genuine decision, not a rubber stamp. Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Schools and home values move together. When buyers shop for a home in Summerlin, Green Valley, Centennial Hills, or Southern Highlands, one of the first things they check is the assigned school. A strong, stable school zone can add real dollars to a listing. A district in chaos can scare buyers off. The board you elect helps decide which way that goes.

This is true even for people who do not have kids in school. If you own a home, the perception of local schools affects your resale value whether you ever set foot on a campus. Renters feel it too, because landlords price units partly on the desirability of the neighborhood, and schools are a big part of that. A board that steadies the district protects the value of the biggest asset most families own.

Safety is another direct link. CCSD police already announced a school-zone crackdown after a sharp rise in students getting hit by vehicles, many involving e-bikes and e-scooters. The board sets the policies and the budget behind police staffing, crossing guards, and campus security. Parents in neighborhoods like Mountains Edge and Skye Canyon, where new schools sit near busy roads, have a real stake in who wins these seats.

Then there is money. The board approves a budget in the billions, and that budget is funded in large part by local property taxes. When the district faces a shortfall, the pressure lands on programs, staffing, and sometimes talk of new revenue. Voters who care about how their tax dollars get spent should treat this race as seriously as any other on the ballot.

Background and History

CCSD has been through a rocky stretch. The district has wrestled with budget shortfalls, leadership changes, and public fights over how schools should be governed. A few years back, the state moved to give more control to individual school communities, and the tug of war between central district control and local campus control has shaped many board debates since.

Enrollment has also been shifting. Superintendent Jesus Ebert recently noted that roughly 17,000 kindergartners are enrolling while about 23,000 seniors are graduating. That gap points to a slow enrollment decline, which changes everything from staffing to school funding, since dollars often follow students. The board has to plan for a district that is not simply growing forever.

At the same time, the district hired about 400 new teachers heading into the new year, a sign it is still fighting hard to keep classrooms staffed. Teacher pay and retention have been flashpoints in past board elections, and they remain central this time. Candidates know that voters remember the years of teacher shortages and substitute gaps.

Elections for these seats tend to be lower profile than races for governor or Congress, but they often turn on turnout from motivated parents and educators. A few thousand votes can decide a trustee seat. That is exactly why candidates spend the summer laying out their visions, hoping to build name recognition before ballots go out.

What Happens Next

The calendar now runs toward November 3. Between now and then, expect candidate forums, mailers, and endorsements from teacher groups, parent organizations, and local leaders. Watch for debates where candidates get pressed on specifics like safety funding, teacher pay, and how they would handle the next budget shortfall.

Voters should confirm their registration and check which trustee district they live in, since only the races on your ballot will matter for your vote. Mail ballots and early voting typically open in the weeks before Election Day in Nevada, so there will be more than one way to cast a vote. Keep an eye on the Clark County election office for exact dates and locations. [NOT VERIFIED: exact early voting and mail ballot dates for November 2026]

After the election, the winners take their seats and immediately face big decisions. The next budget cycle, ongoing safety plans, and the district's long-term response to shrinking enrollment will all land on the new board fast. Whoever wins will help set the tone for CCSD for years, not just months.

Ryan's Take

As a local real estate expert, I watch school board races closely because they quietly shape the market. Buyers ask me about schools constantly. A calm, well-run district makes my job easier and makes homes in strong zones move faster. A district full of headlines and instability makes buyers nervous, and nervous buyers negotiate harder or walk away. This race feeds directly into that.

My advice is simple. Do not skip the school board section of your ballot just because it sits below the flashier races. These seats affect your neighborhood, your property value, and your family in ways that are easy to overlook. Read what each candidate actually plans to do, then vote for the person you trust to steady the ship. Your home and your kids both benefit when this board gets it right.

What You Can Do

Start by getting informed. Read the candidate coverage in local outlets, look up each candidate's website or public statements, and note where they stand on safety, teacher pay, and the budget. If a candidate forum or debate is scheduled in your area, go or watch the recording. Hearing candidates answer tough questions tells you more than any mailer.

Next, make sure you can vote. Confirm your voter registration, check your trustee district, and pick how you want to vote, whether that is by mail, early in person, or on Election Day November 3. If you have neighbors who care about schools, talk to them, because these low-turnout races are decided by the people who actually show up.

Finally, stay engaged after the votes are counted. Board meetings are open to the public, and public comment is your chance to weigh in on safety, budgets, and school decisions in your own neighborhood. The people who pay attention year round have the most say in how CCSD runs.
