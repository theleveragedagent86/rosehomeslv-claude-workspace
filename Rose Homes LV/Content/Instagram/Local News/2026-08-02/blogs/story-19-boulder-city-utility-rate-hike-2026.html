Boulder City Proposes a Utility Rate Hike of Up to 5% Per Year

Boulder City wants to raise utility rates by up to 5% each year for the next three years. That means your electric, water, sewer, and trash bills could climb steadily through 2029, and the city says the money is needed to cover rising costs.

The proposed increase would bring in about $1.5 million more each year for the city. For a town that prides itself on owning its own power and water systems, this is a big conversation. And it lands right when families across Clark County are already watching every dollar.

What Happened

Boulder City leaders put forward a plan to raise utility rates by as much as 5% in each of the next three years. The increase would apply across the city-owned utilities, which include electricity, water, wastewater, and sanitation. City officials say the goal is to keep those systems running and to pay for repairs and upgrades that keep getting more expensive.

The math is straightforward. The city estimates the higher rates would generate roughly $1.5 million in additional revenue every year. That money would go toward the day-to-day cost of delivering power and water, plus the long list of infrastructure needs that come with aging pipes, pumps, and lines. Boulder City runs its own electric utility, which is rare in Nevada, so the city carries these costs directly instead of passing them to a big private company.

Boulder City is unusual in another way. It is one of the few Nevada communities that gets access to low-cost hydropower from Hoover Dam, which sits just a few miles away. For decades that cheap power helped keep local bills lower than what many Las Vegas Valley residents pay. But officials say costs for equipment, labor, and materials have all gone up, and the current rates are not keeping pace.

The proposal is not final. It has to move through public meetings and a formal vote by the City Council before anything changes on your bill. That means residents will have chances to speak up, ask questions, and push back before the numbers are locked in. City staff have framed the 5% figure as a ceiling, meaning the actual increase in any given year could land lower.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Any bill increase hits your wallet, and this one touches four services at once. If you live in Boulder City, a 5% bump on electricity, water, sewer, and trash can add up fast, especially in summer when air conditioning runs almost nonstop. A household paying $250 a month for combined utilities could see that rise by roughly $12 to $13 in the first year, then more as the increases stack in years two and three.

That may not sound huge on paper, but it compounds. A 5% increase three years in a row is not a one-time 15% jump. It builds on itself, so by the third year your base bill is already higher before the next increase applies. For seniors on fixed incomes and young families stretching every paycheck, those small climbs add real pressure to the monthly budget.

There is also a housing angle. Utility costs are part of the true cost of owning a home. When buyers look at Boulder City, they weigh the mortgage, the taxes, the insurance, and yes, the monthly bills. Predictable, reasonable utility rates are part of what makes a town attractive. Steady increases, even small ones, become part of the conversation for anyone deciding where to plant roots.

Renters feel it too. Landlords who cover utilities often pass rising costs along at lease renewal. Tenants who pay their own utilities see it directly. Either way, the increase ripples through the local rental market and adds to the broader affordability squeeze that Clark County families keep talking about.

Background and History

Boulder City has a special relationship with power and water that goes back to the 1930s. The town was built to house the workers who constructed Hoover Dam. Because of that history, Boulder City has long received an allocation of low-cost federal hydropower from the dam. That advantage helped the city keep its own municipal electric utility instead of buying power from a large outside provider.

Owning your utilities has upsides and downsides. The upside is local control. The city decides how to run the systems and where to spend. The downside is that the city also owns every repair, every upgrade, and every rising cost. When transformers, pipes, and pumps wear out, the bill lands on the city, and eventually on ratepayers.

Boulder City is also known for its slow-growth philosophy. Voters passed a controlled growth ordinance years ago that limits how many new homes can be built each year. That keeps the town small and charming, but it also means the cost of running the utilities is spread across a smaller, slower-growing base of customers. Fewer new hookups means less new revenue to offset rising expenses.

This rate proposal also arrives during a busy stretch for Boulder City government. The city has been weighing an appeal of a federal approval for a nearby AI data center, a fight tied to about $2.3 million in annual revenue and questions about water and power. Utilities, growth, and local control are all part of the same larger story about how this small city pays its bills and protects its character.

What Happens Next

The proposal now heads into the public process. Boulder City will hold council meetings where staff explain the numbers and residents can comment. Nothing changes on your bill until the City Council votes to approve the plan. Watch the city's meeting agendas and the Boulder City website for the exact dates and details.

Because the 5% figure is described as a maximum, the actual increase each year could come in lower depending on the city's finances and public feedback. Residents who show up and speak during public comment can shape that outcome. Local rate decisions are often where a single well-attended meeting makes a real difference, since the audience is small and the council is listening.

Keep an eye on how the increases are structured too. A phased plan spread over three years is easier to absorb than a single large jump, but it also means the conversation will come back around each year. If costs keep rising, do not be surprised if the city returns with more requests down the road. Staying informed now makes it easier to follow what comes next.

Ryan's Take

As someone who helps people buy and sell homes across Clark County, I always tell clients to look past the mortgage payment and add up the real monthly cost of a home. Utilities are a big piece of that, and Boulder City has long been a place where those costs stayed reasonable thanks to Hoover Dam power. A slow, phased increase is not the end of the world, but it is worth paying attention to, because it signals where the town is heading on costs.

Here is the honest read. Boulder City remains one of the most unique and desirable small towns in the region, and a modest utility increase does not change that. What it does change is the math for budget-conscious buyers and retirees who chose the town partly for its affordability. If you own here or you are thinking about buying, factor these increases into your plan, and go to a meeting so your voice is part of the decision. Small towns run best when residents stay involved.

What You Can Do

Start by reading the actual proposal. The Boulder City website posts council agendas, staff reports, and meeting schedules. Look for the utility rate item and read how the increases would apply to your specific services. Knowing the real numbers puts you in a strong position to ask good questions.

Then show up. Public comment is your best tool at the local level. Attend a City Council meeting, sign up to speak, or send written comments to the council before the vote. If you cannot attend in person, ask whether comments can be submitted by email or phone. City clerks can point you to the right process.

Finally, look at your own usage. Small changes at home, like adjusting the thermostat, sealing leaks, and running big appliances during off-peak hours, can soften the impact of higher rates. If money is tight, ask the city whether payment plans or assistance programs are available for utility customers. A little planning now keeps a rate increase from catching you off guard later.
