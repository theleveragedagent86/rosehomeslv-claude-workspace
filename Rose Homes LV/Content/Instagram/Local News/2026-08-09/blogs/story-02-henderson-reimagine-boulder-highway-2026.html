Henderson Breaks Ground on the $172M Reimagine Boulder Highway Project

Henderson has started building a $172 million makeover of Boulder Highway, and the plan shrinks a 7.5 mile stretch from six lanes down to four. City leaders broke ground on August 5, 2026, and the work is expected to run for about three years. If you drive this road, your commute is about to change in a big way.

The project is called Reimagine Boulder Highway. It stretches from Wagonwheel Drive to Tulip Falls Drive. The goal is to make one of the valley's most dangerous roads safer for drivers, walkers, cyclists, and bus riders. That is the promise. The trade-off is years of construction and fewer lanes for cars.

What Happened

On the morning of August 5, 2026, Henderson leaders gathered at Boulder Highway and Greenway Road for a groundbreaking ceremony. Henderson Mayor Michelle Romero and city council members were there. They were joined by Nevada Congresswoman Dina Titus and Regional Transportation Commission of Southern Nevada CEO M.J. Maynard. Together they marked the official start of the $172 million project.

The plan covers a 7.5 mile section of Boulder Highway. It runs from Wagonwheel Drive on the north end to Tulip Falls Drive on the south end. Right now much of that stretch carries six lanes of traffic. When the work is done, it will carry four. The freed-up space will go to transit, safer crossings, and bike lanes.

The changes go well beyond paint on the pavement. Plans call for center transit lanes so buses can move faster. Crews will add new traffic signals, better street lighting, and upgraded storm drains to handle flooding. There will also be elevated, buffered bike lanes that separate riders from cars. The idea is to turn a fast, car-first road into a street that works for everyone.

The RTC of Southern Nevada and the City of Henderson are leading the effort together. Construction is expected to last about three years, with the finish line set for August 2027. That means drivers should plan for lane closures, detours, and slower travel along the corridor for a long stretch of time. The city has asked residents to be patient and to watch for updates as different segments come online.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Boulder Highway is not a side street. It is a major artery that thousands of people use every day to get to work, school, and stores. Cutting it from six lanes to four is going to be felt. During construction, and even after, some drivers will see slower trips and heavier backups at busy times. If you live in Henderson or commute through it, this touches your daily routine.

There is a safety reason behind the change, though, and it is a serious one. Boulder Highway has long been one of the deadliest roads in the whole Las Vegas valley for people on foot. Wide lanes and fast traffic have made it hard and risky to cross. The new design adds safer crossings, better lighting, and slower speeds by narrowing the road. For families who live nearby, that could mean fewer tragedies.

Renters and homeowners along the corridor have a stake in this too. Big public projects like this often lift the value of nearby property over time. A road that is safer, greener, and easier to walk tends to attract more shops, restaurants, and new housing. What looks like a headache today can become a selling point in a few years. That is worth keeping in mind if you own near Boulder Highway.

Bus riders may be the biggest winners. The center transit lanes are built so buses do not get stuck in the same traffic as everyone else. For workers who count on the bus to reach jobs on the Strip or across Henderson, faster and more reliable service is a real gain. In a region where car costs keep climbing, better transit helps a lot of households stretch their budgets.

Background and History

Boulder Highway is one of the oldest roads in Southern Nevada. It was once the main route between Las Vegas and the Hoover Dam area, and it grew up in a time when cars were king and pedestrians were an afterthought. Over the decades it became lined with motels, shops, and homes, but the road itself stayed built for speed. That mix of fast traffic and people on foot turned deadly.

For years, safety advocates and local leaders pushed for a fix. Crash and fatality data kept pointing at this corridor. The Reimagine Boulder Highway plan grew out of that pressure. Planners studied the road, held public meetings, and drew up a design that borrows from "complete streets" ideas used in other cities. The point was to balance cars with buses, bikes, and walkers instead of favoring cars alone.

Funding a $172 million project takes many partners. The RTC of Southern Nevada, the City of Henderson, and federal support all played a role, which is why a member of Congress stood at the groundbreaking. Projects this size do not happen fast. Years of planning, design, and budgeting came before a single shovel hit the dirt on August 5. The groundbreaking was the payoff for a long buildup.

This is also part of a bigger trend across the valley. Local governments have been rethinking old, wide roads and adding transit and bike space. Henderson has leaned into growth and quality-of-life upgrades as it has become one of the fastest-growing cities in Nevada. Reimagine Boulder Highway fits that story. It is the city betting that a safer, more modern corridor pays off for the long haul.

What Happens Next

The work will roll out in phases over roughly three years, with a target finish of August 2027. Crews will not tear up the whole 7.5 miles at once. Instead, they will move through segments, closing lanes in sections and shifting traffic as they go. Expect the busiest disruption near intersections where new signals, drains, and crossings are going in.

Drivers should get ready for a long stretch of detours and slower travel. It is smart to build extra time into your commute if you use Boulder Highway, and to have a backup route in mind for the worst days. The city and the RTC plan to post updates as each phase begins. Signs, alerts, and local news will flag the biggest closures before they happen.

As segments finish, you will start to see the payoff piece by piece. New bus lanes, fresh lighting, and buffered bike paths will open along completed sections even while other parts are still torn up. Over the next year, one-year progress reports on similar projects have already shown new traffic signals and bus infrastructure taking shape. By 2027, the full corridor should look and feel very different from the road drivers know today.

Sign up for local traffic notices if you can. Knowing which segment is closing next lets you plan around it instead of getting stuck by surprise.
