West Henderson Rezoning: 276 New Units Near St. Rose Parkway Head to the Planning Commission

The Henderson Planning Commission meets on August 13, 2026, to weigh a rezoning that could clear the way for 276 new homes on about 103 acres near St. Rose Parkway in West Henderson. If it moves forward, one of the fastest growing parts of the valley gets a big new dose of housing, and the neighbors who live nearby get a lot to talk about.

The request comes from a builder called Woodfield, which is asking the city to approve RH-36 zoning. That is a high-density label that allows many more units per acre than the low-slung single-family streets most people picture when they think of Henderson. This one decision touches traffic, schools, home values, and how the west side of the city grows for years to come.

What Happened

Here are the basics. The City of Henderson posted an official public notice through the Las Vegas Review-Journal legal notices about a Planning Commission hearing set for August 13, 2026. The item on the table is a rezoning request tied to a residential project of 276 units. The land sits on roughly 103 acres near St. Rose Parkway, on the west side of Henderson.

The applicant is Woodfield, a homebuilder seeking RH-36 zoning for the site. In Henderson's zoning code, the "RH" stands for high-density residential, and the number points to how many units per acre the category allows. RH-36 is at the dense end of the residential scale. That matters because the label sets the ground rules for what can be built, how tall, and how packed together.

The Planning Commission is not the final word, but it is a big step. Commissioners will review the plan, take public comment, and then vote to recommend approval, recommend denial, or send it back for changes. A yes vote pushes the project along toward the Henderson City Council, which usually has the final say on zoning changes like this one.

Legal notices like this one are easy to miss, which is part of why they matter. Cities are required to publish them so the public gets fair warning before a decision. The notice for this rezoning ran through the Review-Journal's legal section and points people to the August 13 Planning Commission agenda. If you only read the headlines, these hearings can sneak up on you, and by the time a project is visible from the road, the big zoning call has already been made.

It is worth being precise about what is and is not settled. The 276-unit count and the RH-36 request are what the applicant is asking for. The August 13 hearing is where the public first gets a formal chance to weigh in. The exact mix of the homes, whether they lean toward apartments, townhomes, or smaller single-family lots, is the kind of detail that gets shaped during this process. If any of those specifics are still being finalized by the city, treat them as subject to change until the commission and council act.

To put the numbers in plain terms, 276 units spread across 103 acres is not wall-to-wall towers. A big chunk of that acreage often goes to roads, open space, drainage, and setbacks. The RH-36 label sets the ceiling for what could be built, not a promise that every inch gets packed. Still, the density request is the flashpoint, because it signals the builder wants flexibility to fit more homes on the site than a standard single-family zoning would allow. That is the exact word that tends to fill a hearing room.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

If you live in West Henderson, this is not a far-off headline. It is a change that could land within a short drive of your front door. New units near St. Rose Parkway mean more cars on nearby roads, more kids in local schools, and more neighbors sharing the same grocery stores and parks. That is why zoning hearings draw crowds. People show up because it affects the street they drive every day.

For homeowners, the impact cuts both ways. More housing supply can help slow runaway price growth, which is good news if you have kids trying to buy their first place nearby. At the same time, some owners worry that dense apartments next to single-family streets could change the feel of a neighborhood or press on their home values. Both reactions are normal, and both deserve a fair hearing at the commission.

For renters and first-time buyers, projects like this are part of the answer to a real problem. Henderson has been one of the most in-demand places in the valley, and demand has pushed prices up for years. Adding 276 units in a growing corridor gives more people a shot at living in the area. High-density zoning is often how a city fits more homes into land that is already close to jobs and freeways.

There is also a traffic angle that hits everyone. St. Rose Parkway is a major artery on the south and west side of Henderson. More homes usually mean more trips in and out during rush hour. The city will look at road capacity and access as part of the review. If you commute through that area, the details of how the project connects to the road network are worth watching.

Schools are another piece families ask about. When hundreds of new units land in a corridor, the local elementary, middle, and high schools feel it over time. Clark County School District boundaries and capacity are part of the bigger picture, even if a single rezoning does not decide them. Parents near St. Rose Parkway are right to ask how growth on the west side lines up with seats in the classroom, and those questions belong on the record at the hearing.

Then there is the simple matter of neighborhood character. People who bought in West Henderson for wide streets and mountain views tend to have strong feelings about what goes up next door. That is fair. The flip side is that a growing city cannot freeze in place. The healthiest outcome is usually a project that adds real housing while respecting the scale and feel of what is already there. Getting to that balance is exactly what the public process is for.

Background and History

West Henderson has been the growth story of the city for the better part of a decade. Once mostly open desert, the area has filled in fast with master-planned neighborhoods, new schools, retail centers, and big employers. The Raiders built their headquarters and practice facility out here, and the corridor around St. Rose Parkway and the 215 Beltway has become a magnet for builders. When land is available and jobs are close, housing follows.

That growth is exactly why zoning fights keep popping up. Henderson has spent years balancing new development against the wishes of residents who moved here for space and quiet. High-density requests like RH-36 tend to draw the most attention because they represent the biggest change from the low-density pattern that shaped early West Henderson. Each project becomes a small test of how the city wants to grow.

The valley as a whole is under pressure to build more homes. Clark County is mostly ringed by federal land, which limits how far the metro can sprawl. That squeeze pushes cities to allow denser projects on the land they do have. It is the same force behind the recent BLM land deals for affordable homes and the city's approval of new housing types. Henderson is not making these calls in a vacuum. It is part of a region trying to keep up with demand.

Woodfield asking for RH-36 fits this bigger pattern. Builders go for higher density when land is pricey and demand is strong, because more units help a project pencil out. The Planning Commission's job is to weigh that business reality against the impact on the people already living nearby. That tension is the heart of almost every rezoning story in a fast-growing city.

It also helps to know how Henderson tends to handle these requests. The city has a long track record of approving new housing, but often with conditions attached, such as height limits, landscaped buffers, wall requirements, or road upgrades tied to the project. That means the outcome is rarely a simple yes or no. It is usually a negotiated version of the plan. Residents who follow the process closely can shape those conditions, which is where the real, lasting details get decided.

What Happens Next

The next date to circle is August 13, 2026. That is when the Henderson Planning Commission takes up the rezoning at its scheduled hearing. Residents can attend, submit written comments, or speak during the public comment portion. The commission will then vote on whether to recommend the change.

A recommendation from the commission usually heads to the Henderson City Council for a final decision. The council can approve the rezoning, deny it, or approve it with conditions, such as limits on height, buffers between the new homes and existing streets, or road improvements. If conditions get attached, the final project can look different from the first proposal. That is normal and often where neighborhood concerns get addressed.

If everything moves forward, the timeline after approval depends on the builder. Site work, permits, and vertical construction can take many months to a couple of years before anyone moves in. Nothing about an August hearing means shovels hit the dirt this fall. This is the early, decision-making stage, which is exactly why it is the best moment for residents to speak up.

If the commission recommends denial, or if the council rejects the rezoning, the project as proposed would stall. A builder can come back with a revised plan, a lower density, or a different approach, but that resets the clock. Either way, the August 13 meeting is the moment that sets the tone. Watch how commissioners react to the density, the traffic study, and the public comment. Their questions usually preview where the council lands weeks later.

Ryan's Take

As a local agent, I watch these Henderson rezonings closely, because they tell you where the next wave of homes and buyers is heading. West Henderson has been a top pick for families for years, and that demand is not slowing down. Adding 276 units near St. Rose Parkway is a sign the market still wants to be out here. For buyers priced out of the newest single-family streets, higher-density options can be a real foot in the door.

My honest read is that this project, if approved, is more likely to help balance the market than hurt nearby values. Well-built, well-managed housing in a strong corridor tends to hold up fine next to single-family homes, especially when the city attaches smart conditions on design and traffic. The bigger risk is doing nothing. When a growing city refuses to add supply, prices climb and locals get pushed out. If you own in West Henderson, I would pay less attention to the fear of "apartments next door" and more attention to how the project handles roads, parking, and buffers. Those details matter far more to your day-to-day than the zoning label itself.

One more thing I tell clients. Zoning is a long game. A single hearing rarely makes or breaks a neighborhood, but the pattern of decisions over five or ten years shapes everything about how an area lives, drives, and holds value. West Henderson is still writing that story. If you own or plan to buy out here, treat these meetings as free market research. They tell you where the growth, the traffic, and the next round of demand are heading before it shows up in the listings.

What You Can Do

If you care about this one, the most useful thing you can do is show up or send a comment before the August 13 hearing. The Henderson Planning Commission takes public input, and firsthand voices from people who live nearby carry weight. You can find the agenda and details through the City of Henderson's website and its planning department. Look for the Planning Commission meeting materials tied to the Woodfield rezoning near St. Rose Parkway.

Come prepared with specifics. General "yes" or "no" comments are fine, but commissioners respond best to concrete points about traffic at a certain intersection, school capacity, drainage, or how the project meets the existing street. If you support more housing, say so too. Hearings often fill with opponents, so supportive residents can help balance the record.

You can also sign up for city notifications so you do not miss the next step. Rezonings that clear the Planning Commission usually get a follow-up hearing at the City Council, and each stage takes public comment. If you cannot make the August 13 meeting, a written comment on file still counts, and it keeps you in the loop for the council date.

And if you are thinking about buying, selling, or investing in West Henderson, keep an eye on how this and similar projects play out. New supply, road changes, and zoning shifts all move the market in ways that affect timing and price. Knowing what is coming helps you make a smarter call on your own home. A rezoning next door is not automatically good or bad for your value. What matters is the design, the traffic plan, and how the finished project fits the street.
