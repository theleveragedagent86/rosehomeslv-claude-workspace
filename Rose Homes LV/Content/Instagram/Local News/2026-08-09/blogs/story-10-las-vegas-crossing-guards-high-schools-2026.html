Las Vegas Adds Crossing Guards at 5 More High Schools as Students Head Back to Class

The city of Las Vegas is putting adult crossing guards at five more high schools this school year, and the timing is no accident. Students head back to class right now, and the busy streets around big campuses are some of the most dangerous places for young people to walk. The five new schools are Bonanza, Centennial, Desert Pines, Shadow Ridge, and Western.

This is part of a larger crossing guard program that now costs about $3.59 million and covers 92 schools across the city. For parents who live near these campuses, it means a trained adult in a bright vest will be standing at the corner when the first bell rings and when school lets out. That small change can be the difference between a safe walk home and a scary near miss.

What Happened

The city of Las Vegas announced that it is expanding its crossing guard program to five more high schools for the new school year. The five campuses getting guards are Bonanza High School, Centennial High School, Desert Pines High School, Shadow Ridge High School, and Western High School. The news landed on August 10, the same day more than 280,000 Clark County students returned to class.

With this expansion, the program now reaches 92 schools across the city. The full cost of the crossing guard program is about $3.59 million. That money pays for the guards, the training, the safety gear, and the day-to-day management of putting an adult at busy corners twice a day. It is a real budget line, not a one-time gesture.

High schools might seem like an odd place to add crossing guards. People often picture guards helping little kids cross the street outside an elementary school. But high schools bring their own risks. Teen drivers are new behind the wheel. Students walk and bike to school in large groups. And many big high schools sit on wide, fast roads where cars move quickly. A trained guard helps manage all of that.

The expansion is happening alongside a broader push for school safety across the valley. On the same day, the Clark County School District rolled out its own set of security upgrades, including single entry points and perimeter fencing at more campuses. The crossing guard news fits into that bigger back-to-school safety story. The message from local leaders is clear. They want students protected both inside the school and on the way there.

It helps to know where these five schools sit. Centennial and Shadow Ridge are both in the fast-growing northwest, an area that has seen heavy home building and rising traffic for years. Bonanza sits in the central part of the city, Western is in a busy older neighborhood near the core, and Desert Pines serves the east side. These are not quiet side streets. They are large campuses on roads that carry a lot of daily traffic, which is exactly why the city picked them.

The guards themselves go through training before they ever step into a crosswalk. They learn how to read traffic, how to hold cars, and how to move a group of students across safely. They wear bright vests and carry stop signs so drivers can see them from far away. It is a simple system, but it works because a trained adult can make split-second calls that a painted line and a signal light cannot.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

If you live near one of these five high schools, this news touches your daily life in a direct way. Morning drop-off and afternoon pickup are the busiest, most stressful times on neighborhood streets. Cars back up. Kids dart across the road. A guard at the corner slows everything down and adds a set of trained eyes right where you need them most.

For families who send their teens to school on foot or by bike, the peace of mind is worth a lot. Not every student gets a ride. Plenty of Las Vegas teens walk to Bonanza, Western, or Desert Pines every day. A guard at the crossing means someone is watching out for them at the spot where cars and students meet. Parents at those schools have asked for this kind of help for years.

There is also a money angle that touches every homeowner. School zones and street safety are part of what makes a neighborhood feel livable. When buyers look at a home near a high school, they think about traffic, noise, and safety. A visible safety program can help ease those worries. Homes near well-run, safe schools tend to hold their value better over time.

Even if you do not have kids in these schools, you drive these roads. The corners near Centennial in the northwest and Shadow Ridge in the same area get crowded fast on school days. A guard managing the crossing keeps traffic moving in a more orderly way. That means fewer sudden stops, fewer close calls, and a calmer commute for everyone who shares the road.

Renters feel the effect too. A large share of families near these campuses rent rather than own, and they care just as much about a safe walk to school. When a neighborhood feels safe and well managed, it stays in demand. That steady demand helps keep the whole area stable, which is good for renters, owners, and small businesses alike. Safety is not just a school issue. It is a neighborhood issue.

There is a real human cost when a school zone goes unmanaged. A single crash near a campus can change a family forever and leave a whole community shaken. Crossing guards are one of the cheapest ways to lower that risk. For a few hours a day at each corner, the city puts a trained person right where the danger is highest. When you think about the stakes, $3.59 million spread across 92 schools starts to look like a bargain.

Background and History

Crossing guard programs are not new to Las Vegas, but the reach keeps growing. For years, the city has funded guards mostly at elementary schools, where the youngest and most vulnerable walkers need the most help. Over time, city leaders and parents pushed to bring that same protection to older students at middle and high schools, where traffic dangers are just as real.

The growth to 92 schools shows how far the program has come. Adding five high schools in a single year is a meaningful jump. It reflects a steady trend in Southern Nevada of treating student safety as a shared job between the school district, the city, and local police. No single agency handles it all. The pieces fit together, and the crossing guard program is one of the most visible parts.

Las Vegas has struggled with pedestrian safety for a long time. The valley is built around wide, fast roads that were designed to move cars, not protect people on foot. Nevada has ranked among the more dangerous states for pedestrians in past national reports. School zones are one place where the city can make a fast, real difference, and crossing guards are a low-cost, proven tool.

The push also lines up with the back-to-school season, when safety is top of mind for every family. Each August, the valley sees a wave of reminders about school zone speed limits, bus stop rules, and safe walking routes. Rolling out new crossing guards during this window makes sense. It puts protection in place exactly when foot traffic near schools spikes.

The growth of the northwest tells part of this story too. Neighborhoods around Centennial and Shadow Ridge have filled with new homes and new families over the past decade. More families means more students walking, biking, and getting dropped off near those campuses. The road network has not always kept up with that growth, so adding guards is one way the city catches up with the demand that all this new housing created.

It is worth remembering that crossing guards are a proven idea, not an experiment. Cities across the country have used them for generations because they work. They cost far less than rebuilding a road or adding a traffic signal, and they can be put in place quickly. For a growing city like Las Vegas, that speed and low cost make them one of the most useful safety tools available.

What Happens Next

The five new crossing guard posts go live for the current school year, so parents at Bonanza, Centennial, Desert Pines, Shadow Ridge, and Western should start seeing guards at the crossings right away. If a corner near your school still feels unsafe, that is worth flagging to the city, since programs like this often adjust based on where the real problems show up.

Watch for the city to review how the program is working as the year goes on. When a program grows to 92 schools and $3.59 million, leaders tend to track whether it is making a difference. Fewer close calls and smoother traffic near these campuses would build the case to keep expanding. Parents can help by reporting both what is working and what still needs fixing.

The bigger picture is worth following too. This crossing guard expansion is one piece of a wave of school safety changes across Clark County this year. The district is adding security upgrades at more campuses, and new student rules are rolling out at the same time. Expect safety to stay a hot topic in local meetings and family group chats all year long.

There is also a budget cycle to keep an eye on. Programs like this get funded year by year, so the crossing guard line will come up again when the city plans its next budget. If parents and neighbors show up and speak in favor of it, that support helps lock in the funding and can push the program to grow. Local safety spending tends to survive when the community makes noise about how much it matters.

What You Can Do

If your teen attends one of the five schools, take a few minutes to walk or drive the route with them before the busy season really kicks in. Point out the new crossing guard post and remind them to use it. The guard is there to help, but students still need to cross at the right spot and wait for the signal to go.

Talk to your school and the city if a crossing near you still feels unsafe. Parent voices are a big reason programs like this grow. If you see a dangerous corner, a blocked crosswalk, or drivers speeding through a school zone, report it. The city added these guards because families spoke up, and that same feedback loop can bring safety help to more corners.

You can also help by driving carefully yourself. Slow down in school zones, put the phone away, and watch for the guard's stop sign. The best safety program in the world cannot work if drivers speed through it. Every one of us who uses these roads has a part to play, and small habits behind the wheel keep kids safe more than any single program can.

Stay in the loop on future safety plans too. City and school district meetings are where these decisions get made, and they are open to the public. If you want more guards, safer crosswalks, or a lower speed limit near your school, that is the room where your voice counts. Sign up for local alerts, follow your school's updates, and speak up when the topic comes around.
