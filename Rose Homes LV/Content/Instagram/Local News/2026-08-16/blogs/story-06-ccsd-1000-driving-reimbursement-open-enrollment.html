CCSD Will Pay Parents Up to $1,000 to Drive Their Kids Out of a One-Star School

The Clark County School District will pay some parents up to $1,000 this school year to drive their child to school, and the first deadline to qualify is Friday, August 21, 2026. That is only days away.

The money comes from a new pilot program. It is not for everyone. It is for families who are zoned for a one-star school and whose child attends a three-, four-, or five-star CCSD school through open enrollment. Parents get $500 at the end of each semester, for a total of $1,000 over the full school year.

Here is the part that catches people off guard. Students who attend a school through open enrollment do not get bus service. Families have to handle the driving themselves. This program is CCSD's attempt to help pay for that gas and mileage. If you have been thinking about open enrollment for your child, the calendar matters more than anything else in this article. August 21 is the cutoff to apply for the current school year.

What Happened

CCSD publicly announced the transportation reimbursement pilot program in July 2026, ahead of a school year that began Monday, August 10. Nevada Current reported the details on August 10, including several rules most parents had not seen yet.

The program is only for parents or guardians of CCSD students who are zoned for a one-star school but are attending a three-, four-, or five-star school through the district's open enrollment policy. Open enrollment was formerly called a change of school assignment, or COSA. It lets CCSD parents apply to send their child to a different district school that has open seats. Open enrollment is available to all students, but students zoned for one- or two-star schools get priority when seats at the requested school are limited.

To decide who qualifies, the program uses school star ratings from the 2024-25 school year. Those ratings are public and posted on NevadaReportCard.NV.gov. Families can look up their zoned school and their requested school in a couple of minutes.

There are two separate deadlines, and mixing them up is the easiest way to lose the money. The deadline to apply for open enrollment for the current school year is August 21, 2026. The deadline to apply for the transportation reimbursement program itself is September 4, 2026. You need the first one to get the second one.

The rules keep going. Students cannot miss more than 10 school days in one semester. Ten absences, excused or unexcused, is the state threshold for chronic absenteeism, and CCSD has been working hard to bring those numbers down. Parents also have to fill out an online form and then attest at the end of the semester that they faced financial hardship from transporting their student to and from school.

Not every open enrollment family qualifies. Students enrolled in magnet schools are not eligible for the reimbursement. On the other hand, students who drive themselves to school are eligible, and so are students driven by an adult other than a parent. In every case, the checks go to the parents or guardians listed in Infinite Campus.

The dollar amount is not flat for everyone. According to CCSD, the amount depends on how far a family has to travel. Families who drive 41 miles or more one way are expected to receive more than families driving 40 miles or less. Funding also depends on how many families qualify overall. CCSD projects that about 6,000 families will receive money.

Families with more than one child at different open enrollment schools, say an elementary student and a middle school student, may be eligible for money for each school they drive to. The district has not published those amounts yet.

CCSD declined Nevada Current's request for an interview. Instead the district sent a short pre-recorded video of CCSD Chief Academic Officer Gia Moore talking about the program. "Our families that are accessing our open enrollment choice options, they select those schools for a number of reasons," Moore said. "Some of it might be that they want to attend a higher star rating school, but a lot of it is driven by personal reasons." Moore said the most important part of the program is that it "really helps support our families in trying to access our school choice options."

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

For years, the biggest barrier to open enrollment in Clark County was not paperwork. It was the drive. Once a family leaves their zoned school, the bus is off the table. Somebody has to be in a car twice a day, every school day, at exactly the hours most people are trying to get to work.

That is a real cost. Two round trips a day across a valley this spread out adds up in gas, tires, oil changes, and time. For a household already stretched thin, a $1,000 reimbursement is not a rounding error. It can be the difference between "we would like to try a different school" and "we can actually make this work."

There is a catch families should plan for. The money arrives at the end of each semester, not up front. Parents pay for the gas in August and September and get the first $500 later. If your budget is tight in the fall, know that going in.

The 10-day absence limit is the other detail worth reading twice. Ten missed days in a semester, excused or unexcused, and the reimbursement is gone. Flu season, a family trip, or a stretch of appointments can eat that up fast. If you sign up, treat attendance like part of the deal.

There is a neighborhood effect too. When thousands of families drive across the valley instead of walking to a nearby campus, morning traffic around the most requested schools gets heavier. Pickup and drop-off lines stretch farther down the street. Neighbors who live near a three-, four-, or five-star school may notice more cars at 7:30 a.m. and again in the afternoon. That is not a reason to be against the program, but it is a practical change worth expecting on those streets.

Now zoom out to housing, because in Clark County school ratings and home prices are tied together. Buyers filter by school zone constantly. Two nearly identical houses a mile apart can carry different price tags because they feed different schools. Families who cannot afford to move into the zone they want often feel stuck with the address they can afford.

This program loosens that a little. It says the school your child attends does not have to be decided entirely by which street you could afford. For renters, for first-time buyers, and for anyone who bought a home before they had kids, that is a meaningful option. It does not replace a good neighborhood school, and it does not fix a long commute, but it gives families one more lever to pull.

Star ratings are also worth keeping in perspective. They are one state measure built largely on test scores and growth data. They shift from year to year, and they do not capture a great teacher, a strong band program, or a principal turning things around. Plenty of families are happy at schools with lower ratings, and plenty of families want something different. Both choices are fine. The point is that families now have information and a little money behind whichever choice they make.

Background and History

This pilot did not start with the school district. Nevada lawmakers created the transportation reimbursement program in a bipartisan bill passed during the 2025 legislative session. Lawmakers funded it for one academic year only.

That single year of funding is the most important thing to understand about the whole program. There is no money set aside for the 2027-28 school year unless the Nevada State Legislature chooses to fund it again when it meets for the 2027 regular session. Families should treat this as a one-year benefit until lawmakers say otherwise.

The timing also says something about where CCSD stands. District enrollment has been falling. Charter school enrollment outside the district has been climbing. A federal voucher program may pull more students toward private and homeschool options. A Las Vegas Review-Journal analysis earlier this year found that the steepest elementary enrollment drops were concentrated in some of Clark County's poorest ZIP codes.

Fewer students means less funding, and the district has acknowledged it is studying whether to consolidate or close some schools. Seen in that light, a program that pays families to stay inside CCSD rather than leave for a charter is not just a favor to parents. It is also a retention strategy. CCSD's own FAQ makes that plain. A charter student zoned for a one-star CCSD school would qualify for the money if they returned to the district and used open enrollment to attend a higher rated school. A homeschooled student zoned for a one-star CCSD school would qualify the same way.

It also helps to know how open enrollment got here. For years the process was known as a change of school assignment, and it had a reputation among parents for being confusing and slow. The district rebranded and simplified it under the open enrollment name and put it online at itsyourchoice.ccsd.net. Adding priority for students zoned for one- and two-star schools was the next step, and paying for the drive is the newest one. Each change chips away at the same problem, which is that families zoned for a struggling school historically had the fewest realistic ways out.

Transportation has been a sore subject in Clark County all month. Families reported buses running badly behind schedule during the first week of classes, and CCSD has been working through routing problems tied to new software. Against that backdrop, telling open enrollment families "here is help paying for your own car" lands differently than it would have a year ago.

What Happens Next

The near-term calendar is short and unforgiving. Open enrollment applications for the current school year are due August 21, 2026. Applications for the transportation reimbursement program are due September 4, 2026. Both are online. If you want this money for your family, those two dates are the entire assignment for the next three weeks.

After that, the process runs on the semester clock. Parents track attendance, keep their student under the 10-day absence limit, and attest to financial hardship at the end of the fall semester. The first $500 follows. The same cycle repeats in the spring for the second $500. Checks go to the parent or guardian on file in Infinite Campus.

A few things are still unsettled. CCSD has not published how much households with multiple children at different open enrollment schools will receive. The final per-family amount also depends on how many families qualify statewide and how far each one drives, so the exact numbers will firm up after the September 4 deadline passes and the district can count applicants against its 6,000-family projection.

The bigger question arrives in 2027. Lawmakers funded one year. If the 2027 Legislature does not renew it, families who built a school-year routine around this reimbursement will be back to paying the full cost themselves in 2027-28. Watch that session if your family plans to keep using open enrollment.

Also watch the enrollment and school consolidation conversation. If this program pulls students back from charters and homeschool, it strengthens CCSD's argument for keeping campuses open. If it does not move the needle, the closure and consolidation study will carry more weight.

Ryan's Take

I have watched school zones move Las Vegas home prices for years. Buyers walk into a house they love, pull out their phone, look up the assigned schools, and either lean in or lose interest. It happens in Summerlin, in Green Valley, in Mountains Edge, in Aliante, all over the valley. Two homes with the same square footage and the same finishes can sell for noticeably different numbers because of which schools they feed.

So a program that helps a family attend a different CCSD school without moving is genuinely interesting to me. It slightly loosens the knot between an address and a school assignment. For a buyer who can afford one neighborhood but wants a different school, that is a real option. Just do not build a purchase decision on it. This is one year of state funding with a 2027 renewal question attached, and star ratings themselves change. Buy the house and the neighborhood you actually want to live in for the next several years, then use open enrollment as a tool on top of that, not as a substitute for it.

And if you are already zoned for a one-star school and you have been curious about open enrollment, this is the cheapest time you will ever have to try it. Five days to decide is not much notice, but $1,000 for a drive you might have made anyway is worth an evening of research.

What You Can Do

Start with two lookups tonight. First, find your zoned school's star rating from the 2024-25 school year on NevadaReportCard.NV.gov. Second, visit CCSD's open enrollment page at itsyourchoice.ccsd.net to see which schools have open seats. If your zoned school is rated one star and the school you want is rated three, four, or five stars, you are in the eligible lane.

Then move on the deadlines. Submit your open enrollment application by August 21, 2026. Once your child is enrolled, submit the transportation reimbursement form by September 4, 2026. Remember that magnet school students are not eligible, and that students who drive themselves or who ride with another adult still count. Full program details are posted on CCSD's transportation reimbursement page.

Finally, plan the practical side. Map the real drive at 7 a.m. and at dismissal, not at noon on a Sunday, so you know what you are signing up for. Set aside money for gas in the fall, because the first $500 comes at the end of the semester. Track attendance closely and stay under 10 absences. If you have more than one student, ask the district in writing what your household can expect, since those amounts are still being worked out.
