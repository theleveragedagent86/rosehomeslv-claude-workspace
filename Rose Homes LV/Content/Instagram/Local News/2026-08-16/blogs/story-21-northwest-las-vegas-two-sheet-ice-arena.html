Las Vegas Is Still Picking a Builder for a Two-Sheet Ice Arena in the Northwest Valley

The city of Las Vegas is still reviewing proposals to build a two-sheet ice arena in the northwest valley, and city officials say they plan to pick a winning bid by the end of 2026. The site is five acres of city-owned land at North Hualapai Way and West Deer Springs Way, just off Centennial Parkway near the 215 Beltway.

Nothing has been awarded yet. The city opened the proposal period in the spring, and the window for developers to submit closed on June 15, 2026. Since then, staff has been working through the submissions. No winning bid has been announced, and no groundbreaking date exists.

Still, this is one of the quieter stories in the valley that matters a lot to a specific group of families. If you live in Centennial Hills, Skye Canyon, Providence, Tule Springs, or anywhere along the northwest edge of the city and you have a kid who plays hockey or figure skates, you already know the problem. The closest sheet of ice is at least 10 miles from your driveway, and the good practice slots start before sunrise or run past bedtime.

Councilwoman Francis Allen-Palenske put it plainly: "We don't have enough ice for the demand in hockey." She also pointed out that roughly 3,500 homes are coming online in the northwest corridor. More homes means more kids, and more kids means more demand for a resource the northwest simply does not have right now.

What Happened

In April 2026, the city of Las Vegas opened a formal proposal period for a new ice arena in the northwest part of the city. The city owns about five acres at the corner of North Hualapai Way and West Deer Springs Way. That land sits near Centennial Parkway and the 215 Beltway, which makes it easy to reach from most of the northwest.

The city is not building the arena itself. It is looking for a private partner to design, build, and operate the facility on city land. That is a common setup for sports venues at this scale, and it lets the city add an amenity without carrying the full construction cost on its own books.

The requirements are specific. The city asked for two separate sheets of ice, and both must meet NHL regulations. That means each sheet has to be sized and built to the same standard the pros use, not a small recreational oval. The city also asked that both sheets be available year round for youth hockey, adult leagues, and semi-pro play. Year round matters in Las Vegas, where a seasonal rink would be almost useless for a competitive travel program.

The submission window ran through June 15, 2026. City officials said they would review the proposals and select a winning bid by the end of 2026. As of the middle of August 2026, no announcement has been made. That means the decision is still open, and anyone telling you a builder has been chosen is ahead of the facts.

One quick note on timing. The original reporting on this proposal period ran on April 6, 2026. That is older than the news we usually cover in this roundup. We are including it because the decision itself is still pending and it lands before the end of this year. Treat this as a live process, not a finished deal.

Two sheets is the detail worth underlining. A single-sheet rink in a growing area fills up almost immediately and then becomes its own kind of bottleneck. Two sheets lets a facility run a game on one side and practices on the other, host a tournament without shutting down learn-to-skate classes, and spread ice time across more teams. It roughly doubles the usable hours in the same building.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Start with the drive. Right now the nearest sheets of ice to the northwest valley are all at least 10 miles away. City National Arena sits in Summerlin. Las Vegas Ice Center is on Flamingo Road. Hylo Park Arena is in North Las Vegas. America First Center is in Henderson. Every one of those is a real trip from Skye Canyon or the north end of Centennial Hills, and the Henderson option is a cross-valley haul.

For a hockey family, that distance is not a one-time inconvenience. It is a weekly, sometimes daily routine. Practices, games, skill sessions, and tournaments all stack up. Parents in the northwest are often driving 20 to 40 minutes each way, several times a week, at the worst hours of the day. Add a second kid in the program and the schedule gets close to impossible.

That drive also shapes who plays. When the nearest ice is 10 or 15 miles out, plenty of families never sign up in the first place. Kids who might love the sport end up in something closer to home. A rink in the northwest would not just shorten trips for current players. It would open the sport to a whole set of families who were priced out by time rather than money.

There is a broader amenity story here too. The northwest has grown fast, and the housing has arrived ahead of the recreation. Roughly 3,500 more homes are coming online in that corridor. Rooftops tend to show up first, and the parks, sports facilities, and gathering spots follow years later. A two-sheet arena would be a meaningful catch-up piece for a part of the valley that has been building homes faster than it has been building places for kids to do something.

And it is not only hockey. A year-round two-sheet facility usually supports public skating, learn-to-skate lessons, figure skating, adult leagues, birthday parties, and summer camps. In a city where July afternoons sit well over 100 degrees, an indoor cold-weather activity is a genuinely useful thing to have within a few miles of home.

Traffic is part of this too. Every one of those cross-valley trips puts another car on the 215 Beltway or on Rainbow and Rampart during the evening rush. A facility in the northwest keeps a chunk of that driving local. It will not fix anyone's commute on its own, but it takes a repeat trip off the map for hundreds of families, and those trips happen at the exact hours the roads are worst.

There is also a jobs and small-business side that gets overlooked. Rinks employ coaches, skate techs, front-desk staff, and concession workers, and they usually pull a pro shop and a snack bar into the building. Tournaments bring visiting teams that book hotel rooms and eat at nearby restaurants. For a corner of the valley that is mostly rooftops and strip retail right now, a facility that draws weekend crowds is a small but real economic anchor.

Background and History

Las Vegas was not a hockey town for most of its history. That changed fast. The Vegas Golden Knights arrived in 2017, and the sport went from niche to mainstream in the valley almost overnight. Youth registration climbed, adult leagues filled, and learn-to-skate programs started running waitlists.

The ice supply did not keep up. City National Arena opened in Summerlin as the Golden Knights practice facility and quickly became the busiest community rink in the valley. Las Vegas Ice Center on Flamingo Road has been serving skaters for years. North Las Vegas added Hylo Park Arena, and Henderson has America First Center. That is a short list for a metro area with more than 2 million people.

The valley also lost ice along the way. The rink at Fiesta Rancho in North Las Vegas closed when that property shut down, which removed a sheet that a lot of local families relied on. Losing a rink in a market that already had a shortage made the squeeze worse, and it pushed even more demand onto the remaining facilities.

Meanwhile the northwest kept growing. Skye Canyon, Providence, and the newer stretches of Centennial Hills added thousands of homes over the past decade. Those neighborhoods are full of young families, which is exactly the demographic that fills youth sports programs. The geography created the gap: a lot of new families on one side of the valley, and almost all of the ice on the other.

The pro side of the sport kept growing too. The Henderson Silver Knights play their home games at Lee's Family Forum, which gave the valley a second team and a second set of fans. Every winning season and every playoff run sends more kids to a learn-to-skate class. That pipeline is good for the sport, but it lands on the same handful of sheets.

So the city looking for a private partner to put two NHL-sized sheets on land it already owns is not a random idea. It is a direct response to a shortage that people in the northwest have been complaining about for years.

What Happens Next

The next real milestone is the bid selection. City officials said they plan to review the proposals and pick a winner by the end of 2026. That gives us a window between now and December. Watch for a Las Vegas City Council agenda item naming the selected developer, because that is where a decision like this usually becomes public.

After a bid is selected, the work is far from done. A project like this still needs a development agreement or ground lease with the city, design work, permitting, and financing. Only then does construction start. Even on a smooth path, that sequence takes time. If you are hoping to skate there next winter, that is not realistic.

It is also fair to say the project could change or stall. Proposal periods do not always produce a partner the city wants to sign. Costs can come in higher than expected. A selected developer can walk. None of that is a prediction, just a reminder that a pending bid review is not the same thing as a funded, permitted project.

The details in the eventual agreement will matter as much as the building. Watch for how the ice hours get split between youth programs, adult leagues, and public skating, because that split decides whether a local kid can actually get a reasonable practice slot. Watch the pricing structure too. A rink two miles from your house is not much help if the hourly ice rate prices out house league teams.

Here is what would count as real progress: a named winning bidder, a council vote approving the agreement, a published site plan, and a construction timeline with dates. Until you see those, the honest summary is that the city wants this built, owns the land, and is still choosing who builds it.

Ryan's Take

I talk to a lot of buyers moving into the northwest, and youth sports comes up more often than people outside real estate would guess. Families do not just ask about square footage and school ratings. They ask where the kids will practice, how far the drive is, and whether the neighborhood has anything to do on a Saturday. A parent with a hockey kid will absolutely factor a 30-minute drive to the nearest rink into where they buy.

That is why I think this project matters more than the size of the headline suggests. Amenities like this quietly shape demand. When a specialized facility lands in an area, it pulls in the families who need it and it gives everyone else one more reason to stay. Centennial Hills and Skye Canyon already have the homes and the young families. What they have been missing is the stuff that keeps a family rooted in a specific part of the valley instead of shopping across it.

I would not buy a house today because of a rink that has not been awarded yet. That would be getting ahead of the facts. But if you are already weighing the northwest, this is a genuine point in its favor, and it is worth tracking through the end of the year.

What You Can Do

If you want this arena built, say so where it counts. The site sits in the northwest, and Councilwoman Francis Allen-Palenske has been the public voice pushing for more ice. City council members and their staff read constituent email, and a short, specific note about your family and your current drive time is more useful than a generic complaint. Ward information and contact details are available on the city of Las Vegas website.

Watch the Las Vegas City Council agendas between now and the end of 2026. Agendas are posted publicly ahead of each meeting, and a development agreement or ground lease for this site would show up there. If the item gets scheduled, public comment is open, and a room with hockey parents in it tends to get noticed.

In the meantime, plan around the ice we have. If your family needs practice time this season, get on registration and lesson lists early at City National Arena in Summerlin, Las Vegas Ice Center on Flamingo Road, Hylo Park Arena in North Las Vegas, or America First Center in Henderson. Slots at the reasonable hours go fast, and early sign-ups are the only real advantage available while the northwest waits.

Talk to your youth hockey association as well. Local clubs and leagues are usually the loudest and best-informed voices in these conversations, and they often hear about facility news before it hits the paper. If your club is organizing letters or turnout for a council meeting, that is the most efficient way to add your name to the effort.

And if you are house hunting in the northwest right now, look at what is actually built versus what is proposed. Parks, schools, and sports facilities that already exist are worth more to your daily life than the ones on a map. Ask about both, and know which is which.
