Clark County Approves Parking, Retail, and a 2,500-Seat Theater on 26 Acres Around the A's Ballpark

The Clark County Commission approved a package of land use permissions on Wednesday, August 19, 2026, covering the 26 acres that wrap around the Athletics ballpark site on the Las Vegas Strip. The approved plans include a three-level parking garage with 953 spaces, a retail and dining plaza built on top of it, a 2,500-seat theater, and a temporary surface lot with roughly 532 spaces.

One big piece did not make it through. Bally's proposed high-rise hotel towers were left out of the approval because they still need clearance from the Federal Aviation Administration. That means the ballpark district is moving forward in pieces, not all at once, and the commission is set to look at hotel-casino permits again on September 16.

If you live anywhere on the south end of the valley, this is the story that decides what the neighborhood around the stadium actually looks like when the first pitch is thrown. A ballpark by itself is a building. A ballpark with parking, restaurants, shops, a theater, and eventually hotel towers is a district. This vote was about the district.

What Happened at the August 19 Meeting

Clark County commissioners took up a set of land use requests tied to the property surrounding the Athletics ballpark site. The land sits on the Strip and is part of the larger redevelopment of the old Tropicana property. The requests came from Bally's, which controls the land around the stadium footprint.

The commission signed off on a mixed-use podium at the northwest corner of the site. Underneath it sits a three-level parking garage with 953 spaces. On top of that garage goes a retail and dining plaza. That is the piece that turns a parking structure into a place people actually walk through, because the cars go below and the shops and restaurants sit above.

At the southwest corner, the commission approved a 2,500-seat theater. That is a mid-size venue, smaller than an arena and larger than a club. Las Vegas already has plenty of both. What it does not have much of on that stretch of the Strip is a room that size sitting right next to a Major League Baseball stadium, which makes it useful on nights when there is no game.

The package also included a temporary surface lot with roughly 532 spaces. Temporary is the key word. Surface parking is what a site uses while the permanent structures get built, and it usually gets replaced later by something taller and denser. Between the garage and the surface lot, the approved plan puts close to 1,500 parking spaces on the site.

It helps to picture the 26 acres as a ring around the stadium. The ballpark takes the middle. The corners are where the money-making pieces go, because corners get the foot traffic and the street visibility. The northwest corner got the podium with parking below and retail above. The southwest corner got the theater. That leaves the rest of the ring open for future phases, which is where the hotel proposals live.

The hotel towers are the part that stalled. Bally's has proposed high-rise hotel buildings on the property, and those did not get approved on August 19. The reason is the FAA. Harry Reid International Airport sits just east of this land, and any tall building near a major airport has to clear federal height review before local government can approve it. Until that clearance comes through, the towers stay on hold. Commissioners are scheduled to review hotel-casino permits on September 16.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Start with traffic, because that is what most people ask about first. The south Strip already backs up on event nights. Adding a baseball stadium with a full season of home games means a lot more cars moving through the same intersections. How many parking spaces get built, and where they sit, changes how bad that gets. A 953-space garage plus a 532-space temporary lot is not enough to hold a full stadium crowd on its own, which tells you the plan leans on nearby resort garages, rideshare, and walking.

That matters for people who live in the neighborhoods just west of the Strip. Streets like Dean Martin Drive and the residential pockets off Tropicana Avenue are the natural overflow when a venue runs out of parking. Homeowners in those areas have watched this happen with other Strip venues. The parking plan approved this month is one of the tools the county has to keep game-night traffic on the Strip side instead of pushing it into side streets.

Then there are jobs. Construction on the podium, the garage, and the theater means work for local trades over the next couple of years. After that, retail, dining, and a 2,500-seat venue mean permanent hospitality jobs. Those are the jobs that fill apartments and starter homes in Enterprise, Paradise, Spring Valley, and the southwest valley. Construction jobs are temporary. Restaurant, retail, and venue jobs stick around and support steady housing demand.

For renters and buyers near the south Strip, this is a signal about where value is headed. Areas near large entertainment districts usually see rents firm up and buyer interest pick up as the district gets built out. That cuts both ways. If you own a home nearby, you may benefit. If you are renting nearby and hoping prices stay soft, a big new entertainment district is not working in your favor over the long run.

The theater is the piece I would watch if you care about the character of the area. A 2,500-seat room can book touring acts, comedy, residencies, and corporate events. It fills weeknights when the stadium is dark. That steady flow of people is what keeps restaurants and shops alive year round instead of only during baseball season. Districts that only work 81 nights a year tend to feel empty the rest of the time, and everybody in Las Vegas has seen that movie before.

There is a convention angle too. Las Vegas runs on trade shows, and a mid-size venue attached to hotel rooms and a stadium is a package that convention planners like. If the hotel towers eventually clear the FAA and get built, this corner becomes a self-contained destination. Guests could land at Harry Reid, get to their room, attend a session, catch a show, and see a ballgame without ever getting in a car. That kind of density is exactly what drives long-term demand for housing in the surrounding valley, because it takes staff to run all of it.

Background and History

This land has a long Las Vegas story attached to it. The site is tied to the old Tropicana property on the corner of Tropicana Avenue and Las Vegas Boulevard, one of the oldest resort corners on the Strip. The Athletics ballpark project put that land back into play, and the acreage around the stadium became one of the most valuable undeveloped blocks in Southern Nevada.

Bally's controls the land around the ballpark footprint, and the company has adjusted its plans more than once as the project has moved along. That is normal for a project this size. Costs shift, financing shifts, and what pencils out in year one does not always pencil out in year three. What is worth noticing is that the company brought forward parking, retail, and a theater first, and left the hotel towers for a later step.

The FAA piece is not new to Las Vegas either. Because Harry Reid International Airport sits so close to the south end of the Strip, height limits shape what can be built there. Developers have run into this for decades. Any high-rise proposed in the flight path has to go through federal review, and that review runs on its own timeline, separate from anything the county controls. So a hotel tower can be perfectly fine under county zoning and still sit and wait.

This is also not the first ballpark-related item the commission has handled. Earlier coverage this year focused on construction spending and structural milestones at the stadium itself, including roof work. Those were about the building. This approval is about everything around the building, which is a separate set of decisions and a separate set of permits.

One more piece of history worth knowing is how Clark County handles land use on the Strip. Most of the Strip is not inside Las Vegas city limits. It sits in unincorporated Clark County, which is why the County Commission and not the Las Vegas City Council votes on these items. People get this wrong all the time. If you want to weigh in on anything happening on the Strip, the County Commission is your body, and the commissioner for that district is your representative.

It also explains why the approvals come in stages. Large Strip projects usually move through a series of use permits, design reviews, and waivers rather than one giant vote. Each stage gets its own hearing and its own public comment window. That is slower, and it frustrates developers, but it gives residents repeated chances to see what is actually being proposed instead of finding out after the fact.

What Happens Next

The date to circle is September 16. That is when the Clark County Commission is scheduled to review hotel-casino permits tied to this property. If the FAA clearance is in hand by then, the tower conversation can move forward. If it is not, expect another delay, because the county cannot approve what the federal review has not cleared.

Beyond that meeting, watch for building permits and construction activity on the northwest corner. Land use approval is the first gate, not the last one. A project still needs construction permits, and the timing of those permits tells you whether the developer is actually ready to build or simply locking in entitlements for later. Entitlements have value on their own, and it is common for approved projects to sit for a while before a shovel hits dirt.

Also keep an eye on how the parking plan evolves. That temporary 532-space surface lot is a placeholder. At some point there will be a proposal to replace it with something permanent, and that proposal will come back through the county. Every one of those steps is a chance for the public to see the plan and comment on it.

There is a broader construction picture here as well. The south Strip is one of the busiest building zones in the country right now, and this district is only part of it. When several large projects run at the same time, labor gets tight, material costs move, and schedules stretch. If timelines on the ballpark district slip a few months, that is not necessarily a sign of trouble. It is what happens when a lot of big work is happening on the same few square miles.

Ryan's Take

I pay attention to approvals like this because they tell you where the money is going before the market catches up. A stadium alone does not move home values much. A stadium with a walkable retail plaza, a theater, restaurants, and eventually hotel rooms is a different animal. That is a district, and districts change the map for the neighborhoods that sit within a couple of miles.

The part I find most telling is the order of operations. Parking, retail, and a theater got approved. Hotel towers did not. That is a developer building the pieces that generate revenue on non-game nights while the big-ticket, capital-heavy piece waits on a federal sign-off. It is a smart sequence, and it also tells you the towers are further out than a lot of people assume. If you are making a buying decision near the south Strip based on hotel towers going up soon, slow down and watch the September 16 meeting first.

For homeowners in Enterprise, Paradise, and the southwest valley, my honest read is that this is a slow, steady positive. Not a spike. Construction and hospitality jobs support demand at the entry price points, and the entertainment district gives the area an identity it did not have ten years ago. That kind of change shows up in values over years, not weeks.

What You Can Do

If you want to follow this closely, the Clark County Commission posts its agendas and meeting materials online ahead of each meeting. The September 16 agenda is the one to look for if you care about the hotel towers. Agendas usually go up several days in advance, and the staff reports attached to each item often contain site maps and details that never make it into news coverage.

Public comment is open at commission meetings, and land use items in particular draw resident input. If you live in a neighborhood that would take on overflow traffic or parking from the ballpark district, that is the room where saying so actually counts. You do not need to be an expert. Describing what happens on your street on a busy night is exactly the kind of information commissioners use.

If you own a home near the south Strip, this is also a good moment to get a clear read on your value. Big development nearby tends to move things in slow steps, and most owners find out what changed only when they finally list. Knowing your number now gives you options later, whether that means selling into the run-up, refinancing, or pulling equity for a second property.

Renters have a play here too. If you are signing a lease in Enterprise, Paradise, or the southwest valley, the rental market right now is soft and landlords are competing hard. That gives you leverage on term length and price. Locking in a longer lease while the market favors renters is a reasonable way to sit out the next stretch of construction and see how the district actually turns out before you commit to buying nearby.

And if you are thinking about buying near the south Strip, walk the area at different times. Drive Tropicana on a weekday afternoon and again on a Saturday night. Look at where the construction fencing sits and where the crews park. The gap between how a neighborhood looks in listing photos and how it feels on a busy night is where most buyer regret comes from, and it is completely avoidable with an hour of your time.
