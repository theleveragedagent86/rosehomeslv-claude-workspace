Hard Rock's Guitar-Shaped Tower Is Nearly Enclosed on the Las Vegas Strip

The 42-story guitar-shaped tower at the Hard Rock Las Vegas is now almost completely wrapped in blue-tinted glass, and the roughly $4 billion resort is on pace to open in late 2027. Construction photos taken on Friday, August 21, 2026, show the exterior of the Guitar Hotel nearing completion at one of the most valuable corners on the Las Vegas Strip.

That matters to a lot more people than tourists. A resort this size means thousands of construction jobs right now and thousands of permanent hospitality jobs later, and those paychecks turn into rent checks and mortgage payments across the Las Vegas valley. If you live in Clark County, this is not just a building. It is a jobs engine going up in glass and steel.

What Happened

Photos from Friday, August 21, 2026, show the Hard Rock Las Vegas guitar tower largely enclosed. The exterior glass work is close to finished, which is one of the biggest milestones in any high-rise build. Once a tower is enclosed, crews can work inside no matter what the weather is doing outside, and the interior build-out can run at full speed. On a Las Vegas summer job site, that is a very big deal.

The tower stands about 700 feet tall. The first five floors take up the lower 100 feet of the building. Above that, roughly 675 rooms and suites fill the upper 560 feet. The shape is the whole point. From the street, the tower reads as a guitar, with the body at the bottom and the neck rising toward the top. It is meant to be seen from a long way off, and it already is.

The full resort is much larger than the tower alone. Plans call for about 3,600 hotel rooms across the property, nearly 175,000 square feet of gaming space, two spas, multiple pools, live entertainment venues, and dozens of restaurants and retail shops. The total price tag is around $4 billion. That puts it in the same conversation as the largest single private developments in Nevada history.

The target opening is late 2027. That is still more than a year out, which means the visible glass work you can see from Las Vegas Boulevard is only part of the job. Interior finishes, gaming floor build-out, kitchen installations, entertainment venue systems, and licensing all still have to happen. The Las Vegas Review-Journal reported the construction progress and the late-2027 pacing.

Breaking Down the Numbers

It helps to slow down and look at what the published figures actually describe, because "$4 billion resort" is an easy phrase to skim past. Around 3,600 hotel rooms is a small city. For comparison, a typical new apartment community in the southwest valley might hold 300 units. This single property will hold roughly a dozen of those, stacked on one piece of Las Vegas Boulevard, and every room needs to be cleaned, stocked, repaired, and sold.

Nearly 175,000 square feet of gaming space is another number worth sitting with. That is a floor plate larger than three football fields. Gaming floors that size need dealers on every shift, plus supervisors, cage staff, surveillance, slot techs, and cocktail service. Casinos run around the clock, which means every position is really three or four positions once you account for shifts, days off, and coverage.

Then layer on two spas, multiple pools, live entertainment venues, and dozens of restaurants and retail shops. Each restaurant is its own small business with its own kitchen crew, front of house staff, and management. Each entertainment venue needs sound, lighting, stagehands, box office, and security. This is why a resort of this size is less like one employer and more like a business district under one roof.

The tower dimensions tell the same story from a different angle. About 700 feet of height, with the first five floors filling the lower 100 feet and roughly 675 rooms and suites stacked in the upper 560 feet. Those lower floors are where the public space lives. The upper floors are the revenue engine. Building both at once, on an active corner of the Strip, is a logistics problem as much as a construction problem.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Start with the jobs, because that is the part that touches the most households. A $4 billion resort build employs thousands of trade workers. Electricians, plumbers, glaziers, drywall crews, HVAC techs, elevator installers, and finish carpenters all get years of steady work out of a project this size. Those are good wages, and in Clark County a good construction wage is often the difference between renting and buying.

Then comes the permanent side. A property with about 3,600 rooms, 175,000 square feet of gaming, two spas, multiple pools, and dozens of restaurants needs a very large staff. Think housekeepers, dealers, cooks, servers, bartenders, valets, security, front desk agents, engineers, and managers. Resorts of this scale in Las Vegas typically employ thousands of people once they are running at full speed. Every one of those workers needs somewhere to live.

That housing demand does not land on the Strip. It lands in the neighborhoods where hospitality workers actually live. That means the southwest valley, Spring Valley, Paradise, Winchester, Sunrise Manor, the north valley near Aliante, and the growing Henderson corridors along the 215. When a new resort staffs up, rental demand in those areas tightens first, and entry-level home demand follows.

There is also the traffic and construction reality to live with in the meantime. Crews, deliveries, and equipment move in and out of the Strip corridor daily. If you commute anywhere near Las Vegas Boulevard, Harmon, Flamingo, or the I-15 ramps in that stretch, you are already sharing the road with this project. That continues through 2027.

Which Neighborhoods Feel It First

Strip jobs are shift jobs. Swing shifts, graveyard shifts, split days off, and mandatory overtime around big weekends. That schedule shapes where people choose to live more than almost any other factor. Nobody wants a 45 minute drive home at 4 in the morning. So the housing pressure from a new resort lands in a fairly predictable ring around the Strip corridor.

Paradise and Winchester sit closest. These are older unincorporated Clark County neighborhoods with a mix of apartments, condos, and small single family homes, and they have absorbed Strip workers for decades. Spring Valley to the west offers a similar deal with slightly newer housing stock and easy access via Flamingo, Tropicana, and Harmon. All three are the first places rental listings tighten when a big property staffs up.

Push a little further and you hit the southwest valley, which has newer construction and more inventory but a longer drive. Sunrise Manor and the east valley offer some of the most affordable purchase prices in the county with reasonable Strip access. North Las Vegas and the Aliante area draw workers who want a yard and are willing to trade commute time for square footage. Henderson along the 215 works for people who want schools and amenities and can live with the drive.

The pattern is worth understanding even if you have no interest in hospitality work. If you own a home or a rental in the inner ring, a new $4 billion resort a few miles away is a demand story for your property. If you are shopping in those same neighborhoods, you are competing in a market that has a known catalyst on the calendar. Neither of those facts guarantees anything, but both are worth factoring into your timing.

There is a commuter angle too. More employees in one place means more cars on the same handful of arterials. Tropicana, Flamingo, Harmon, Koval, Paradise Road, and the I-15 ramps in that stretch already carry heavy Strip traffic. Adding thousands of shift workers to the mix will be noticeable, especially at shift change hours that do not line up with normal rush hour.

Background and History

The site itself has one of the most recognizable histories in Las Vegas. This is the property that spent decades as one of the Strip's signature resorts before Hard Rock took it over and began the conversion. Rebranding and rebuilding a property in that location is not a small remodel. It is a near total reinvention of a large, central piece of Strip real estate.

Hard Rock has already proven the guitar-shaped hotel concept works. The company built a guitar-shaped tower at its Florida property, and it became an instant landmark and a marketing machine. Photos of that building traveled the world. Bringing that same idea to Las Vegas Boulevard was an obvious next step for a brand that sells music culture as much as it sells rooms.

Las Vegas has a long pattern here. The valley tears down and rebuilds its Strip properties roughly every generation. Each cycle brings a bigger, more expensive, more amenity-heavy resort. Each cycle also brings a construction boom, a hiring wave, and a wave of new housing demand that ripples out into the neighborhoods. Locals who have lived here 20 or 30 years have watched this movie several times.

What is different this cycle is the sheer amount happening at once. The A's ballpark district is moving through approvals. Downtown properties are trading hands. Transportation projects and new mobility rules are reshaping how people move around the valley. The Hard Rock guitar tower is one large piece of a much bigger construction and investment wave hitting Clark County at the same time.

What Happens Next

The next phase is interior work. With the tower enclosed, crews move to finishes, mechanical systems, guest room build-out, and the gaming floor. Expect the exterior to look basically done long before the inside is close. That is normal. It is also why people driving by often assume a resort is about to open when it is still a year or more away.

Watch for hiring announcements. Large Strip resorts typically begin large-scale recruiting several months before opening, sometimes with job fairs that draw thousands of applicants. If the late-2027 target holds, that recruiting push would likely begin sometime in 2027. That is the moment the housing impact becomes real rather than theoretical.

Also watch for gaming licensing and regulatory steps, opening date updates, and any announcements about restaurant and entertainment partners. Those details tend to trickle out over the final 12 to 18 months. Construction timelines on projects this size can and do shift, so treat late 2027 as a target rather than a promise.

One more thing to track is the pace of visible change. Enclosure is the point where a tower stops looking different every week. For the next stretch, the biggest signs of progress will be things you cannot see from the road, like elevator commissioning, mechanical testing, and floor by floor guest room turnover. If the building looks frozen for months, that does not mean work stopped. It means the work moved indoors.

Ryan's Take

I get asked about this building constantly, usually by clients driving down the Strip who point at it and say "what is that thing." My honest answer is that the guitar shape is the least interesting part. The interesting part is what a $4 billion, 3,600-room resort does to the local labor market and, by extension, to housing demand in the neighborhoods around the Strip.

Here is the practical read. Big Strip openings create thousands of new paychecks in a fairly short window. Those workers need housing near their shifts, and Strip workers overwhelmingly want short commutes because of the hours they keep. That pushes demand into Paradise, Winchester, Spring Valley, the southwest, and the older east valley neighborhoods with existing rental stock. If you own a rental in those areas, this is a tailwind. If you are shopping for one, the window to buy ahead of the hiring wave is now, not in 2028.

I would not overstate it either. One resort does not move the entire Las Vegas market, and we have a lot of inventory and a lot of rental concessions right now. But layered on top of the ballpark district, the downtown activity, and everything else in the pipeline, the cumulative effect over the next few years is real. Pay attention to the pattern, not just the one building.

What You Can Do

If you are in the trades, this project and the others in the pipeline mean the Clark County construction labor market should stay busy through 2027. Union halls and the larger general contractors working the Strip are the places to watch for hiring. Multi-year projects like this one offer something rare in construction, which is predictable work over a long stretch.

If you work in hospitality or you know someone who does, keep an eye out for pre-opening recruiting in 2027. Large resorts hire in waves and often fill thousands of positions in a matter of weeks. Getting in early on a brand new property usually means better shift selection and better long-term positioning than joining after opening.

If you own or are thinking about buying near the Strip corridor, look at the neighborhoods where Strip workers actually live rather than the Strip itself. Paradise, Winchester, Spring Valley, and parts of the east valley have older housing stock, short commutes, and rental demand that responds quickly when a big property staffs up. Run the numbers on rent versus purchase price before the hiring wave, not after.

If you rent in Paradise, Winchester, or Spring Valley, it is worth thinking about your lease timing now. Renters in those neighborhoods have real negotiating room in a soft market, and locking in a longer term at today's rate is one way to get ahead of any tightening that comes with a hiring wave later on.

And if you just want to watch it go up, the tower is visible from a large stretch of Las Vegas Boulevard and from the I-15. It is going to keep changing month to month between now and 2027.
