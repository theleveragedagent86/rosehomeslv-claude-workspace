What Comes Next for the Bally's Ballpark Site in Paradise

The next big decision on the land around the Athletics ballpark lands on September 16, 2026, when the Clark County Commission takes up permits for the future hotel casino. That is the piece everyone in Paradise has been waiting on, and it is the piece that still has one more hurdle after the county: the Federal Aviation Administration also has to sign off before anything tall goes up on that corner.

The first phase of land use approvals is already done. Commissioners cleared entitlements for 26 acres surrounding the ballpark site on August 19, 2026, according to NVBEX. That vote settled the supporting pieces of the district. It did not settle the resort itself.

We covered the approved package in Clark County Approves Parking, Retail, and a 2,500-Seat Theater on 26 Acres Around the A's Ballpark. This piece is about what happens from here, how the September 16 hearing works, and why the FAA review matters more than most people realize.

Where Things Actually Stand Right Now

Here is the plain version. Bally's owns the land where the Tropicana used to sit, on the corner of Las Vegas Boulevard and Tropicana Avenue in unincorporated Clark County. The Athletics ballpark is going up on part of that property. Bally's is building its own project on the rest of it. Those are two separate efforts on one shared site.

On August 19, 2026, the Clark County Commission approved the first phase of land use permissions for roughly 26 acres around the ballpark. NVBEX reported the package on August 26, 2026. The approved pieces include a mixed-use podium on the northwest corner, parking, and a 2,500-seat theater on the southwest corner.

That is meaningful, but it is also the easier half. Parking structures, retail podiums and theaters are all things Clark County has approved thousands of times. They fit inside existing zoning categories. They do not usually raise hard questions.

The hotel casino is different. A resort tower on the Strip needs its own use permits, its own design review, and its own height clearance. NVBEX reported that those permits are scheduled to go before the commission on September 16, 2026, and that the project also needs Federal Aviation Administration approval. Both of those are still open.

So if you drive past the corner today and wonder why the resort has not been announced with a name, a room count and an opening date, that is why. The paperwork that makes a resort legal is not finished. Phase one gave the district its bones. Phase two gives it the building everyone pictures when they hear "Bally's ballpark district."

Why the September 16 Hearing Matters to People Who Live Here

A Clark County Commission land use hearing sounds like something only lawyers care about. In practice, these meetings decide what your commute looks like, what your neighborhood sounds like at 11 p.m., and what your house is worth in five years.

Start with traffic. Paradise already absorbs Strip traffic, airport traffic, and University of Nevada, Las Vegas traffic in the same few square miles. Add a ballpark and a resort on the same block and the intersections at Tropicana Avenue, Las Vegas Boulevard and Koval Lane carry a lot more load on game days. The conditions attached to a use permit are where the county gets to require things like driveway placement, turn lanes, pedestrian bridges and traffic study updates. Those conditions are written at hearings like the one on September 16.

Then there is construction itself. A resort tower is a multi-year build with cranes, night pours, truck routes and lane closures. If you rent an apartment off Koval Lane or own a condo near Harmon Avenue, the difference between a well-conditioned permit and a loosely conditioned one is the difference between an annoying couple of years and a genuinely rough couple of years.

Jobs are the upside. Resort construction employs a lot of people, and a finished hotel casino employs a lot more on a permanent basis. Southern Nevada's economy still runs on hospitality payrolls. A new resort at one of the busiest corners on the Strip is a hiring event that ripples out to the apartment complexes and starter neighborhoods where those workers live, from Paradise to Winchester to the southeast valley.

And then there is the housing effect, which is slower and quieter. Big permanent employers change where people want to live. When a corner gets a ballpark, a theater and a resort, the rental buildings within a 15 minute drive start pricing differently. That does not happen the week a permit is approved. It happens over the years that follow.

The 2,500-seat theater from the phase one package deserves its own line of thinking here, because it is the piece that changes the corner on nights when there is no game. A ballpark fills a district in bursts tied to a schedule. A theater that size fills it on random Tuesdays. Venues in that seat range tend to book comedy, touring music and residency-style runs, which means steady evening foot traffic instead of 81 spikes a year. For nearby businesses and for anyone renting out a unit close by, steady beats spiky.

Parking is the other quiet piece with real neighborhood consequences. When a district does not build enough of it, the overflow goes somewhere, and in Paradise that somewhere is residential streets and apartment lots off Koval Lane and Harmon Avenue. Structured parking inside the district is what keeps game night out of your neighborhood. That is not a glamorous approval, but it is the one your street feels most directly.

The FAA Review Nobody Talks About

This is the part that surprises people. The Clark County Commission is not the last word on how tall a building near the Strip can be. The Federal Aviation Administration has a say too, and NVBEX specifically noted that the hotel casino needs FAA approval.

The reason is geography. Harry Reid International Airport sits directly southeast of the Tropicana corner. Planes approach and depart low over that part of the valley all day long. Federal rules require developers to file notice for structures that could affect navigable airspace, and the FAA then studies whether the proposed height creates a hazard to air navigation.

The FAA does not zone land and cannot stop a project by itself. What it issues is a determination. A building can be found to be no hazard, sometimes with conditions such as lighting and marking. Or it can be found to be a hazard, which in practice pushes a developer to redesign, usually by getting shorter.

That determination shapes everything downstream. Room count, floor count, silhouette, even the economics of the project all flow from how tall the tower is allowed to be. It is entirely normal for a Strip resort near the airport to end up shorter than the first sketch. Anyone telling you exactly how many floors this hotel will have before the FAA weighs in is guessing.

For residents, the practical takeaway is simple. Two clocks are running at the same time. The county clock has a date on it, September 16. The FAA clock does not, and federal reviews move at their own pace. A county approval on September 16 would be a real milestone, but it would not mean shovels the next morning.

How This Corner Got Here

The Tropicana opened in 1957 and spent more than six decades as one of the four corners at Tropicana Avenue and Las Vegas Boulevard. Bally's Corporation took over operations, the resort eventually closed, and the buildings came down to clear the site. That left one of the most valuable pieces of dirt in Nevada sitting mostly empty.

The Athletics deal is what filled it. The team's move from Oakland to Southern Nevada put a Major League Baseball ballpark on part of the Tropicana land, which changed the math for everything around it. Instead of redeveloping a resort corner in isolation, Bally's ended up with a corner anchored by a stadium that draws crowds on a schedule.

That is a different kind of real estate problem, and it explains the shape of the phase one package. A ballpark needs parking. A ballpark district needs somewhere for people to go before and after a game, which is what a mixed-use podium and a 2,500-seat theater are for. You build the supporting cast first because the supporting cast is what makes the resort worth building.

It also explains the sequencing. Entitlements for parking and a theater are lower risk and can move early. The resort waits, because the resort is the piece that depends on financing, on design, and on a federal height determination. Developers almost always take the certain approvals first.

Clark County has run this play before along the Strip corridor. Large parcels get entitled in phases over several years, with each phase locking in a little more certainty. Residents who have lived here a while know the pattern. Approvals come faster than buildings do.

There is one more piece of history worth knowing, and it is about who has authority here. This corner is in unincorporated Clark County, not inside the City of Las Vegas. People mix that up constantly. The Strip has never been in the city limits. That is why the commission, and not a city council, is the body deciding these permits, and it is why the county's zoning code and its resort corridor standards are the rules that apply.

It matters for a practical reason too. If you want to comment on this project, you need to be talking to the right government. Emails to the wrong body go nowhere. Your county commissioner represents you on this, and district boundaries are easy to look up on the Clark County website.

What to Watch Between Now and the End of the Year

The first thing to watch is the September 16, 2026 commission agenda. Clark County posts agendas ahead of meetings, and the staff report attached to an item usually tells you more than the news coverage does. It lists the specific permits requested, the staff recommendation, and the conditions being proposed. If you want to know what is actually being approved, that document is where it lives.

The second thing to watch is whether the item gets heard or held. Land use items get continued to a later date all the time, sometimes at the applicant's request, sometimes because a neighboring property owner raises an issue. A continuance is not a rejection. It is a delay, and delays on projects this size are ordinary.

The third thing to watch is the FAA side. Airspace determinations for proposed structures are public record, and they are searchable by location. If you are the kind of neighbor who wants to know how tall the tower will be before the renderings come out, that is the place to look.

Fourth, watch for building permits rather than land use approvals. Those are two different things. Entitlements say you are allowed to build a category of thing. Building permits say the county has reviewed your actual construction drawings. The gap between the two can be a year or more on a project like this.

Fifth, watch the pace of work on the ballpark itself. The stadium and the surrounding district are on linked timelines. Contractors, staging areas and street closures get shared across the site, and a schedule change on one side tends to move the other. If ballpark milestones slow down, it is reasonable to expect district milestones to follow.

Ryan's Take

I have watched a lot of Strip announcements come and go, and the honest read on this one is that the phase one approvals are more important than they sound and the September 16 hearing is less final than it sounds. Getting 26 acres entitled around a ballpark is the hard, unglamorous work that makes the rest possible. It also does not put a single room key on that corner.

What I tell clients who own near Paradise, Winchester and the southeast valley is to think in terms of five to ten years, not five to ten months. Districts like this reprice their surroundings gradually. Rental demand moves first because construction workers and hospitality staff need somewhere close to live. Owner-occupied demand follows later, once the noise is over and the amenities are open. If you are holding a rental within a short drive of that corner, time is probably working for you.

If you are thinking about buying nearby specifically because of the ballpark, go in with clear eyes. You are buying into a construction zone first and a finished district second. That can be a good trade, and it can also be a long stretch of dust and detours. The right answer depends on your timeline, not on the headline.

What You Can Do

If you want a say, show up or write in. Clark County Commission land use meetings are open to the public, and public comment is part of the record. You can find the agenda and meeting details through the Clark County website ahead of the September 16 date. Written comments submitted before a hearing get included even if you cannot attend in person.

If you live close enough to be affected by construction, get on the notification lists now rather than later. Neighborhood associations and the county's own alert tools are the fastest way to hear about lane closures and night work before they show up on your street. It is much easier to plan around a closure you knew about a week early.

If you own property in Paradise or the surrounding neighborhoods, this is a good moment to get a current read on your value. Districts under construction move in ways that automated home value estimates handle badly. A real comparable market analysis, done by someone who knows which streets are about to be affected and which are not, is worth far more than an online estimate right now.
