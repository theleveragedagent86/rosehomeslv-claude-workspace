CCSD Chronic Absenteeism Falls to 26.9 Percent as Suspensions Drop

Chronic absenteeism in the Clark County School District fell from 31.3 percent in 2023-24 to 26.9 percent in 2025-26, and suspensions dropped from 31,693 to 28,160 over the same two years. Trustees voted 7-0 on August 27, 2026 to approve the progress report that laid out those numbers.

The report covers the Safe and Supportive Schools priority inside the district's 2026-2031 Strategic Plan. It is the first public checkpoint on whether the district's attendance and discipline work is moving in the direction leaders said it would. On the headline measures, it is. The same report also shows that suspension rates are not spread evenly across student groups, and those gaps are wide.

What Happened at the August 27 Board Meeting

The Clark County School District Board of Trustees met on August 27, 2026. One item on the agenda was a progress report on the Safe and Supportive Schools priority, one of the pillars of the 2026-2031 Strategic Plan the district adopted for the next five years. Trustees approved the report by a 7-0 vote. That vote does not change policy on its own. It accepts the district's accounting of where the numbers stand.

The attendance figure is the one most parents will recognize. Chronic absenteeism in Clark County was 31.3 percent in the 2023-24 school year. In 2025-26, it was 26.9 percent. That is a drop of 4.4 percentage points, or roughly a 14 percent reduction from where the district started. The five year goal attached to this priority is a 40 percent reduction, so the district is somewhere around a third of the way to its own target with several years still on the clock.

Chronic absenteeism is generally defined nationally as a student missing 10 percent or more of enrolled school days, which works out to about 18 days in a standard 180 day year. [NOT VERIFIED] The board presentation's exact definition and the specific day threshold CCSD applied were not spelled out in the coverage of the meeting, so treat the 10 percent framing as the common national standard rather than a confirmed district definition.

On discipline, the district reported 31,693 suspensions in 2023-24 and 28,160 in 2025-26. That is 3,533 fewer suspensions, about an 11 percent decline. Those are counts of suspensions, not counts of individual students, so a single student suspended more than once shows up more than once in that total.

The report also broke suspensions down by student group. Black and African American students were 2.71 times more likely than average to be suspended. Students receiving special education services were 2.11 times more likely than average. Those are ratios against the district average, not against any one other group. The overall suspension count went down while those disparities were still present in the data. The district did not attach a cause to either figure in the material that was made public, and this article is not going to guess at one.

Finally, the district said a revised Pre-K through Grade 12 Code of Conduct is planned for the 2027-28 school year. That is the document that spells out what behaviors carry what consequences at each grade band. A rewrite of that document is the most concrete forward-looking item in the whole report.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Clark County School District is the fifth largest district in the country and serves close to 280,000 students. When a number like chronic absenteeism moves 4.4 points, that is tens of thousands of student attendance records shifting. It is not a rounding error and it is not a small pilot program. It touches almost every neighborhood in the valley.

Attendance is the quiet variable behind almost everything else in a school. A student who misses 18 or more days in a year is missing the equivalent of a full month of instruction. That shows up later in reading scores, in math proficiency, in graduation rates, and in how many kids need credit recovery to finish on time. Parents rarely see the absenteeism percentage on a school report card, but they feel it in how far behind a class is by spring.

Suspensions matter for a similar reason. A suspended student is out of the classroom, so a discipline number is also an instruction number. Fewer suspensions can mean fewer students losing days. It can also raise a fair question from teachers about whether behavior is being handled or just handled differently. Both readings are worth holding at once, and the district's own progress report does not settle that debate.

For families deciding where to live in the valley, school data is part of the calculation whether anyone says it out loud or not. Buyers ask about zoned schools constantly. Right now most of the publicly available comparison data is test scores and star ratings. Attendance and discipline data is arguably closer to what a parent actually wants to know, which is what a normal Tuesday feels like on that campus. This report is a step toward that information being public and trackable year over year.

There is also a practical household angle. Attendance problems in a big district are often transportation problems, work schedule problems, health problems and housing stability problems wearing a school uniform. Families who move mid-year, families juggling shift work on the Strip, and families in areas with long bus rides all run into the same friction. A district-wide improvement in attendance usually means at least some of that friction got easier somewhere.

Teachers feel this before anyone else does. A classroom where a rotating cast of students is absent every week is a harder room to teach in, because the teacher is re-teaching material for returning students while trying to move the rest of the class forward. Better attendance is one of the few changes that helps a teacher without adding anything to their plate. Staff retention in a district this size is its own long-running story, and classroom conditions are part of it.

Renters should pay attention to this too, not just buyers. A large share of valley households rent, and renting families tend to move more often than owning families do. Every mid-year move means a new school, a new bus route and a stretch of missed days while enrollment paperwork catches up. Attendance data at the district level captures some of that churn even though it never gets labeled as a housing issue.

Background and History

The 2023-24 school year is the baseline the district chose for this priority, and that choice matters. That year sat in the stretch after pandemic school closures when attendance habits across the country had not returned to where they were before. A 31.3 percent chronic absenteeism rate means roughly one in three students was missing enough school to qualify. Districts nationwide reported elevated numbers in the same window, so Clark County was not alone, though that is not much comfort to a parent whose kid was one of the three.

Safe and Supportive Schools is one of the priorities inside the 2026-2031 Strategic Plan, which is the five year framework CCSD is now operating under. Strategic plans in a district this size are how leadership sets measurable targets and how the public gets a scorecard. The 40 percent reduction goal on chronic absenteeism is an example of that. It is a specific number the district can be held to, and the August 27 report is the first meaningful public read on it.

The discipline side of this has a longer history. School districts across the country have spent more than a decade examining how suspension is used, who it lands on, and whether removing a student from class addresses the underlying behavior. Different districts have landed in different places. Some have tightened rules, some have loosened them, and many have layered in alternatives to out-of-school suspension. The disparity ratios CCSD published, 2.71 for Black and African American students and 2.11 for special education students, are the kind of figure districts now report as a matter of course.

Reporting those numbers publicly is itself a change from how discipline data was handled a generation ago, when a suspension count was an internal administrative figure and nothing more. Whatever a reader concludes about the numbers, they exist in a board packet now instead of a filing cabinet.

It is also worth understanding what a ratio like 2.71 does and does not tell you. It compares a group's suspension rate to the district average, so it describes how outcomes are distributed. It does not, on its own, explain why. Enrollment patterns, grade level, campus, the specific categories of behavior involved and the way rules get applied all sit underneath a single ratio, and none of that detail was published with the summary figure. That is a reason to take the number seriously and a reason not to stretch it past what it says.

The Code of Conduct rewrite planned for 2027-28 fits this history. That document was written to be applied across every grade from pre-kindergarten through twelfth, which is a wide range of ages to cover with one framework. Revisiting it is how a district converts findings like these into rules that principals and teachers actually apply on a given morning.

What Happens Next

The most concrete date on the calendar is the 2027-28 school year, when the revised Pre-K through Grade 12 Code of Conduct is planned to take effect. That timeline means the drafting and public comment work happens through the 2026-27 year. Historically, this is the stage where parent input has the most influence, because once a code of conduct is adopted it tends to stay put for years.

Between now and then, expect the Safe and Supportive Schools priority to come back to the board on a regular reporting cycle. The August 27 report was approved as a progress report, which by its nature implies more of them. Each one should carry an updated chronic absenteeism number and an updated suspension count, which is how the public will know whether the trend from 31.3 to 26.9 keeps going or flattens out. [NOT VERIFIED] The district did not publish a set schedule for the next progress report in the material covered.

Watch the disparity ratios in particular. Overall suspension counts and group-level ratios can move in opposite directions, so a lower district total in the next report will not automatically mean the 2.71 and 2.11 figures moved with it. Those two numbers are the ones that will show whether the changes reach every campus and every student group or only some of them.

Also worth watching is whether attendance gains hold. Absenteeism responds to things outside a district's control, including illness seasons, transportation changes and family economics. A single good year is a data point. Three consecutive years pointed the same direction is a trend, and only a trend gets a district to a 40 percent reduction.

One more thing to keep an eye on is how the suspension total is reported going forward. The figures released so far are counts of suspensions rather than counts of students. Both are useful, and they answer different questions. A count of students tells you how many kids were affected. A count of suspensions tells you how much class time was lost. If future reports carry both, the picture gets a lot clearer for parents trying to read it.

Ryan's Take

I sell homes in this valley, and schools come up in nearly every conversation with a family. What buyers actually ask me is not "what is the star rating." They ask whether the school is calm, whether their kid will be safe walking in, and whether teachers stay. Attendance and discipline data gets closer to those questions than a test score does, so I am glad it is public and I hope the district keeps publishing it the same way every year. Consistency is what makes a number useful. A figure you can compare across five years beats a better looking figure you can only see once.

On the housing side, I would be careful about reading too much into a single district-wide statistic when you are shopping a specific neighborhood. CCSD covers Summerlin and Sunrise Manor and Henderson and Mountain's Edge and everything between. A district average is not a description of any one campus. If a school matters to your purchase, tour it, walk the drop-off line at 7:30 in the morning, and ask the front office how many teachers came back this year. That fifteen minutes will tell you more about the building than any spreadsheet. Use the district numbers for direction and the campus visit for the decision.

What You Can Do

Start with your own school. CCSD board meetings are public, agendas and supporting documents are posted ahead of time, and public comment is part of the process. If you want to see the Safe and Supportive Schools material yourself rather than reading a summary of it, the board packet is the place to look. That is also where the next progress report will land when it comes.

If attendance is the piece that concerns you, talk to your school directly. Ask what the campus attendance rate is, what triggers an outreach call home, and what supports exist for a family that is struggling to get a kid to school on time. Schools generally have more tools here than parents realize, including help with transportation issues and connections to health services, but those tools do not activate until somebody asks.

If discipline is the piece that concerns you, the Code of Conduct rewrite scheduled for 2027-28 is the moment that matters most. Watch for public input opportunities during the 2026-27 school year. Attend a School Organizational Team meeting at your campus, since those are the smallest and most accessible venue for a parent voice, and they meet on a regular schedule during the year. If you have a student receiving special education services, the disparity figure in this report is a reasonable thing to raise directly with your school's administration and with your student's team.
