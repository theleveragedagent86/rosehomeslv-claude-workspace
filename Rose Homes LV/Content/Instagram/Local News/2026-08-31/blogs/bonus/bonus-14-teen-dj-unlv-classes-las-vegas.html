A 13-Year-Old Las Vegas DJ Is Taking Classes at UNLV

A 13-year-old Las Vegas musician who performs as DRGN is taking classes at the University of Nevada, Las Vegas while he builds a career behind the decks. The Las Vegas Review-Journal reported on August 22, 2026 that Jeremiah Klein recently played a family rave at The Portal at Area15, and that he hopes his university coursework helps him break into the music industry.

It is the kind of local story that makes people stop scrolling. A kid who is not old enough to drive is sitting in a college classroom and also getting booked for real shows in a city that takes its music seriously. Good for him. But the reason this story is worth more than a quick smile is what it points to. Clark County families have more early college options than most of them realize, and almost none of those options require you to be a headliner at Area15.

This article covers what the Review-Journal reported, and then it goes wider. If you are a parent in Summerlin or Henderson or the northwest valley wondering whether your kid could start college credit early, there is a real answer to that question, and it is more accessible than most people assume.

What Happened

The Review-Journal published its story on August 22, 2026 under the headline "A Las Vegas DJ is taking classes at UNLV. He's only 13." The piece profiles Jeremiah Klein, a 13-year-old who performs under the name DRGN. He is enrolled in coursework at UNLV while pursuing a music career at the same time.

The most recent performance the paper highlighted was a family rave at The Portal at Area15. The Portal is one of the immersive venues inside the Area15 entertainment complex just west of the Strip, off Rancho Drive near Interstate 15. Family rave events are exactly what they sound like. They are all-ages dance shows built for kids and parents to attend together, usually earlier in the evening, with no alcohol focus and a lot of lights.

According to the Review-Journal, Klein said he hopes the university classes help him break into the industry. That is a notably practical way to think about school for someone who is 13. He is not treating college as something that happens after music. He is treating it as part of how music happens.

Area15 itself has become a regular stop for this kind of programming. The complex opened in 2020 near Rancho Drive and Interstate 15, and it has leaned into experiences that work for mixed-age crowds rather than adults only. The Portal, with its wraparound projection surfaces, is one of the spaces inside it that gets used for concerts, watch parties and immersive events. Booking a young local artist into a family show there is a good fit for both the room and the audience.

That is the whole of what the source reported that belongs in a public article about a minor. We are not going to add details about his family, his home, his campus schedule or where he goes to school. He is a kid. The story is his accomplishment, not his address.

What we can do is take the part of the story that applies to thousands of other Clark County households and unpack it. A teenager taking college classes is not a one-off miracle here. Nevada has built a set of formal pathways for it, and Southern Nevada in particular has expanded them quickly over the last five years.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

For most valley families, the practical takeaway is money and time. College credit earned in high school is credit you do not pay full university price for later. Nevada law lets district students enroll in college-level courses that count toward both high school graduation and college graduation at the same time. Those authorizations live in NRS 389.160, NRS 389.300 and NRS 389.310.

That double counting is the point. A student who finishes high school with 12 or 24 or 30 transferable credits has effectively skipped a semester or a full year of tuition, fees, books and living costs. In a valley where the median existing single-family home sold for $480,000 in July 2026 and household budgets are already stretched, shaving a year off a college bill is not a small thing.

There is a second benefit that parents underrate. Early college coursework teaches a kid what a college workload actually feels like while they still have a support system at home. Deadlines are firmer. Nobody chases you about missing assignments. Office hours exist but you have to go. Students who learn that at 16 in one class tend to handle 15 credits at 18 much better than students who learn it all at once during freshman year.

And then there is the piece that fits DRGN's story specifically. Not every talented kid is on a straight academic track. Some of them are musicians, athletes, coders or entrepreneurs who need school to bend around a thing they are already doing well. Flexible early college structures are one of the few ways a school system can say yes to that kid without asking them to quit the thing they love.

Clark County has a lot of those kids. This is a city where the entertainment industry is the local economy, not a distant dream. A 13-year-old DJ in Las Vegas is not chasing something abstract. The venues are here, the residencies are here, the production and lighting and audio jobs are here. A pathway that lets a young performer build credentials while building a name has more real-world value in Southern Nevada than it would almost anywhere else.

Renters feel this too. A family leasing in Spring Valley or Aliante has more freedom to move than an owner does, and school access is one of the top reasons renters relocate inside the valley. Knowing that a college pathway may already run through the local campus, or may be available online regardless of address, takes some of the pressure off that decision.

Background and History

Early college in Nevada is not new, but it looked very different fifteen years ago. For a long time the main option was the College of Southern Nevada, which still runs the largest high school pipeline in the region. CSN dual enrollment is generally built for students age 16 and older who take college courses at CSN alongside their high school load.

CSN also runs Jumpstart, a concurrent enrollment program with CCSD schools plus charter and private high schools. In that model the college course happens on the high school campus during the regular school day, taught by a high school teacher who has been certified by the matching CSN academic department. It is a lower-friction version of the same idea, because the student never has to leave campus or arrange a commute.

The financial structure matters here too. CCSD covers tuition and fees for up to twelve approved CSN credits per semester, with students responsible for costs above that ceiling. Twelve credits a semester is a substantial subsidy. Families who assume early college is a luxury item are often surprised by how much of it the district already pays for.

UNLV built its own version more recently. The Rebel College Accelerator lets students earn high school and college credit at the same time, either through high school partnerships where the course runs during the regular school day, or through campus-based dual enrollment where UNLV faculty teach the class on campus or online. UNLV has reported partnering with 17 high schools, working with close to 80 instructors, and offering more than 30 courses. In its fifth year the program had helped more than 1,800 Southern Nevada students graduate with college credit already banked.

Both systems grew out of the same pressure. Nevada spent years near the bottom of national college-going and college-completion rankings, and early credit is one of the more reliable levers a state has to move those numbers. A student who arrives on campus already carrying credit is a student who is statistically more likely to finish. The programs also give high schools a way to serve advanced students without building an entire separate honors infrastructure at every campus.

Placement rules still apply, especially in math and English, where students typically need qualifying ACT or SAT scores or a college placement test result. Those gates exist to make sure a student is set up to pass rather than to fail publicly in a course that follows them on a permanent transcript.

What Happens Next

For DRGN, the next chapter is his to write, and the honest answer is that nobody outside his circle knows what it looks like. He is 13. He has coursework and he has bookings. That is a full plate for anyone.

For families reading this and wondering how to get their own student into a similar lane, the calendar is the thing to watch. Dual and concurrent enrollment run on college registration deadlines, not on school district ones, and those deadlines usually fall well before the semester starts. Spring registration conversations generally need to happen in the fall, not in January. Counselors at comprehensive high schools handle a lot of these applications, and the college and career readiness office at CCSD maintains dual enrollment guidance for students and parents.

The other thing to watch is transferability. A credit is only worth what a receiving institution will accept. Courses taken through NSHE institutions like CSN, UNLV, Nevada State University and UNR generally move cleanly inside the Nevada system. If a student is aiming at an out-of-state school, that is a question to ask the target school directly and early, before committing a semester to a course that may not carry over.

Grades are the third thing to watch, and this one catches families off guard. A dual enrollment course produces a permanent college transcript. A rough semester at 16 can follow a student into scholarship applications and graduate school admissions years later. That is not a reason to avoid the programs. It is a reason to start with one course rather than four, and to pick a subject the student is already strong in.

Nevada has also kept dual enrollment on the policy agenda. The Legislature's interim education committee has studied it, and the state Department of Education has convened a dual enrollment task force. That means program rules, subsidies and eligibility ages can change between school years. Verify the current terms with the college each year rather than assuming last year's rules still hold.

Ryan's Take

I sell houses for a living, so let me tell you what this story looks like from my chair. Families move across this valley for schools constantly. I have watched buyers pay a premium to land inside a specific attendance zone, and I have watched sellers price a home confidently because of the campus down the street. School quality is one of the most powerful forces in Clark County real estate, and it is not close.

What stories like this one do is widen the definition of a good school situation. A family that only evaluates a neighborhood by the rating on the zoned high school is missing half the picture. If a student can take UNLV or CSN courses during the school day, or online, or on a campus fifteen minutes away, the zoned school stops being the whole ceiling. That changes which neighborhoods are actually viable, and it usually opens up more affordable options than the ones people fixate on.

I also think there is something specifically Las Vegas about a 13-year-old DJ enrolling in college. This city rewards people who build a skill early and keep showing up. Half my clients came here to work in an industry that does not exist at scale anywhere else. Seeing a kid pair a stage with a syllabus feels exactly right for this town, and I hope he keeps both.

What You Can Do

If you have a student who might be ready for college coursework, start with the school counselor. Ask specifically about dual enrollment, concurrent enrollment and the Rebel College Accelerator, and ask which courses your campus already offers during the school day. Campus-based offerings are the easiest entry point because there is no extra commute and no separate schedule to juggle.

Next, go to the source. The CSN high school and early college pages list eligibility, placement requirements and program options, and UNLV's Early Outreach and Rebel College Accelerator pages do the same on the university side. Confirm ages, fees, deadlines and credit caps directly with the institution, because those details shift from year to year and a counselor's notes are not always current.

If your student is more DRGN than valedictorian, do not count them out of this. Ask about single-course enrollment, online sections and summer terms. One class is a legitimate starting point. It builds a transcript, it tests whether the workload fits, and it does not require reorganizing an entire school year around a maybe.

It is also worth supporting the venues that make room for young performers. All-ages and family-friendly shows at places like Area15 give kids a stage before they are old enough for the club circuit, and those bookings only keep happening if people show up to them. If you have children who love music, those events are a cheap night out and a look at what a working stage actually requires.

And if you are house hunting in Clark County right now, factor early college access into how you evaluate an area, not just the zoned campus rating. Proximity to a CSN campus, a partner high school or UNLV itself is a practical amenity for a family with a motivated teenager.
