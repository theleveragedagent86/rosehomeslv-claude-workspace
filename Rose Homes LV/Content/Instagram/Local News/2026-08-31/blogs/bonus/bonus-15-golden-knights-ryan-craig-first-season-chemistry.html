Ryan Craig's First Season Behind the Golden Knights Bench

The Vegas Golden Knights are heading into their 10th NHL season with a new head coach, and the two words the team keeps coming back to are togetherness and consistency. An August 24, 2026 report covered the team leaning on chemistry as it prepares for the year ahead under Ryan Craig, who was promoted to head coach three days after Vegas lost Game 6 of the 2026 Stanley Cup Final to the Carolina Hurricanes.

That is an unusual way to start a coaching era. Most teams change coaches after a bad year. Vegas changed after one of the best runs in franchise history ended one win short. So the story here is not a rebuild. It is a handoff, and the man taking the clipboard has been part of this organization since the very beginning.

For Las Vegas fans, that matters. The Golden Knights are not just a team here. They are a civic identity that arrived before the Raiders, before the Aces titles, before the Athletics broke ground. How this season goes shapes fall and winter for a lot of families across the valley.

What Happened

On August 24, 2026, a report syndicated through Gray Media and carried by WVVA covered the Golden Knights as they turn toward a new season. The theme of the piece was simple. The team is leaning on togetherness and consistency heading into the year. Those are the words the organization is using as it opens its 10th season in the National Hockey League.

The biggest change is behind the bench. Ryan Craig is the head coach. He was promoted three days after Vegas lost Game 6 of the 2026 Stanley Cup Final to the Carolina Hurricanes. Three days. That is a fast turn from the end of a championship series to naming the next voice in the room, and it tells you the club already knew who it wanted.

Craig is not an outside hire. He has been with the Golden Knights organization since the start. That includes six seasons as an assistant coach at the NHL level and three seasons as a head coach in Henderson. He has stood on the bench for the highs, and he has run his own room in the American Hockey League, which is where a lot of NHL head coaches learn how to actually manage a group day to day.

Three seasons running a bench in Henderson is the part a lot of fans skip past, and it should not be skipped. The American Hockey League is where an organization figures out whether a coach can actually coach, not just draw up a forecheck. Rosters change constantly. Players get called up and sent down. A coach has to keep a group focused while the group itself keeps changing shape. Craig did that job for three years inside this same organization.

Put those pieces together and you get the reason the team is talking about consistency. A coach who has been in the building for the entire history of the franchise does not have to install a brand new culture. He already knows the systems, the staff, the training habits, and most of the players. The learning curve is short because there barely is one.

The togetherness part is the other half. Coming off a Cup Final loss is its own kind of test. A group that gets that close and falls short can either splinter or tighten up. The message coming out of the organization in late August is that Vegas is trying to tighten up. Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Hockey in Las Vegas is not a side interest. It is a season-long routine for tens of thousands of households. Game nights at T-Mobile Arena set traffic patterns on the south Strip. They fill restaurants in Park MGM and New York-New York. They decide whether a Tuesday in January feels busy or quiet for service workers who count on those crowds.

A coaching change at the top of that operation ripples outward in small ways. Ticket demand, watch party attendance, jersey sales, and youth signups all move a little with how the team is playing. When the Golden Knights are winning, sheets of ice across the valley get busier. When the team is flat, the energy dips. That is just how a hockey market works, and Vegas has become a real one in less than a decade.

There is also a neighborhood angle. City National Arena in Summerlin is the team practice facility, and it has been a magnet since the day it opened. Families drive across the valley to watch morning skates for free. Kids take learn-to-skate lessons on the same ice. Homes near that corner of Summerlin have marketed proximity to the rink for years, and buyers ask about it more often than people outside the market would guess.

For renters and homeowners on the south end of the valley, the practical impact is simpler. More home dates mean more evening traffic on Tropicana, Frank Sinatra Drive, and the Harmon area. If you live in the Southwest, in the Strip-adjacent condo towers, or in Paradise, you already plan around it. A deep season means more of those nights, and a short one means fewer.

The bigger point is stability. Sports franchises anchor a city's calendar. When a team keeps its identity through a coaching change instead of blowing everything up, the community gets to keep its routines too. That is not a small thing in a town that grew as fast as this one did.

Think about how much of a Las Vegas fall is built around this team. Kids wear the jersey to school. Bars in Henderson and the northwest put the game on every screen. Neighbors who would never talk about politics will absolutely talk about a power play. A shared team gives a fast-growing, transplant-heavy city a common language, and that is worth something in a place where a huge share of residents did not grow up here.

Background and History

The Golden Knights entered the NHL as an expansion team and immediately became one of the most talked-about clubs in the league. Vegas did not follow the usual expansion script of losing for years first. The team was competitive right away, and the fan base formed almost overnight. That early success set the expectation that has followed the franchise ever since.

Ryan Craig's path is tied to all of it. He joined the organization at the start and stayed. Six seasons as an NHL assistant coach means he was in the room for a long stretch of that history, learning how this specific front office and this specific market operate. That is a rare résumé in a league where coaching staffs turn over constantly.

The three seasons as head coach in Henderson matter just as much. Henderson is where the organization develops players, and running that bench means handling young pros who are trying to earn a call-up. A coach in that job learns how to teach, how to hold a young player accountable, and how to keep a room together when the roster changes week to week. Those are the exact skills the Golden Knights are asking for now.

Then there is the 2026 Stanley Cup Final. Vegas lost Game 6 to the Carolina Hurricanes. Ending a season in the Final is both the best and the hardest way to finish. The team was two periods of hockey away from a parade, and instead the offseason started. Three days later, the organization named Craig its head coach. That timeline is the whole story in one line. Vegas did not want a reset. It wanted the next chapter written by someone who was already there for the first nine.

What Happens Next

The next milestones are the ones every hockey season runs through. Training camp opens, the club works through preseason games, and then the regular season starts. Vegas will do most of its on-ice preparation at City National Arena in Summerlin before the schedule shifts to T-Mobile Arena for game nights. Exact camp dates and the opening night matchup were not included in the August 24 report, so check the team's official schedule for those. [NOT VERIFIED]

What is worth watching is whether the togetherness talk holds up when the schedule gets hard. Every team says the right things in August. The real test comes in a stretch of road games in December, or after a three-game losing streak, when a first-year head coach has to decide how much to change. Craig's advantage is that he already knows the personalities. He does not need half a season to figure out who responds to what.

The other thing to watch is how the Cup Final loss shows up. Some teams come back from that with an edge and use it as fuel. Others carry it as weight. There is no way to predict which version Vegas gets until the games count. What we do know is that the organization chose continuity, and continuity is usually the choice a team makes when it believes the group is close.

One more thing worth tracking is the Henderson connection. Craig spent three seasons running the bench down there, which means a number of players who have come through that pipeline already know his voice, his standards, and what he expects in a defensive-zone read. For young players trying to stick in the NHL, that familiarity can shorten the adjustment. For fans, it makes the Henderson roster more interesting to follow, because the two benches are now linked by a coach who has stood behind both.

Finally, watch the calendar around the arena district. Home dates drive a lot of activity on the south Strip, and the pattern of the schedule shapes how busy the Tropicana and Harmon corridors feel week to week. Once the full schedule is public, it is worth a look if you live nearby or work in that area.

Ryan's Take

I have watched this franchise change how people feel about living here. When the Golden Knights arrived, Las Vegas was still a city that people described by what visitors did on the Strip. The team gave locals something that was ours, on a weeknight, in a building we drive to. Almost 10 years in, that is still true, and it still shows up in conversations with buyers.

Here is where it touches real estate in a real way. Buyers moving here from Minnesota, Michigan, upstate New York, and Alberta ask about ice time within the first few showings. They want to know how far their kid is from a rink, whether the youth programs are any good, and how bad the drive is from Summerlin or Henderson to the closest sheet. A stable, winning NHL club feeds all of that. It keeps rinks busy, keeps programs funded, and keeps the sport visible for a generation of Vegas kids who have never known a version of this city without a team.

A homegrown head coach reinforces that story. It says the organization builds people, not just rosters. If you are deciding between neighborhoods and hockey is part of your family's week, that stability is a fair thing to factor in alongside schools, commute, and price per square foot.

What You Can Do

If you want to follow the season closely, start with the team's official channels for schedule releases, camp dates, and roster news. Those are the only places where dates get confirmed, and they update before secondhand reports do. Local outlets covering the club will carry the day-to-day details once camp opens.

If you want to see the team without buying a ticket, City National Arena in Summerlin has long been the spot. Public practice viewing has been part of the experience there since it opened, and it is one of the easiest free family outings in the valley. Confirm the current viewing policy and hours with the facility before you drive out, because practice schedules move constantly during the season. [NOT VERIFIED]

If you have a kid who wants to play, this is a good time of year to look. Youth programs and learn-to-skate sessions run out of rinks across the valley, including in Summerlin and Henderson. Call the rink directly, ask what age groups still have room, and ask what the equipment cost looks like before you commit. Hockey is not the cheapest sport to start, but most local programs have loaner gear for beginners, and it is worth asking about.

If you are an adult who has never played, the valley has beginner and adult league options too. Plenty of people who moved here from cold-weather states pick the game back up in their thirties and forties, and a few who never touched a puck growing up start from zero. It is one of the faster ways to meet people in a city where a lot of neighbors are also new. Ask the rink what nights the adult sessions run and whether there is a no-checking division.

It also helps to know the geography of ice in Clark County before you commit to a program. Rinks are spread out, and the drive from Summerlin to Henderson at 6 a.m. on a Saturday is a very different life than a 10-minute hop. Map the actual practice location, not just the league name, and then decide.

And if you are new to Las Vegas and trying to figure out where to land, think about the whole week, not just the house. Where the rink is, where the school is, how long the drive to T-Mobile Arena takes on a game night. Those small things add up to whether a neighborhood actually fits your life.
