The Northwest Valley Ice Arena Timeline: How Five Acres at Hualapai and Deer Springs Got to Late August With No Builder

The city of Las Vegas has spent most of 2026 trying to find a developer for a two-sheet ice arena on five acres of city-owned land at North Hualapai Way and West Deer Springs Way, and as of late August 2026 no developer had been publicly selected. The first proposal arrived in March. The deadline was pushed to June 15. Then the calendar kept moving and the site stayed empty.

This piece is not a rehash of the news. We covered the original decision and the search itself in Las Vegas Is Still Picking a Builder for a Two-Sheet Ice Arena in the Northwest Valley. What follows is the timeline, step by step, with the dates lined up in order, plus a clear look at what another year without a rink actually costs families in Centennial Hills and Skye Canyon. If you have been trying to figure out why a project that sounds simple has taken this long, the sequence explains a lot.

The Timeline, Step by Step

Start with the land. The city of Las Vegas owns roughly five acres at North Hualapai Way and West Deer Springs Way in the northwest valley. That is the site at the center of everything here. The city did not propose to build an arena itself. It opened a request for proposals, often shortened to RFP, asking private developers to come forward with a plan to build and open a two-sheet ice facility on that ground.

That is an important distinction and it shapes the whole timeline. A city-built project moves on a city schedule with city money and a city construction calendar. An RFP moves at the speed of whoever answers it. The city sets the terms and waits. If the terms do not pencil out for a builder, nothing happens, and there is no press conference announcing that nothing happened.

In March 2026, the city received its first proposal. One. Not a stack of competing bids, not a shortlist. A single response was on the table at that point in the process. That fact alone tells you the city was not in a position to run a quick comparison and pick a winner.

The city then extended the RFP deadline to June 15, 2026. Extensions like this are common and they usually mean one of two things. Either the city wanted more competing proposals so it could negotiate from a stronger position, or the terms needed clarifying so more builders felt comfortable responding. Either way, an extension is a signal that the first round did not produce enough to move forward on.

June 15 came and went. Then July. Then August. As of late August 2026, no developer selection had been publicly announced. The Las Vegas Review-Journal reported on the search in an article published April 6, 2026, and the project has carried March and June updates since. What has not arrived is the announcement that a builder is on board.

So the honest state of play, laid out in order, looks like this. City land identified. RFP opened. One proposal received in March. Deadline extended to June 15. No public selection through late August. That is five steps across roughly six months, and step five is still a blank.

It is worth saying plainly what has not happened, because in a project like this, the absence of news gets misread constantly. No developer has been selected. No groundbreaking date exists. No construction timeline has been published. Anyone telling you a specific rink opening date for this site is guessing.

One more thing about the March proposal deserves attention. A single response to a public RFP is not automatically a bad proposal. It can simply mean the pool of firms that build ice facilities is small, which it is. There are only so many developers in the country with real experience putting up a two-sheet arena and running it profitably. When your bidder pool is that narrow to begin with, one response might be most of the market, and extending the deadline may not add much.

That is the quiet tension inside this timeline. The city wants competition to get the best terms for public land. The specialty of the building limits how much competition is available. Those two facts pull against each other, and the calendar is what gives.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Here is the part that actually touches daily life. Families in Centennial Hills and Skye Canyon currently drive across the valley for ice time. Not across the neighborhood. Across the valley. That is the practical problem this project was meant to solve, and every month the site sits empty is another month those drives continue.

Think about what a normal week looks like for a hockey family in the northwest. Practices often land early in the morning or late at night, because ice is scarce and the good slots go fast. If the nearest sheet is a long drive away, a 6 a.m. practice means leaving the house well before 5 a.m. A 9 p.m. practice means a kid gets home near 11 on a school night. Multiply that by two or three sessions a week, then add weekend games, and you understand why some families quit before the season ends.

It is not only hockey. A two-sheet facility serves figure skating, learn-to-skate programs, public skate sessions, birthday parties, adult leagues, and drop-in stick time. Those are the ordinary uses that make a rink a neighborhood amenity instead of a specialty sports building. Right now, none of that exists within easy reach for the far northwest.

There is a cost angle too. Long drives mean gas, wear on the car, and hours a parent cannot spend working or with other kids. Scarce ice tends to mean expensive ice, because programs pay premium rates for whatever slots they can lock down and those costs land on families. A new sheet does not automatically make hockey cheap, but adding supply in a market this tight tends to relieve pressure rather than add to it.

And then there is the housing angle, which is where I pay close attention. Buyers moving to Centennial Hills and Skye Canyon ask about amenities constantly. Parks, trails, schools, community centers, and yes, recreation facilities. A rink at Hualapai and Deer Springs would be a genuine draw for a specific and growing slice of buyers. Its absence is not a dealbreaker for most people, but it is a gap those neighborhoods still carry.

There is also a simple time math problem that families in the northwest live with. A round trip across the valley in traffic can eat two hours before you count the practice itself. Do that three times a week and you have spent roughly six hours in a car for a sport your kid plays for maybe four hours. Nobody sustains that for many seasons. Programs in the far northwest lose players to that math every year, and it has nothing to do with how much the kids love the game.

Background and History: Why Demand Kept Climbing

Southern Nevada was not a hockey market a decade ago. It is now, and the change happened fast enough that the buildings did not keep up with the people.

The Vegas Golden Knights are the obvious driver. An NHL team in the valley created a generation of kids who grew up watching hockey as a local sport rather than something on television from a colder city. Interest turned into learn-to-skate signups, which turned into house leagues, which turned into travel teams. Each of those steps needs ice, and ice is the one thing you cannot improvise.

City officials also pointed to the 2026 Winter Olympics as a demand driver. The United States men's and women's teams both won gold in hockey at those Games. A gold medal in a sport your city already loves is a signup surge waiting to happen. Rinks across the country see it after big international wins, and Las Vegas is no exception.

The trouble is that ice sheets are expensive, specialized buildings. They need refrigeration plants, dehumidification, tall clear-span roofs, locker rooms, and parking. They are not a warehouse with a floor poured in it. That construction reality is exactly why the city went looking for a private developer instead of writing the check itself, and it is also why the response has been thin. A builder has to see a path to operating revenue that covers a heavy upfront cost.

Geography made it worse in the northwest specifically. The far northwest, Centennial Hills, Skye Canyon, and the surrounding master plans, grew explosively over the last fifteen years. Rooftops arrived faster than recreation infrastructure, which is a familiar pattern in Las Vegas. Homes come first because homes are what the market builds fastest. Amenities catch up later, sometimes much later.

What Happens Next

The next real milestone is a public announcement that the city has selected a developer, or a decision to restructure and reissue the RFP. Those are the two live paths. Until one of them happens, the project stays exactly where it is.

If a developer is selected, the steps after that are predictable even if the dates are not. There would be a negotiated agreement covering the land, then design, then permitting and plan review, then construction. On a specialized building like an ice facility, that sequence is measured in years, not months. So even a selection announced tomorrow would not put skates on that ice this year.

If instead the city reworks the RFP, watch for changes to the terms. A longer land lease, a different revenue split, an operating partner attached, or a change in what the city requires the facility to include would all be signs the city is trying to make the numbers work for a builder. Those changes would be the tell that the first version simply asked for more than the market would give.

The place to watch is the city of Las Vegas. Council agendas and city procurement postings are where this will surface first, usually before any news coverage. If you want to know the moment something moves, that is the source, not social media chatter. I would also expect local hockey organizations to amplify any real news quickly, because their families have the most riding on it.

There is a third possibility worth naming, which is that the city decides the site is better used for something else entirely. That is not what officials have signaled, and nothing suggests it is on the table right now. But five acres of city land in a growing part of the valley is a valuable asset, and cities do reassess when a plan stalls. I mention it only so nobody treats the rink as inevitable. It is a proposal, not a promise.

Ryan's Take

I have watched the northwest valley grow for years, and this is the classic Las Vegas sequence playing out again. Homes go up quickly because builders can build homes quickly. The amenities that make a neighborhood feel finished arrive on a slower clock, and some of them stall out in exactly this kind of limbo where the land is ready and the money is not.

What I would tell a buyer looking at Centennial Hills or Skye Canyon right now is simple. Do not price this rink into your decision. A parcel with an open RFP is not a project with a shovel in the ground. If a two-sheet arena eventually opens at Hualapai and Deer Springs, it will be a genuine plus for those neighborhoods and for the families already there. But buy the home, the street, the schools, and the commute you have today. Treat the rink as upside if it comes.

The flip side is that the demand is real and it is not going away. A valley with an NHL team, a growing youth hockey base, and a fresh Olympic bump is going to keep pushing for ice. That pressure does not disappear because one RFP went quiet. It usually just finds a different building, a different site, or a different developer. I would be surprised if the northwest goes another five years without a sheet somewhere.

One more practical note for sellers in those neighborhoods. If you are listing a home in Centennial Hills or Skye Canyon, do not market a rink that does not exist. I have seen agents lean on rumored amenities and it creates problems later when a buyer finds out the truth. Sell what is there now. There is plenty in those communities to talk about without borrowing from a project that has no builder attached.

What You Can Do

If this project matters to your family, the most useful thing you can do is pay attention to the city of Las Vegas directly. City Council meeting agendas are published in advance and are open to the public. Procurement and RFP status updates come through the city as well. Watching those is how you learn about a selection before it becomes a headline, and it is also how you get the chance to comment while a decision is still being made.

Public comment carries more weight on projects like this than people assume. When elected officials hear from a room full of parents who drive across the valley three nights a week, that becomes part of the record. Reaching out to your ward's council office with a short, specific note about your family's ice time situation is a low-effort, high-value step.

In the meantime, the practical move is to plan around the rinks that exist today. Register early for programs, because the best slots at existing facilities go quickly and the fall calendar fills up fast. Carpooling with other families in your neighborhood cuts the driving burden significantly, and most youth programs have parents already organizing exactly that. Confirm schedules and fees directly with each rink, since program details change season to season.
