A 313-Room Casino Hotel Is Proposed Right Next to an Enterprise Neighborhood

A developer wants to build a 313-room, six-story hotel casino at Las Vegas Boulevard and Ford Avenue in Enterprise, and the property sits right next to a single-family neighborhood. The Clark County Zoning Commission is scheduled to take it up on September 2, 2026, after the item was postponed from the August 19 agenda.

The plan comes from Mary Barstas 7, LLC. It calls for 274,058 square feet of building, gaming space, restaurants, retail, a pool, and a three-story parking garage with 428 spaces. To make it work, the developer needs Clark County to sign off on a special use permit that expands the Gaming Enterprise District to cover the site.

That last part is the piece most people miss. This is not just a hotel getting approved. It is a change to the map that decides where casinos are legally allowed to exist in Clark County. Once a parcel is inside that district, the rules for what can go there change for good.

What Happened

Mary Barstas 7, LLC filed plans with Clark County for a hotel casino at the corner of Las Vegas Boulevard and Ford Avenue. The building would rise six stories and total 274,058 square feet. Inside, the plans show 313 hotel rooms, a casino floor, multiple restaurants, retail space, and a pool deck. Parking would be handled by a separate three-story garage with 428 spaces.

The item was originally set for the Clark County Zoning Commission agenda on August 19, 2026. It was postponed. It is now scheduled for the September 2, 2026 meeting. Postponements like this are common in Clark County land use cases. They happen when a developer needs more time to answer staff questions, when neighbors ask for more notice, or when the applicant wants to meet with residents before the vote.

The approval the developer is chasing is a special use permit tied to the Gaming Enterprise District. Nevada does not let casinos open anywhere someone wants to put one. Non-restricted gaming, meaning a full casino floor rather than a handful of slot machines in a bar or grocery store, is limited to specific mapped areas. Adding a parcel to that district requires a public hearing and a vote.

The site sits next to a single-family residential neighborhood. That is the detail that turns a routine zoning item into a packed hearing. A six-story building with a casino floor, restaurant deliveries, and a garage full of cars is a very different neighbor than an empty lot or a strip of small commercial buildings. Residents who bought homes there are now looking at plans for something much taller and much busier on the other side of their block wall.

The numbers themselves tell you a lot about the scale. A 274,058-square-foot building with 313 rooms is not a boutique property, but it is also not a Strip megaresort. For comparison, most Strip towers carry two to four thousand rooms. This is closer in size to the locals casinos scattered around the valley, the kind that draw from a few zip codes rather than from the airport. The 428-space garage is sized for that, and it tells you the developer expects most guests and visitors to arrive by car.

The pool, restaurants, and retail space are the pieces that create day-to-day activity. Those are also the pieces neighbors tend to ask about first, because a pool deck with music and a restaurant taking deliveries at five in the morning are what you actually hear from a backyard. Site plan details like where the loading dock sits and which direction the pool faces end up mattering more to nearby homeowners than the total square footage does.

NVBEX reported the proposal on August 28, 2026. As of that reporting, the county had not voted. Nothing is approved yet. The September 2 hearing is the next formal step, and it is open to the public.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Enterprise is one of the fastest growing parts of the Las Vegas Valley. It is unincorporated Clark County, which means it does not have its own city council. Land use decisions get made by the Clark County Commission and the Clark County Zoning Commission. If you live in Enterprise and you want a say in what gets built near you, the county meeting downtown is the room where it happens.

For homeowners closest to the site, the immediate concerns are the usual ones. Traffic on Las Vegas Boulevard and Ford Avenue. Light spill from a six-story building and a parking structure. Noise from deliveries, pool events, and cars coming and going at all hours. Whether their view of the mountains stays a view of the mountains.

There is also a property value question, and the honest answer is that it cuts both ways. Homes directly backing up to a large commercial project often see buyers hesitate. Buyers ask about noise, headlights, and resale. On the other hand, a well-run resort brings jobs, restaurants, and services within walking distance, and some buyers want that. In my experience the closest homes feel the downside the most, and homes a few streets back often see little change or even a small lift from the added amenities.

Renters in the area have a stake too. New hotel and casino jobs in south Las Vegas mean more people looking to live nearby. That is demand, and demand pushes rents. Enterprise already has a lot of newer rental product, and a project like this would add hundreds of hospitality jobs to the local labor pool if it gets built and opens.

Traffic deserves its own mention because Las Vegas Boulevard south of the resort corridor already carries a heavy load. Ford Avenue is a smaller cross street feeding into it. Adding a hotel, a casino floor, restaurants, and a garage means new turning movements at an intersection that was not designed around that kind of volume. Whether the county requires a traffic study and road improvements as a condition of approval is one of the most practical things residents can push for at the hearing.

Families with kids in the area think about this differently than investors do. Parents ask about after-dark activity, about who is walking through the neighborhood at two in the morning, and about whether the school bus stop still feels the same. Those concerns are real and they are worth raising in public comment, but they are also the kind of thing that can be addressed with design conditions like controlled access points, fencing, and lighting that points down instead of out.

Then there is the precedent. Expanding the Gaming Enterprise District at this corner sets a marker. Other landowners along that stretch of Las Vegas Boulevard will look at the decision and decide whether to file their own applications. One approval rarely stays a single approval in a growth corridor like this one.

Background and History

The Gaming Enterprise District exists because Nevada decided a long time ago that casinos should be concentrated, not scattered. The idea was to protect residential neighborhoods from ending up with a casino at the end of the street. Parcels inside the district can pursue non-restricted gaming licenses. Parcels outside it cannot, unless the map gets changed through a public process.

That process is exactly what is happening here. The special use permit request is the mechanism to bring this corner into the district. Because the change is permanent and because it affects neighbors, Nevada law and county code require notice and a hearing. This is why the September 2 meeting matters more than a typical building permit.

The south end of Las Vegas Boulevard has changed fast over the last 15 years. What used to be open desert and scattered commercial buildings south of the resort corridor is now surrounded by master planned neighborhoods, apartments, retail centers, and schools. Enterprise and the surrounding area absorbed a huge share of the valley's residential growth. Rooftops arrived first. Now commercial and resort proposals are following the rooftops.

Clark County has approved a long list of large projects in recent years, and it has also turned some down or sent them back for changes. Zoning Commission hearings are genuinely contested. Staff writes a recommendation, the applicant presents, neighbors speak, and commissioners vote. Recommendations do not always match the outcome, which is a reminder that public comment can move the needle.

Enterprise itself is worth understanding here. It is one of the largest unincorporated towns in Clark County by population, and it grew from mostly empty desert into a dense suburban area in about two decades. Because it is unincorporated, residents do not vote for a mayor or a city council that handles their zoning. They rely on the county commission, a town advisory board, and their own turnout at hearings. That structure catches a lot of newer residents off guard the first time a large project shows up near their street.

It is also worth remembering that approval is not the same thing as construction.

Southern Nevada has a long list of announced casino projects that never opened, and a shorter list of ones that opened years later than planned and in a different form than originally drawn. Land near the south end of Las Vegas Boulevard has been especially prone to this. Plans get filed, entitlements get approved, then the property trades hands and a new owner comes back with something different. Treat the September 2 hearing as an important checkpoint, not as a finish line.

Plenty of entitled projects in Southern Nevada sit for years while financing, licensing, and market conditions catch up. A gaming license from the Nevada Gaming Control Board and the Nevada Gaming Commission is its own separate, lengthy process. A yes vote on September 2 would be a first step, not a groundbreaking.

What Happens Next

The next date to watch is September 2, 2026, when the Clark County Zoning Commission is scheduled to hear the item. At that meeting, county staff will present its analysis, the applicant will make its case, and members of the public will get time to speak. Commissioners can approve, deny, approve with conditions, or postpone the item again.

Conditions are the outcome a lot of neighborhood groups actually aim for. Rather than trying to kill a project outright, residents often push for specific limits. Things like a height step-down along the residential edge, taller perimeter walls, restrictions on where trucks can enter and unload, downward-facing shielded lighting, hours of operation for outdoor event space, and a traffic study with required road improvements. Those conditions become enforceable if they are written into the approval.

There is also a quieter timeline running in the background. Even a fully approved project needs an operator, a construction lender, and a gaming license holder. Those pieces do not always line up. Watch for follow-up filings at the county, for any change in ownership of the parcel, and for whether the applicant names a hotel brand or a gaming operator. Those are the signals that separate a project that is really moving from a set of drawings meant to add value to land before a sale.

If the commission approves the special use permit, the developer would still need to work through building permits, a full gaming licensing process at the state level, and financing. Each of those steps takes time. If the commission denies it, the applicant can revise the plan and reapply, or appeal. If it gets postponed again, watch for a new date on a future Zoning Commission agenda.

Ryan's Take

I have watched a lot of these hearings, and the pattern is consistent. The people who show up early and speak specifically get results. The people who wait until the concrete is poured get nothing. If you own a home within a few blocks of Las Vegas Boulevard and Ford Avenue, September 2 is your window, and it is a short one.

On the value question, I would not panic. Enterprise is a strong submarket with real buyer demand, good newer housing stock, and easy access to the airport and the resort corridor. A hotel casino at that corner is not going to erase that. What I would think carefully about is proximity. If your backyard shares a wall with the site, that is a legitimate resale conversation and you should plan for buyer questions. If you are three or four streets in, I expect the effect to be small, and the added restaurants and jobs may end up being a net positive over time.

For buyers looking in Enterprise right now, I would say the opposite of panic. Ask the question. If you are touring a home near Las Vegas Boulevard and Ford Avenue, pull up the county agenda and see what is pending nearby. That is a five-minute check that tells you more about your next ten years in a house than almost anything on the listing sheet. Knowing what is proposed is not a reason to walk away, it is a reason to negotiate with your eyes open.

My honest advice to sellers in the immediate area is this. Do not rush a listing based on a proposal that has not been voted on. Nothing is approved. Decisions made out of fear of a headline usually cost more than they save.

What You Can Do

Start by checking the Clark County Zoning Commission agenda for September 2, 2026 on the county website at clarkcountynv.gov. Agendas are posted in advance and include the application number, the staff report, and the site plans. Reading the staff report before the meeting is the fastest way to understand what is actually being asked for versus what is being repeated on social media.

You can attend the meeting in person and speak during public comment, and you can submit written comment ahead of time. Written comment counts. It becomes part of the record, and commissioners read it. Be specific. Name your street, describe the exact impact you expect, and ask for a concrete condition rather than a general complaint. "Please require a traffic study and a step-down in height along the residential edge" lands better than "I do not want this."

If you cannot make the meeting, you still have options. Contact the county commissioner who represents your district and share your position in writing. Ask the Enterprise Town Advisory Board when the item was reviewed and what recommendation it made. Sign up for agenda notifications on the county site so future items near you do not surprise you. Most people find out about a project when the fence goes up, and by then the vote happened months ago.

Talk to your neighbors and your HOA board, if you have one. A group of residents making the same specific request carries more weight than 20 people making 20 different ones. And keep an eye on future agendas after the vote, because conditions, permits, and any follow-up applications will show up there too.
