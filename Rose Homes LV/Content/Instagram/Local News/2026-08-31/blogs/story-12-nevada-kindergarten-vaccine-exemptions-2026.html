Nevada Kindergarten Vaccine Exemptions Hit a Record High

New federal data shows that vaccine exemptions among Nevada kindergarteners have climbed to a record high, and the share of five and six year olds arriving at school with all their required early childhood shots has fallen. Nevada now ranks among the states with the highest exemption rates in the country for the 2025-26 school year, trailing only Idaho, Utah, Oregon, and Arizona.

The shift is not only a Nevada story. Nationwide, the kindergarten exemption rate rose to a record 4.2 percent, up from 3.6 percent the year before. That works out to roughly 155,000 children across the United States who started kindergarten with at least one exemption on file instead of a completed vaccine record.

For Clark County, where the Clark County School District enrolls the large majority of Nevada's public school students, those statewide numbers are mostly our numbers. If you have a child in a CCSD elementary school, a charter school, or a private school in Henderson, Summerlin, North Las Vegas, or anywhere else in the valley, this data is describing the classroom your kid sits in every day. Here is what the numbers actually say, where they come from, and who to talk to if you have questions about your own family.

What the New Data Shows

The Las Vegas Review-Journal reported on August 31, 2026 that federal figures show fewer Nevada kindergarteners are getting the required early childhood vaccinations while exemption rates hit record highs. The reporting places Nevada among the states with the highest exemption rates in 2025-26. Only Idaho, Utah, Oregon, and Arizona posted higher rates.

The national picture in the same release provides the benchmark. Across the country, the kindergarten exemption rate reached 4.2 percent, a record. The prior year figure was 3.6 percent. That is a jump of six tenths of a percentage point in a single school year, which sounds small until you convert it into children. About 155,000 kindergarteners nationally now have an exemption on file for one or more required vaccines.

Two things are moving at once here, and it is worth separating them. The first is the exemption rate, which measures the share of kindergarteners whose parents filed paperwork opting out of one or more required vaccines. The second is the overall immunization rate, which measures the share of kindergarteners who are fully up to date. Those two numbers usually move in opposite directions, and in Nevada both moved in the direction that raises attention: exemptions up, immunizations down.

It is also worth understanding that an exemption is not the same thing as a child who has received zero vaccines. A family can file an exemption for a single required vaccine while their child is current on everything else. The federal counts group all of those situations together, so the headline number covers a wide range of individual family circumstances. That is a limitation of the data, not a criticism of it, and it is a good reason to be careful about drawing conclusions about any specific school or neighborhood from a statewide figure.

Nevada, like every state, sets its own list of required immunizations for school entry and its own rules for how a family can be excused from them. Nevada allows medical exemptions signed by a licensed physician and religious belief exemptions. Nevada does not offer a broad personal or philosophical belief exemption the way some states do. Parents who want to confirm the current requirement list and the current exemption process for their child should check directly with the Southern Nevada Health District or their school registrar rather than relying on secondhand summaries, including this one.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Clark County is the center of gravity for Nevada schools. CCSD is one of the largest school districts in the United States, and the valley holds roughly three quarters of the state's population. When a statewide school health number moves, Clark County is doing most of the moving. So a story framed as "Nevada" is functionally a story about Las Vegas, Henderson, North Las Vegas, and the unincorporated communities in between.

The practical effect for families is straightforward. Local school and public health officials use immunization coverage numbers to plan. Those figures shape how the Southern Nevada Health District staffs clinics, how it targets outreach, and how it responds if a case of a vaccine preventable illness shows up in a school. If you get a letter home from your child's school about an exposure, coverage data is part of what determines how the district and the health district respond.

There is a scheduling angle too, and it is one parents feel every August. Nevada requires proof of certain immunizations before a child can start school. Every year, a share of Clark County families end up scrambling in the final two weeks of summer because an appointment was hard to get or a record was missing. Southern Nevada has a well documented shortage of health care providers relative to its population, and pediatric appointment availability is part of that. Some of what looks like a choice in the aggregate data is actually access, timing, and paperwork.

Class size is another quiet factor in how these numbers play out here. CCSD elementary classrooms are large by national standards, and a kindergarten room in the valley can hold well over twenty children. That is simply a fact about the district, and it means the health office at a single Clark County elementary school may be tracking records for hundreds of five and six year olds at once. Scale makes the paperwork side of this harder than it would be in a small district, which is one more reason the compliance window at the start of the year matters.

For families new to the valley, and Las Vegas gets a lot of those, the record transfer piece is its own hurdle. A child moving here from another state arrives with a different set of state requirements, sometimes a paper record, sometimes an electronic one, and the family has to reconcile it with Nevada's list. Nevada operates a statewide immunization registry that providers use, but records from out of state do not automatically land in it. Building in time for this before the first day of school saves a lot of stress.

Background and History

School immunization requirements in the United States are set state by state, not federally. The federal government collects and publishes the data, but the actual list of required shots, the enforcement mechanism, and the exemption categories all come from state law and state regulation. That is why the state-by-state spread is so wide, and why Nevada's ranking is measured against places like Idaho and Utah rather than against a single national standard.

The national exemption rate has been trending upward for several years now. The move from 3.6 percent to a record 4.2 percent in one year is the latest step in a longer climb rather than a sudden break. Public health researchers have pointed to a mix of factors behind the trend, including disruptions to routine pediatric care during and after the pandemic years, a decline in trust in public health institutions, easier online access to exemption paperwork in some states, and a general drift away from routine well child visits.

Nevada's position near the top of the exemption list is also not brand new. The state has ranked high in previous years, and the western states as a group have consistently posted higher exemption rates than the national average. Idaho, Utah, Oregon, and Arizona sitting above Nevada in 2025-26 fits a regional pattern that has held for a while. Exactly why the western United States clusters this way is a question researchers are still working on, and it likely involves a combination of state law, provider density, and local culture rather than any single cause.

There is a Clark County specific wrinkle worth naming. CCSD has spent much of the last several years dealing with staffing shortages that reach into school health offices. School nurses are the people who chase down incomplete records, contact families, and get children into compliance before deadlines. When those positions are hard to fill, follow up gets slower. That does not explain a statewide trend on its own, but it is part of the local machinery that turns a requirement into a completed record.

One more piece of history helps explain why the federal number exists at all. States report kindergarten immunization and exemption data to the federal government each year, and those reports get compiled into a single national snapshot. The methodology is not identical in every state, and some states survey a sample of schools while others attempt a full census. That means small differences between states should be read carefully, though a state sitting near the top of the list year after year, as Nevada has, is a more durable signal than any single year's ranking.

What Happens Next

The immediate calendar item is the current school year. Nevada schools enforce immunization requirements at enrollment, which means the compliance work for 2026-27 is happening right now across the valley. Families with incomplete records typically receive notice from their school and are given a window to either complete the requirement, file an exemption, or provide documentation that a shot is scheduled.

Longer term, the exemption question is a legislative one. Nevada's list of required immunizations lives in state regulation, and the exemption categories live in state law. Any change to either would run through the Nevada Legislature or the State Board of Health, not through CCSD. The Legislature next meets in regular session in 2027, so if bills touching school immunization law are introduced, that is the window to watch. Interim committees can take up the topic before then.

Locally, expect the Southern Nevada Health District to continue running its immunization clinics and back to school outreach. The health district publishes clinic locations, hours, and appointment information on its website, and it has historically increased capacity around the start of the school year. School based and community based clinic events also pop up through the fall. If a new set of state or county level numbers is released breaking Nevada's figure down by district or by school, that would be the next meaningful data point for Clark County parents specifically.

The other thing to watch is next year's release. Because the federal data comes out annually, the real question is whether the 2026-27 numbers show Nevada's exemption rate continuing to climb, flattening out, or reversing. A single year can be noise. Two or three years pointing the same direction is a trend, and that is when policymakers at the state level tend to pay closer attention. Parents who want to track this can simply mark the calendar for late next summer, when the next round of school year figures typically lands.

Ryan's Take

I am a real estate agent, not a doctor, and I want to be direct about that. I am not going to tell you what to do with your child's medical care, and I would be suspicious of anyone in my line of work who tried. What I can tell you is how a story like this tends to land with the families I work with, because I talk to a lot of people who are choosing a neighborhood and a school at the same time.

Here is what I see. School data drives housing decisions in this valley more than almost anything else except price. Buyers pull up ratings, test scores, boundary maps, and increasingly anything else they can find about a campus before they make an offer on a house three streets away. A statewide number like this one does not tell you anything about a specific school, and I would caution anyone against treating it like it does. Nevada's figure is an average that spans Elko to Enterprise, and Clark County alone runs hundreds of schools with very different populations.

My honest advice is to get your information from the source rather than from a headline or a comment section. If you have a question about your own child, that is a conversation for your pediatrician. If you have a question about what your school requires or what the local numbers look like, that is a call to your school office or to the Southern Nevada Health District. Those are the people with the actual answers, and both are easier to reach than most parents assume.

What You Can Do

Start with your own paperwork. Pull your child's immunization record and compare it against Nevada's current school entry requirements, which your school registrar can provide and which the Southern Nevada Health District publishes. If you have moved here from another state, do this early, because reconciling an out of state record almost always takes longer than families expect.

For medical questions, talk to your pediatrician or family doctor. That is the right place for questions about your specific child, about scheduling, about catch up schedules if a record is incomplete, and about medical exemptions. If your family does not currently have a pediatrician, the Southern Nevada Health District can point you toward clinic options, and CCSD school health offices can often help connect families to local resources.

For public health questions, including clinic locations, appointment availability, immunization records, and current requirements, contact the Southern Nevada Health District directly at snhd.info. The health district runs immunization services in the valley and is the local authority on what Nevada requires. Your school registrar is the right contact for enrollment deadlines, compliance letters, and what documentation your specific campus needs on file.

If you want to follow the policy side, watch the Nevada Legislature ahead of the 2027 session and the Nevada State Board of Health, since both are where school immunization rules are actually set. CCSD board meetings are the place for questions about how the district handles compliance and school health staffing, and those meetings include public comment.
