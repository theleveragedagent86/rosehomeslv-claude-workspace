Golden Knights Get 15 Nationally Televised Games for the 2026-27 Season

The Vegas Golden Knights will appear on national television 15 times during the 2026-27 season, and 11 of those games are at T-Mobile Arena. The NHL and its broadcast partners released the full national schedule on August 27, 2026, spreading those Vegas games across ESPN, ESPN+, TNT, and ABC.

If you already bought tickets, read this part carefully. Six Vegas games had their start times changed to fit the national windows. That means the game you planned around may not start when you think it does, and the parking plan you had in mind may need a rewrite.

The season opener is the headliner. Vegas hosts the Chicago Blackhawks on September 29 and the game airs on ESPN, which puts the Golden Knights in front of a national audience on night one.

What Happened

On August 27, 2026, the NHL and its national TV partners published their broadcast schedules for the coming season. The release is an annual ritual, and for Vegas fans it is the moment the calendar finally becomes real. Until the networks pick their games, nobody knows which nights turn into events and which start times shift.

The number that matters most is 15. That is how many Golden Knights games will be carried on national television across ESPN, ESPN+, TNT, and ABC. Eleven of those 15 are home games at T-Mobile Arena. The other four are on the road.

The home-heavy split is worth pausing on. Networks pick games they think will draw viewers, and they also pick buildings that look good on camera. Eleven national broadcasts from T-Mobile Arena means the Strip skyline, the arena, and the Vegas crowd all get airtime that the local tourism industry could never buy outright.

The league also announced updated start times for six Vegas games. When a national network takes a game, it often slides that game into a specific window so it does not collide with another broadcast. A 7:00 p.m. local start can become a 7:30 p.m. start, or an afternoon game can move earlier or later. The league did not change the dates, only the clocks, but for anyone holding tickets the clock is the part that matters.

The September 29 opener against the Chicago Blackhawks lands on ESPN. Opening night is already the loudest night of the Vegas hockey year. Putting it on ESPN turns a local celebration into a national one, and it sets the tone for a season where Vegas is clearly still viewed as a marquee franchise by the people who buy the broadcast rights.

All of this sits on top of the local broadcast plan. The Golden Knights are also putting 69 regular season games on Scripps Sports and KnightTime+, carried locally on The Spot-Vegas 34. Add the 15 national games and the overwhelming majority of the season is available to Clark County fans without a cable subscription.

It helps to understand how the four outlets differ, because they are not interchangeable. ESPN and TNT are cable channels that most streaming bundles carry. ABC is broadcast television, which means it reaches the widest possible audience including anyone with a basic antenna. ESPN+ is a paid streaming service, and games that land there are not on the cable channel at the same time. When a Vegas game shows up on ESPN+, fans who only have cable will not find it on their usual channel.

That mix is why a 15 game national slate feels bigger than the number suggests. Each of those broadcasts reaches an audience far beyond Clark County. A regular local telecast reaches Las Vegas households. A national telecast reaches every hockey market in the country at once, and the announcers spend three hours talking about the team, the arena, and the city.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Start with the practical piece. Six changed start times means six nights where your plans need a second look. If you have tickets, check your account before you head downtown. A 30 minute shift sounds small until you are sitting on Tropicana Avenue watching the first period start without you.

Eleven national home games also changes traffic patterns on the south end of the Strip. National broadcasts tend to draw bigger walk-up crowds and more visitors, which means the arena district gets busier earlier. If you live in the neighborhoods off Tropicana, Harmon, or Dean Martin, those 11 nights are worth marking on a calendar. Locals who commute through that corridor already know the difference between a regular Tuesday and a Golden Knights Tuesday.

There is a money angle too. Games that appear on national television usually carry higher ticket prices on the resale market. Sellers know a nationally televised game against a strong opponent is a hotter product. If you are buying, the non-national nights are almost always the better value. If you are selling tickets you cannot use, those 11 home broadcasts are your best inventory.

Then there is the part nobody puts a number on. Eleven nationally televised games from T-Mobile Arena is 11 nights where Las Vegas is on screen as a real city with a real team and real fans, not just a backdrop for a party. That image matters. It is one of the quiet reasons families keep relocating here, and it shows up in the questions I get from out of state buyers who watched a game and started wondering what life here actually costs.

For families with kids in youth hockey, national broadcasts do something else. They give young players a reason to care. The rinks around the valley fill up after a big televised win, and youth programs at City National Arena and elsewhere feel it. A team on national TV builds the next group of players.

Local businesses feel the schedule too. The bars, restaurants, and small shops near the arena build their staffing around game nights, and a nationally televised game usually means a longer, busier evening. Neighborhood sports bars across the valley, from Henderson to Centennial Hills, see the same pattern on a smaller scale. When a game is on ESPN or ABC instead of only the local channel, more people make it a night out instead of watching from the couch.

And for anyone who works nights or has a schedule that does not bend, the six changed start times are more than a nuisance. A game moving 30 minutes later can be the difference between catching the third period and missing it entirely. Knowing the new times ahead of the season lets you plan shifts, childcare, and dinner instead of scrambling.

Background and History

The Golden Knights have been a national television regular since the very beginning. The 2017-18 expansion season turned into one of the great stories in modern sports, and networks have never really let go of that. A team that reached the Stanley Cup Final in its first year and won the Cup in 2023 does not have to argue for airtime.

The current broadcast landscape took shape when the NHL signed its national deals with ESPN and Turner. ESPN carries games on its main channel, on ABC for the biggest windows, and on ESPN+ for streaming. TNT carries its own slate. That structure is why a single team's 15 national games get scattered across four different places instead of living on one channel.

Start time changes come with the territory. National windows are built to avoid overlap, so the league adjusts local start times to fit. Vegas fans have dealt with this every year since the national deals began. It is routine for the league and mildly annoying for the person who booked a dinner reservation.

The local side of the picture changed more recently. The Golden Knights moved to a free over-the-air model with Scripps Sports, which put the bulk of the season on antenna television instead of behind a cable paywall. That was a real shift for a market like Las Vegas, where a large share of households have cut cable entirely. The national schedule and the free local schedule now work together to make the season unusually easy to follow.

It is also worth remembering how unlikely all of this looked not long ago. Las Vegas spent decades as the largest American city without a major professional franchise. The conventional wisdom said a transient town with a tourism economy could not support one. The Golden Knights proved that wrong quickly, and the Raiders, the Aces, and now a growing hockey and sports calendar at T-Mobile Arena followed. Fifteen national broadcasts is a footnote in that larger story, but it is the kind of footnote that would have sounded absurd in 2016.

What Happens Next

The next date on the calendar is September 29, when Vegas hosts Chicago at T-Mobile Arena on ESPN. Before that, preseason games give fans a chance to see the roster and get back into the rhythm of the season. The regular season then runs through the spring, with the national games sprinkled throughout.

Between now and opening night, expect a few more schedule details to firm up. Networks sometimes flex games later in the season, especially in the final weeks when playoff races decide which matchups are worth national attention. The 15 games announced in August are the starting point, not necessarily the final count.

If you hold season tickets or single game tickets, the most useful thing you can do is check your ticket account for the six games with updated start times. The league changed the times, but the ticket in your app should reflect the new time. Verify it yourself rather than assuming, especially for any game you bought before August 27.

Watch for the local broadcast details as well. The 69 games on Scripps Sports and KnightTime+ through The Spot-Vegas 34 will have their own schedule, and knowing which games are national and which are local saves you from hunting for a channel at 7:00 on a game night.

One more thing to keep an eye on is the back half of the season. If Vegas is in a tight playoff race in March and April, the networks have every reason to add games. The team has been a consistent draw since 2017, and late season national additions have become fairly normal for franchises in that position. Plan on 15 and treat anything beyond that as a bonus.

Ryan's Take

I have watched this city change since the Golden Knights arrived, and I do not think most people appreciate how much a professional sports team does for a housing market. It is not that hockey makes home prices go up. It is that hockey made Las Vegas feel like a place where people live year round instead of a place people visit for a weekend. Buyers respond to that.

When I show homes to families relocating from Chicago, Minnesota, or Michigan, the arena comes up constantly. They want to know how far T-Mobile Arena is from Summerlin, whether there is youth hockey nearby, and how bad game night traffic gets. Those are the questions of people planning a life here, not people planning a vacation. Fifteen national broadcasts means 15 more nights where somebody in a cold city sees this place and starts asking those questions.

The neighborhoods closest to the arena corridor and the ones near City National Arena in Summerlin have both benefited from that identity shift. It is a small factor compared to jobs, schools, and interest rates, but it is real, and it has been remarkably consistent since 2017.

My honest advice for anyone using this schedule is simple. Do not overpay for the national games just because they are on ESPN. The best value in the building is a Tuesday in January against a team nobody is talking about, and the hockey is exactly the same. Save the money and put it toward something that actually builds equity.

What You Can Do

First, check your tickets. If you bought seats for any Golden Knights game before August 27, open your ticket account and confirm the start time. Six games moved. The team and the ticketing platform should show the corrected time, and a two minute check now beats a rushed drive later.

Second, sort your viewing setup before opening night. The national games live on ESPN, ESPN+, TNT, and ABC. The bulk of the season lives on The Spot-Vegas 34 through Scripps Sports and KnightTime+, which is free over the air. If you have cut cable, a basic antenna picks up the local games at no monthly cost. Scan your channels once and you are set for the year.

Third, plan around the 11 national home nights if you live or commute near the arena. Those evenings bring heavier traffic to the south Strip. Leaving 20 minutes earlier or taking a different route saves a lot of frustration, and it is the kind of local knowledge that makes living here easier.

Finally, if you have kids who are getting interested after watching a game, look into the youth programs at City National Arena in Summerlin and the other rinks around the valley. Registration windows fill up quickly once the season starts and the televised games get people excited.

One small habit that pays off all season: put the 11 national home dates in your phone calendar with a reminder the morning of. That single step handles the traffic planning, the ticket price question, and the start time confusion all at once. It takes about five minutes to set up in late September and saves you from a dozen small headaches between October and April.

If you are new to Las Vegas and still figuring out the rhythm of the city, this schedule is a good place to start. Game nights are one of the few things that make the whole valley move at the same time, and learning that rhythm early makes settling in a lot easier.
