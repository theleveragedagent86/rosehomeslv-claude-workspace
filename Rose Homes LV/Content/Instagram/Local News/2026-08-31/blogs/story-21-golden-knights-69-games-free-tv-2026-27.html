Golden Knights Will Put 69 Games on Free Over-the-Air TV in Las Vegas

The Vegas Golden Knights will put 69 regular season games on free over-the-air television in Las Vegas this season. You do not need cable. You do not need a streaming subscription. If you have a TV and a basic antenna, you can watch almost the entire season for nothing.

The team's Season X broadcast plan runs those 69 games through Scripps Sports and KnightTime+, carried locally on The Spot-Vegas 34. That sits on top of the 15 nationally televised Golden Knights games the NHL announced for 2026-27. For a lot of families in Clark County, this is the cheapest their hockey season has ever been.

What Happened

The Golden Knights released their Season X broadcast plan for the 2026-27 campaign. The headline number is 69. That is how many regular season games will be produced and carried locally through Scripps Sports and the team's KnightTime+ platform. In the Las Vegas market, those games land on The Spot-Vegas 34, which is a free over-the-air broadcast station.

Over the air means exactly what it sounds like. The signal travels through the air to an antenna. There is no monthly bill attached to it. You buy an antenna one time, you plug it into your television, you scan for channels, and the games show up. That is the entire process.

The 69 locally broadcast games are separate from the national schedule. On August 27, 2026, the NHL and its national television partners released their broadcast schedules for the coming season. The Golden Knights drew 15 nationally televised games spread across ESPN, ESPN+, TNT, and ABC. Eleven of those are home games at T-Mobile Arena and four are on the road. The league also adjusted start times for six Vegas games, so anyone who already blocked out a night should double check the clock.

The season opener is Tuesday, September 29 against the Chicago Blackhawks, and that one airs on ESPN. So the very first puck drop of the year is a national broadcast, not a local one. After that, the bulk of the schedule shifts to the local over-the-air package that most fans will use night after night.

Scripps Sports has been the Golden Knights' local broadcast partner since the team moved away from the traditional regional sports network model. That move was unusual when it happened. Most NHL, NBA, and MLB teams historically sold their local rights to a cable regional sports network, which then charged cable companies a carriage fee, which then showed up on your monthly bill whether you watched hockey or not. Vegas went a different direction and put the games on broadcast television instead.

KnightTime+ is the second half of the setup. It is the team's own branded platform for game coverage, and it works alongside the over-the-air signal rather than replacing it. The idea is that a fan should be able to find the game without hunting through a cable guide or hitting a login screen. Between the antenna feed and KnightTime+, the team is trying to remove every step between a Las Vegas resident and a Golden Knights broadcast.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Start with the money. A typical cable or live TV streaming package that carries a full slate of local sports runs somewhere between 80 and 120 dollars a month for most households. Over a hockey season that stretches from late September into April, that adds up fast. A one-time antenna purchase usually runs between 20 and 60 dollars depending on whether you want an indoor model or a rooftop setup.

For a family in Spring Valley, Centennial Hills, or Green Valley that already cut the cord, this is a real quality of life win. You do not have to add a subscription back just to follow the team. You do not have to rotate between free trials in March when the playoff race gets tight. You plug in a device once and you are done for the season.

It also matters for the people who cannot easily add another bill right now. Housing costs in Clark County have climbed steadily. Insurance, utilities, and groceries all went up. When a household is watching every line item, a hockey subscription is one of the first things to go. Free over-the-air access means the fan base does not shrink to only the people who can afford a premium package.

There is a community piece here too. The Golden Knights are one of the few things in this valley that everyone shares. Summerlin, Henderson, North Las Vegas, and the east side all root for the same team. When 69 games are free to anyone with an antenna, the conversation at work on Monday morning includes more people. That is not a small thing in a city where a lot of residents moved here from somewhere else and are still building local roots.

Parents with kids in youth hockey around the valley get something out of this as well. Kids who watch games regularly pick up the pace and the positioning. When the broadcast is free, a family with a nine year old at Lifeguard Arena in Henderson or City National Arena in Summerlin can leave the game on in the background every night without thinking about it.

There is a practical angle for shift workers too. Las Vegas runs around the clock. Plenty of people in this valley work casino floors, hospitals, warehouses, and the airport on schedules that do not line up with a 7 p.m. puck drop. Over-the-air broadcasts are easy to record with a basic digital video recorder box or a tuner that saves to a USB drive, which means a swing shift bartender can watch the third period at two in the morning without paying a monthly fee for the privilege.

Renters get a benefit that is easy to overlook. If you are in an apartment in Paradise or a rental in Mountains Edge and you move every year or two, an antenna moves with you. There is no installer appointment, no equipment return, no early termination fee, and no waiting on a service window. You unplug it, you box it, and it works at the new place the same afternoon.

Background and History

Local sports broadcasting has been shifting under everyone's feet for the past several years. The old model was simple and expensive. A regional sports network bought the rights to a team, put the games behind cable, and collected a fee from every cable subscriber in the market. Cable providers passed that cost along. If you lived in Las Vegas and had cable, you helped pay for hockey broadcasts even if you never watched a single period.

That model started breaking when people cancelled cable in large numbers. Fewer subscribers meant fewer carriage fees, which meant regional sports networks had less money to pay teams. Several of those networks ran into serious financial trouble nationally. Teams around the country started looking for a different way to reach their fans.

Free broadcast television also has a longer history here than most people remember. Before cable took over sports, local games on local channels were the normal way a city followed its team. What is happening now is closer to a return than an invention. The technology changed, the picture quality is far better, and the antennas got a lot smaller, but the basic deal is the same one families had decades ago.

The Golden Knights were early to that shift. Partnering with Scripps Sports moved the games onto broadcast television, where the business model runs on advertising and reach instead of subscriber fees. The tradeoff is straightforward. The team gives up guaranteed carriage money and gets a much larger potential audience in return, because anyone in the market can watch for free.

The KnightTime+ platform sits alongside the broadcast as the team's own distribution channel. Between the over-the-air signal on The Spot-Vegas 34 and the KnightTime+ offering, the goal has been to make the games findable without a cable login. This season's 69-game figure is the clearest version of that strategy so far.

What Happens Next

The preseason comes first. The Golden Knights host the Utah Mammoth on Thursday, September 24 at 7 p.m. and the San Jose Sharks on Saturday, September 26 at 7 p.m. at T-Mobile Arena. Road preseason games are set against the Los Angeles Kings and the San Jose Sharks. Ticketing information for the home preseason dates is available at 702-645-4259.

One more thing to sort out early is your recording setup, if you want one. Over-the-air recording requires a separate tuner box or a television with a built-in recording feature, since there is no cable box handling it for you. Those devices are inexpensive, but they take an afternoon to configure, and September is a better time to do that than the middle of a road trip in January.

Then the regular season opens Tuesday, September 29 against the Chicago Blackhawks on ESPN. From there the local broadcast slate takes over for most nights on the calendar. Anyone planning to watch over the air should set up and test their antenna before the end of September rather than scrambling on game night.

Watch for the full game-by-game channel listing as the season gets closer. With 15 national broadcasts and 69 local ones, a handful of nights will move between platforms, and the six games with adjusted start times are worth confirming individually. If you have tickets or you host a watch party, check the time again in mid-September.

It is also worth watching how the rest of the league reacts. If the Vegas model keeps delivering large local audiences, more teams will follow the same path. That would be good news for sports fans generally, because the trend for the past decade has run the other direction, with games moving behind more paywalls, not fewer.

Keep an eye on the postseason picture as well. National partners typically pick up more games as the calendar moves toward spring and the standings tighten. That means the mix between local and national broadcasts is not fixed across the whole season. Fans relying only on an antenna should expect a few more national pickups in the back half of the year than in October and November.

What You Can Do

First, get an antenna and test it now. Most modern flat screen televisions have a built-in tuner, so you plug the antenna coax into the back of the set, go into the settings menu, and run a channel scan. Indoor flat antennas work fine for a lot of the valley. If you live farther out, in Blue Diamond, Boulder City, or the far northwest, you may need an amplified or attic-mounted antenna to pull in the signal cleanly.

Second, confirm where The Spot-Vegas 34 lands on your channel lineup after the scan finishes. Over-the-air channel numbers do not always match the number in the station's name, so run the scan and look at what actually appears. Do this in early September, not on opening night.

Third, mark the schedule. The season opener on September 29 against Chicago is on ESPN, and 14 more games land on national partners across ESPN, ESPN+, TNT, and ABC. If you only have an antenna, plan ahead for those specific dates, whether that means a friend's house or a local sports bar.

Fourth, tell your neighbors. A lot of people in this city assume they need a paid package to follow the team and never look into it. If you know someone who dropped cable and quietly gave up on watching hockey, this is worth passing along.

A few setup tips help if your first scan comes back thin. Antennas work best near a window and as high off the floor as you can get them, and a window facing the broadcast towers usually pulls a cleaner signal than an interior wall. Stucco, metal roofing, and low-emissivity window film can all cut into reception, which is common in newer Clark County construction. If you get a picture that breaks up, move the antenna a few feet and run the scan again before you assume it does not work.

If you live in a condo or a community with an HOA, check the rules before mounting anything outside. Federal rules protect a lot of over-the-air antenna installations, but the details depend on whether you control the space you are mounting to, so a quick email to your association saves a headache later. In most cases an indoor antenna solves the problem without any of that.
