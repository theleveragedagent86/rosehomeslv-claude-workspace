CCSD Police Traffic Stops Near Schools Are Up More Than 40 Percent Under the New Chief

If you drive through a Clark County school zone this fall, your odds of getting pulled over went way up. Clark County School District Police Chief Jeff Clark said traffic stops near schools rose more than 40 percent during the first three weeks of the school year compared to the same stretch last year. Citations climbed almost as much, just under 40 percent.

Here is the part that matters most to families. Traffic accidents near schools fell almost 15 percent over that same window. So the tickets went up, and the crashes went down. That is the trade the district is making, and it is happening at the same time Nevada doubled the fines for school zone violations.

This is not a quiet policy change buried in a board agenda. It is showing up in drop-off lines at elementary schools in Summerlin, Henderson, North Las Vegas, and everywhere in between. Parents are noticing patrol cars parked where they never used to be. Some drivers are getting towed.

What Happened

CCSD Police Chief Jeff Clark shared the first three weeks of enforcement data from the 2026-2027 school year. Traffic stops near school campuses were up more than 40 percent versus the same period the year before. Citations issued were up just under 40 percent. Traffic accidents in those same areas dropped almost 15 percent.

The department is not only writing tickets. CCSD Police have also been towing vehicles as part of the enforcement push. That is a bigger consequence than a citation. A tow means a fee, a trip to an impound lot, and a very bad morning for whoever was behind the wheel.

The timing lines up with a new state law. Nevada's School Zone Bill took effect on July 1, 2026. It doubles the fines for violations committed inside a school zone. That means the same behavior that cost you one amount last spring costs you twice as much this fall. Speeding, running a stop sign, illegal U-turns, and blocking a crosswalk all fall under that umbrella when they happen in a marked school zone.

The reason behind all of it is the number nobody wants to think about. More than 420 students in Clark County were struck by cars on the way to or from school in a single year. That is not a statistic from another state or a national average. That is local kids, on local streets, walking or biking or crossing to get to campus. When you put 420 next to a 15 percent drop in accidents, you can see why the department decided the drop-off line was worth a police presence.

Chief Clark is new to the role. His swearing-in was covered earlier this year. These first three weeks of numbers are the clearest early signal yet of how he plans to run the department. The message is simple. Traffic safety around schools is a top priority, and the enforcement numbers back that up.

It is worth being precise about what these figures do and do not say. The comparison is three weeks of this school year against the same three weeks of last school year. It is not a full-semester or full-year number. It also does not tell us which schools saw the most activity or which violations drove the citation totals. What it does show is a clear, deliberate change in how the department is spending its hours, and an early result that moved in the direction the district wanted.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Start with the most obvious impact. If you have a kid in a Clark County school, you are almost certainly driving through a school zone twice a day, five days a week. That is roughly 360 trips through an enforcement area every school year. The odds catch up with people. A rolling stop you have made a thousand times without a second thought is now worth double, and there is a much better chance an officer is watching.

The financial side is real. Double fines on a school zone ticket can turn a minor mistake into a few hundred dollars. Add a tow and impound fee on top of that and you are looking at a number that hurts a family budget. Insurance rates can move too, depending on the violation and your carrier. For a household already stretched by rent or a mortgage payment in this market, that is not a small thing.

Then there is the other side of the ledger, and it is the side that should get more attention. Accidents near schools fell almost 15 percent. If you are a parent who lets your child walk to a neighborhood elementary school, that number is worth more than any ticket you might get. Fewer crashes means fewer of those 420 students. That is the whole point of the program.

There is a neighborhood quality-of-life angle too. Anyone who lives on a street that feeds into a school knows what the two rush windows look like. Cars double parked. Drivers making U-turns across two lanes. People blocking driveways. Kids darting between bumpers. Enforcement changes that behavior fast, because the people doing it are the same people every single day. They learn quickly.

Finally, this affects renters and commuters who do not have kids at all. Plenty of Las Vegas workers cut through residential streets to avoid the arterials. If your morning route runs past a campus between 7:00 and 8:30, you are in an active enforcement zone whether you have a child there or not. The doubled fine does not care why you are driving through.

There is a fairness question here that deserves an honest airing, and it is the reason this story gets heated in comment sections. Doubling a fine does not hit every household the same way. For a family with room in the budget, a school zone ticket is an annoyance. For a family living close to the edge, the same ticket plus a tow can mean a missed bill. That is a real critique of double-fine laws in general, and people raise it every time one passes. It does not make the pedestrian numbers less serious, but it is a fair thing to say out loud.

The counterweight is what a single crash costs a family. One child struck in a crosswalk changes a household permanently, and it changes a school community too. When you are weighing a few hundred dollars against that, most parents land in the same place even if they grumble on the way there. The 420 figure is the number that ends most of these arguments.

Background and History

School zone enforcement in Clark County has been an on-again, off-again story for years. CCSD has its own police department, separate from Las Vegas Metro and from the Henderson and North Las Vegas departments. That department has traditionally focused on what happens on campus. Traffic outside the fence was often treated as somebody else's problem, or at least a lower priority when staffing was tight.

The problem is that Clark County is one of the largest school districts in the country, spread across a valley built for cars. Many campuses sit on or near six-lane arterial roads. Sidewalks are inconsistent in older parts of the valley. Crosswalk signals do not always line up with where kids actually cross. Put a few hundred vehicles into a small parking lot in a fifteen minute window and you get the conditions that produce 420 pedestrian strikes in a year.

Nevada lawmakers responded with the School Zone Bill, which took effect July 1, 2026. Doubling fines is a well-worn tool in traffic policy. The theory is that the size of the penalty changes the calculation for drivers who would otherwise treat a school zone speed limit as a suggestion. Similar double-fine construction zone laws have been on the books in Nevada and other states for years.

What makes this fall different is that the law and the leadership changed at nearly the same time. A new chief took over CCSD Police with traffic safety near schools as a stated priority, and a new law gave the citations more bite. The first three weeks of data is what happens when both of those things land in the same August.

Growth is the background to all of it. Clark County keeps adding rooftops on the edges of the valley, and new schools go up to serve them. Roads that were built for a subdivision get traffic loads from three subdivisions. Meanwhile, older campuses in the central valley sit on streets that were laid out decades ago, before anyone imagined this many cars. Both situations produce the same result at 7:45 in the morning.

What Happens Next

Watch for the next data release. Three weeks is a small sample, and the start of any school year brings unusual traffic patterns as families settle into routines. The more meaningful test is whether the accident reduction holds through October, November, and into the spring semester. If stops stay high and crashes stay down, that is a durable result. If crashes tick back up once drivers adjust, the department will likely change tactics.

Expect the enforcement locations to shift. Departments running a program like this usually rotate patrols based on where the incidents actually happen. Schools with the worst pedestrian records and the busiest surrounding roads tend to get the most attention. If your neighborhood school has not seen a patrol car yet, that does not mean it never will.

There is also a question about whether the school board or the county weighs in on infrastructure. Enforcement changes driver behavior, but flashing beacons, better crosswalk striping, curb extensions, and adjusted signal timing change the road itself. Those cost money and require coordination between CCSD, Clark County, and the individual cities. If the pedestrian numbers stay bad in specific spots, expect pressure for physical fixes, not just tickets.

Keep an eye on the school board as well. Enforcement staffing is a budget line, and a program that leans on officers standing at drop-off lines has a cost. If the results hold, the case for funding it gets easier. If they do not, the same hours get argued over at the next budget cycle. Parents who care about this should know that the board meeting is where that conversation actually happens.

Ryan's Take

I look at almost every local story through the lens of what it does to a neighborhood, and this one is more interesting than it looks. School zone safety is one of the quiet things buyers ask about without realizing they are asking about it. When someone tours a house and says the street "feels busy," what they often mean is that they watched the 7:45 traffic and did not like it. A campus with a chaotic drop-off line and a record of crashes is a real drag on the blocks around it.

So a 15 percent drop in accidents near schools is a genuinely good outcome for the residential streets in Summerlin, Green Valley, Centennial Hills, Mountain's Edge, and every other neighborhood built around a school. Walkability is a selling point in Las Vegas, and it only counts if parents actually feel safe letting a kid walk. That said, I understand the frustration. Nobody enjoys being ticketed on the way to work for something that felt harmless. The honest answer is that both things are true at once. The enforcement is annoying, and it appears to be working. If you are going to be irritated about anything, be irritated at how our valley designs streets next to elementary schools, not at the officer holding the radar.

What You Can Do

The simplest step is to know your school zone hours and speed limit. School zone limits in Nevada are in effect during posted times, and they apply whether or not you see a child. Slow down before the sign, not at it. If you are dropping off, use the designated loop rather than making your own stop on the street. Do not make a U-turn in a school zone, and do not block a crosswalk to save thirty seconds.

Give yourself an extra ten minutes. Almost every school zone violation traces back to somebody running late. Leaving earlier is free, and it removes the pressure that leads to the rolling stop and the creative parking job. If your morning is consistently tight, look at whether a slightly different route or a walking meetup with a neighbor makes more sense.

If your neighborhood has a specific problem spot, report it. Talk to your school's principal or the school's parent organization, and contact the CCSD Police non-emergency line about recurring hazards. For street infrastructure like signage, striping, and signal timing, contact your city or Clark County public works department, since the roadway itself is usually not the district's to fix. Documented complaints from multiple parents carry a lot more weight than one call.

And if you got a ticket, read it carefully. Understand exactly what you were cited for, what the doubled fine amount is, and what your options are. Nevada traffic citations have deadlines, and ignoring one turns a bad morning into a much worse problem.

One more thing worth doing, and it costs nothing. Talk to your kids about the crossing itself. Remind them to use the marked crosswalk even when it adds a minute, to make eye contact with drivers before stepping out, and to keep headphones off until they are past the street. Enforcement handles the drivers. That conversation handles the other half.

If you are shopping for a home right now, drive the neighborhood at 7:45 on a school day before you write an offer. It is the single fastest way to learn what a street is really like. A block that is peaceful at 2:00 in the afternoon can be a very different place during drop-off, and that is information you want before you buy, not after.
