A $70 Million Henderson Sports and Entertainment Complex Opens in October

Henderson is about to open a $70 million sports and entertainment complex on St. Rose Parkway, and the weekend tournament calendar is already sold out through the end of 2026. Henderson Sport and Social sits at 3375 St. Rose Parkway near Maryland Parkway, covers 180,000 square feet across two levels, and opens to the public in October.

This is not a small city park project. The city of Henderson put in $60 million of public money and partner KemperSports added $10 million through a public-private partnership. That is a serious bet on the idea that families will use this building every single week, and that out-of-town tournament teams will fill Henderson hotel rooms and restaurants on the weekends.

For anyone living in Anthem, Inspirada, Seven Hills, Green Valley, or the newer neighborhoods along the St. Rose corridor, this changes what a Saturday looks like. It also changes one of the quiet things buyers ask me about all the time, which is what there actually is to do close to home.

What Happened

Henderson Sport and Social is a 180,000-square-foot, two-level complex built at 3375 St. Rose Parkway, on the Maryland Parkway side of the St. Rose corridor in Henderson. The Las Vegas Review-Journal reported on September 3, 2026 that the building will open next month, with a full slate of events already booked.

The money behind it is the part most residents miss. Henderson contributed $60 million. KemperSports, the operating partner, added $10 million. That is a $70 million total through a public-private partnership, which means the city owns a major piece of the asset and a private company runs the day-to-day operation. The city takes on more risk than it would with a simple lease, and it also keeps more control over how the building gets used.

Inside, the layout is built for volume. There are four full-sized indoor basketball courts that convert into eight volleyball courts. There are two hybrid turf indoor fields, which matter enormously in a valley where outdoor soccer and flag football practice in July is basically unsafe. There are 20 bowling lanes, a laser tag arena, an arcade, and outdoor mini golf. There is a restaurant and bar for the parents who are stuck there for six hours on a tournament Saturday.

Two pieces stand out for everyday families rather than tournament families. The first is a gym with memberships starting at $55. The second is an on-site day care center, which is a rare thing to find attached to a public recreation asset in Clark County. A parent can drop a younger child at day care, put an older child on a court, and get a workout in without driving to three different places.

The opening runs in three stages. The first event is the Border League's Wooten Classic on October 1. The public soft opening follows on October 5. The grand opening is October 16. So the building hosts a real tournament before the general public ever walks through the front door as customers, which tells you exactly where the operator's priorities sit in the first month.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Start with the weather, because in Southern Nevada the weather is the whole argument. From late May through early October, outdoor youth sports in Clark County are a scheduling nightmare. Practices get pushed to 8 p.m. Games get moved. Kids get pulled off fields for heat. Two hybrid turf indoor fields and four indoor basketball courts take that problem off the table for a chunk of Henderson families.

Then look at the drive. Right now, plenty of Henderson families haul kids across the valley to Summerlin, the northwest, or the southwest for club practice, tournaments, and skills work. That is a 40 to 60 minute drive in each direction on a bad afternoon, several times a week, for years. A 180,000-square-foot facility on St. Rose Parkway pulls a lot of those trips back inside Henderson. Time is the real currency for parents, and this gives some of it back.

There is a household budget angle too. A gym membership starting at $55 is not free, but it is competitive, and it sits inside a building that also handles birthday parties, bowling, laser tag, arcade time, and mini golf. Families in Anthem, Inspirada, and Seven Hills currently spread that spending across four or five different businesses, several of which are a real drive away. Consolidating it saves gas, time, and usually money.

Finally, there is the money coming the other direction. Weekend tournaments are already booked out through the end of 2026. Tournament families do not just show up and leave. They book hotel rooms, eat at local restaurants, buy gas, and shop. That spending lands in Henderson businesses and, through room tax and sales tax, in local public budgets. That is the mechanism the city is counting on to justify $60 million of public money.

It matters for renters and empty nesters too, not just families with kids in club sports. Twenty bowling lanes, a laser tag arena, an arcade, outdoor mini golf, and a restaurant and bar make this a place to go on a Friday night without driving to the Strip or to Town Square. Henderson has a lot of great restaurants and not a lot of large indoor entertainment under one roof. This fills a gap that residents have complained about for years.

There is one more group worth naming. Local coaches, trainers, referees, and youth league organizers are about to have a modern building to work out of. In a valley where a lot of youth sports run out of aging school gyms and church multipurpose rooms, a purpose-built facility with four convertible courts changes what local programs can offer. Expect new leagues, clinics, and camps to appear in Henderson over the next two years because the space finally exists.

Background and History

Henderson has been chasing sports tourism deliberately for more than a decade, and this complex is the newest piece of a long pattern. The city landed the Henderson Silver Knights and built Lifeguard Arena in Water Street District. It landed Dollar Loan Center, now Lee's Family Forum, on the west side of the city. It has courted youth and amateur tournament business on top of the professional teams, because tournament weekends fill hotel rooms in the shoulder seasons when Las Vegas Strip demand softens.

The logic is simple. Henderson cannot out-Strip the Strip. It can, however, be the place a 12-year-old's club volleyball team flies into for a three-day tournament. That family stays two or three nights, eats out six or seven times, and never sets foot in a casino. Cities across the country have run this exact playbook, from Indiana to Florida to Arizona, and the ones with modern multi-court buildings get the bookings.

The St. Rose Parkway corridor was the natural place to put it. That corridor has absorbed enormous residential growth over the past 15 years, from Inspirada and Anthem to the newer product closer to the M Resort. It has hospital campuses, retail, and freeway access from the 15. It has young families with kids in exactly the age brackets that fill courts and turf fields. Building the complex there puts it near both the demand and the highway ramps that out-of-town teams need.

The public-private structure is also part of a broader trend in Clark County. Governments here increasingly split the cost of large amenity projects with private operators rather than building and running them alone. Clark County just approved a $40 million recreation center at Echo Trail Park in the southwest valley. The Stadium Authority approved $75 million in public money toward a $158 million Allegiant Stadium upgrade. Public dollars going into buildings that private partners help run is now the standard model here, not the exception.

The design choices tell you the operator has done this before. Convertible courts are the difference between a building that hosts one sport and a building that hosts several. Four basketball courts that flip into eight volleyball courts means a single weekend can hold a large basketball tournament or a very large volleyball tournament without rebuilding anything. Hybrid turf indoor fields extend that to soccer, flag football, lacrosse, and training academies. Every added configuration is another set of weekends the building can sell, and that is how a project like this pays for itself.

What Happens Next

The near-term calendar is set. The Border League's Wooten Classic runs on October 1 as the first event in the building. The public soft opening is October 5, which is when regular Henderson residents get their first real look inside. The grand opening follows on October 16 with the full facility running.

After that, watch three things. First, watch whether the weekend booking calendar stays full into 2027. Right now it is sold out through the end of 2026, which is a strong start, but a complex like this lives or dies on repeat bookings from tournament organizers. Second, watch how the city and KemperSports handle the balance between paid tournament weekends and open community access. A building booked solid on Saturdays is great for hotel tax and awful for a Henderson family that just wants court time.

Third, watch traffic and parking on St. Rose Parkway during tournament weekends. A 180,000-square-foot venue drawing hundreds of families at once puts real pressure on nearby intersections. If you live in the immediate area, the first few big weekends in October and November will tell you a lot about what the next several years feel like. Nearby homeowners should pay attention early, because traffic mitigation is easiest to push for in the first year.

Longer term, the question is whether the $60 million in public money produces the return Henderson is projecting. That answer will not be clear for two or three years. The measurable pieces are hotel room nights on tournament weekends, sales tax from nearby restaurants and retail, and how many local residents actually buy gym memberships and party packages. If those numbers land, expect Henderson to look for a second project like this. If they do not, expect a much more cautious City Council the next time a public-private partnership comes up for a vote.

Ryan's Take

Buyers ask me about amenities constantly, and they almost never say the word "amenities." What they actually say is "what is there to do out here with the kids?" For years, the honest answer in the far southeast valley was that you have great parks, great trails, and a long drive to almost everything else. This changes that answer.

I do not think a single building moves home prices in Henderson by some measurable percentage, and anyone telling you otherwise is selling something. What it does is shorten the list of reasons a family says no to a neighborhood. When a buyer relocating from California is comparing a home in Inspirada against a home in Summerlin, and the Henderson home is now nine minutes from four indoor courts, turf fields, bowling, and a gym with day care, that is a real point in the column. Demand is built out of small advantages stacked on top of each other, and this is a big small advantage.

The part I will be watching closely is resale in the immediate St. Rose corridor. Proximity to a busy venue cuts both ways. Two miles away it is a lifestyle selling point. Directly adjacent, on a street that absorbs tournament traffic, it can be a question a buyer asks. If you own near the site, know which side of that line you are on before you list.

The broader lesson for Clark County homeowners is that amenity spending is now one of the clearest signals of where a city is putting its long-term bets. Henderson did not spend $60 million on the far southeast corridor by accident. Clark County did not approve a $40 million recreation center in the southwest by accident either. When a government commits that kind of capital to a specific area, it is telling you where it expects population and household growth over the next decade. That is useful information whether you are buying, selling, or just deciding how long to stay.

What You Can Do

If you have kids in sports, the practical move is to get on the calendar early. The complex is at 3375 St. Rose Parkway in Henderson, and the public soft opening is October 5 with the grand opening on October 16. Weekend tournament slots are already booked through the end of 2026, so if you want court or field time for a local team, league, or clinic, contact the operator now rather than in January. Early users tend to get the best recurring time slots, and those slots stick.

If you are shopping for a gym, price the $55 starting membership against what you pay now, and factor in the day care. Parents of young kids routinely pay for a gym they cannot actually use because there is nowhere to put the toddler. A membership attached to on-site child care is a different product than a membership alone, and it is worth comparing honestly.

If you live in the immediate area, go to the Henderson City Council meetings and watch the traffic and parking conversation in the first six months. Public comment actually matters most in that early window, when the city is still adjusting signal timing, signage, and event-day management. Show up once in October or November and you will have far more influence than a complaint filed in 2028.

And if you are thinking about buying or selling anywhere along the St. Rose Parkway corridor, in Anthem, Inspirada, Seven Hills, or Green Valley, this is now part of the conversation. Know how close a given home is to the site, what the drive time actually looks like on a tournament Saturday, and how to talk about it with a buyer.
