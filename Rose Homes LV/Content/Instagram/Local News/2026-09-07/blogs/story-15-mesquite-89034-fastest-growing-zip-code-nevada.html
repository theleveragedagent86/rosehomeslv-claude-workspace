Mesquite ZIP Code 89034 Named the Fastest-Growing in Nevada

The fastest-growing ZIP code in Nevada is not in Summerlin, not in Henderson, and not anywhere in the Las Vegas Valley. It is 89034, a slice of Mesquite sitting north of Interstate 15 about 80 miles from the Strip, and it added roughly 73.3 residents per 1,000 people between January and May of 2026.

That number comes from a MovingPlace report that studied about 5 million moves across the country. On a per-capita basis, no other Nevada ZIP code grew faster during that stretch. For a state where almost every growth headline is about Las Vegas, North Las Vegas, or Reno, that is a genuinely surprising result.

It is also a real estate story, and a useful one. People do not move 80 miles up the freeway by accident. They do it because something about where they were living stopped working for them, and something about the new place solved it.

What Happened

MovingPlace released a report in late August 2026 that ranked ZIP codes by how many new residents they gained per 1,000 existing residents. The company built the ranking from about 5 million moves logged from January through May of 2026. Instead of counting raw bodies, which always favors big metros, the report measured growth as a rate. That is why a small town can beat a giant suburb.

ZIP code 89034 came out on top for Nevada with about 73.3 new residents per 1,000. The ZIP covers the portion of Mesquite north of Interstate 15. That is the newer side of town, where much of the residential development of the last two decades has gone up. It is golf course country, desert view lots, and a mix of full-time residents and part-year snowbirds.

The report credited a familiar list of reasons. Warm climate. Golf and outdoor recreation. Newer residential development. And a lower-density alternative to Las Vegas, which is the polite way of saying it feels less crowded than the valley does right now.

Nevada's top five also included 89113 in southwest Las Vegas and 89501 in downtown Reno. Those two make more intuitive sense. Southwest Las Vegas has been one of the busiest new-home corridors in Clark County, and downtown Reno has been adding apartments and converting older buildings for years. Seeing them on the list is not a shock. Seeing Mesquite beat both of them is.

One more detail matters for perspective. No Nevada ZIP code cracked the national top 50. So while 89034 is the fastest-growing ZIP in the state, it is not one of the fastest-growing in the country. Nevada is still growing, but the explosive nationwide growth right now is happening elsewhere. That is worth keeping in mind before anyone declares Mesquite the next boomtown.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Here is the part that should get your attention if you live in the Las Vegas Valley. Mesquite is still Clark County. The people moving there are not leaving Nevada, and many of them are not leaving the county either. They are leaving the valley.

When a household decides that an 80-mile drive is a fair trade for a house they can afford, that tells you something about the pressure inside the valley. Buyers have been feeling squeezed by prices, by payments, and by how much house their money actually buys in Summerlin, Green Valley, or Southern Highlands. Mesquite becomes the release valve.

This matters to sellers too. If you own a home in the valley and you are wondering who your buyer is, part of the answer is that a slice of your buyer pool has quietly decided to look somewhere else. That does not mean the valley is in trouble. It means your competition for buyers is wider than it used to be, and it now includes towns most Las Vegas sellers never think about.

For retirees and near-retirees, the math is even more direct. If you are 62 years old, own a home in Henderson with real equity, and no longer need to be near a job or a school, moving to Mesquite can turn a chunk of that equity into cash while lowering your monthly cost of living. Golf is out your door. The weather is close to what you already know, just a bit warmer in summer. And Las Vegas is still there when you want a show, a doctor, or an airport.

There is a commuter angle as well, though a smaller one. Mesquite is not a realistic daily commute to the Strip or the Harry Reid airport area. It is a place for people who work remotely, work locally in Mesquite, or do not work at all. If your job requires you in the valley five days a week, this trend is not for you, and that limit is a big reason Mesquite grows fast in percentage terms but stays small in raw numbers.

Renters in the valley feel a version of this too. When ownership feels out of reach in Clark County, some households stop trying to buy where they already live and start looking for the cheapest door into ownership anywhere in the region. Mesquite is one of those doors. Every household that walks through it is one less bidder on a starter home in North Las Vegas or Spring Valley, which is a small but real relief on entry-level competition.

And for anyone who owns rental property in the valley, this is a reminder that your tenant pool has options. The households most likely to leave are the ones with the most flexibility, which often means retirees on fixed incomes and remote workers. Both groups tend to be stable, long-term tenants. Losing them to a town 80 miles away is not a crisis, but it is a reason to pay attention to what your renewal terms look like.

Background and History

Mesquite has been the quiet third act of Clark County growth for a long time. It sits in the far northeast corner of the county, right up against the Arizona line, along the Virgin River. For most of the twentieth century it was a small farming and ranching town. Then the freeway came through, then the casinos, then the golf courses, and then the retirees.

The pattern that built Mesquite is the same one that built Boulder City and, in a different way, Laughlin. A small Nevada town near a state line gets a highway, gets gaming, and then discovers that its weather and its land prices are worth more than its casinos. Golf development followed in the 1990s and 2000s, and the area north of Interstate 15 filled in with newer subdivisions aimed at buyers who wanted a house with a view and a low-maintenance yard.

What changed recently is the valley, not Mesquite. Over the last several years, Las Vegas home prices climbed hard, mortgage rates climbed with them, and the monthly payment on a typical valley home moved well beyond what a lot of Nevada households earn. Buyers who used to have three or four neighborhoods on their list started widening the search. First to the edges of the valley. Then past them.

Remote work accelerated all of it. A meaningful share of Clark County jobs can now be done from a home office at least part of the week. Once the daily drive stops mattering, distance turns from a dealbreaker into a preference. That is how a town 80 miles up the freeway ends up on a statewide leaderboard.

It is also worth remembering how per-capita growth statistics behave. Mesquite's population base is small compared to Las Vegas. A few hundred new households in 89034 moves the rate dramatically. The same few hundred households arriving in 89113 or 89052 would barely register. The report is measuring real movement, but the size of the number is partly a function of the size of the town.

The Interstate 15 corridor deserves its own mention here. That freeway is the spine of the whole story. It connects Mesquite to Las Vegas in one direction and to St. George, Utah in the other, and St. George has been one of the fastest-growing small metros in the country for years. Mesquite sits between two growth engines with a state line on each side, and that position is a big part of why it keeps drawing people who want cheaper land without giving up access to a city.

Gaming built the town's tax base, but housing is what has been carrying its growth for the last twenty years. That is a meaningful shift. A town that grows on casino jobs looks very different from a town that grows on retirees and remote workers who bring their income with them. The second model puts more money into local restaurants, medical offices, and services, and less into shift work. It also makes the local economy more sensitive to what happens in Las Vegas home prices than to what happens on a casino floor.

What Happens Next

Watch three things over the next year. The first is whether Mesquite's growth rate holds up in the second half of 2026 and into 2027. The MovingPlace figure covers January through May, which includes the tail of snowbird season. A single five-month window is a snapshot, not a trend line. If the same ZIP shows up on the next report, that is a trend.

The second is homebuilding. Fast in-migration with limited new construction pushes prices up quickly in a small market. If builders respond with new phases north of Interstate 15, prices stay reasonable and the growth can continue. If they do not, Mesquite starts to look expensive relative to what people moved there to escape, and the flow slows down on its own.

The third is infrastructure and services. Small towns that grow fast run into the same list every time. Water. Roads. Medical care. Schools. Mesquite draws on Virgin River water rights and Clark County systems, and city leaders have talked about capacity for years. Any household considering the move should read the city's current planning documents rather than assuming the town scales up automatically.

For the Las Vegas Valley, expect the outflow to stay modest but persistent. Southwest Las Vegas showing up on the same top-five list is a reminder that the valley is still absorbing plenty of newcomers. The story is not that people are fleeing Las Vegas. It is that Nevada's growth is spreading out, and the outer edges are getting a bigger share than they used to.

Ryan's Take

I read this report as a pricing story wearing a lifestyle costume. Yes, the golf is great and the weather is warm. But warm weather and golf were available in Mesquite ten years ago too, and it was not the fastest-growing ZIP in Nevada then. What changed is what a house costs in the valley, and what a mortgage payment does to a household budget at current rates.

When I sit with buyers who are stretching, the conversation almost always comes down to one question. What are you willing to trade? Some trade square footage. Some trade a newer build for an older one. Some trade a shorter commute. And a growing number are willing to trade proximity to Las Vegas entirely, because for their stage of life, proximity was never the point.

My honest advice on Mesquite is that it is a genuinely good fit for a specific person and a poor fit for everyone else. If you are retired or remote, if you like quiet, if you golf, and if your equity goes further there, it can be a very smart move. If you have kids in Clark County School District, a job in the valley, or a specialist you see monthly, the 80 miles will wear you down faster than you expect. Rent there for a season before you sell anything.

What You Can Do

If Mesquite is on your list, start with a real visit in July. Anyone can love the place in March. Summer in the far northeast corner of Clark County runs hot, and the honest test of whether you can live somewhere is whether you like it on its worst day. Drive the loop from the valley at least twice so the distance stops being an abstraction.

Next, do the money side properly before you fall in love with a floor plan. Pull a current payoff or equity estimate on your valley home. Get a real loan estimate for a Mesquite purchase, including homeowners insurance and any HOA dues, since many of the golf community homes carry them. Compare total monthly cost, not just purchase price. That comparison is where the decision actually gets made.

Finally, if you are staying in the valley, use this report as market information rather than a reason to worry. Watch what is happening in 89113 in southwest Las Vegas, which also made Nevada's top five. Growth patterns inside the valley tell you where demand is concentrating, and that is useful whether you are buying, selling, or just deciding when to refinance. City of Mesquite planning agendas and Clark County demographic updates are both public, and both are worth a look if you want the raw numbers behind the headline.

One more practical step. If you are selling a valley home to fund a move like this, sequence it carefully. Sell first and you have cash and leverage, but you need somewhere to live during the gap. Buy first and you carry two payments until the valley home closes. Neither answer is automatically right, and the correct order depends on your equity, your timeline, and how much uncertainty you can stomach. Talk that through with an agent before you list anything, because reversing the decision later is expensive.
