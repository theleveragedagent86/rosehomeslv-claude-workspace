About 225 Southern Nevada Teachers Trained on Classroom AI at a Green Valley Ranch AI Skills Jam

Roughly 225 Southern Nevada teachers spent a full day at Green Valley Ranch Resort in Henderson learning how to use artificial intelligence in their classrooms. The event, called an "AI Skills Jam," was hosted by OpenAI Academy and Legacy Traditional Schools on September 4, 2026, and it was part of a national K-12 training series.

If you have a child in a Southern Nevada school, this matters to you. AI tools are already showing up in lesson planning, grading, and the messages you get from your kid's teacher. Training days like this one decide how those tools get used, and how carefully.

The short version is that this was not a sales pitch dressed up as professional development, at least not according to the teachers who were there. Educators in the room pushed back on the idea that AI is going to replace them. That pushback is the most interesting part of the whole story.

What Happened at the Green Valley Ranch AI Skills Jam

OpenAI Academy and Legacy Traditional Schools brought about 225 Southern Nevada educators together at Green Valley Ranch Resort in Henderson for a day-long training event on September 4, 2026. The organizers called it an "AI Skills Jam." It was one stop in a national series aimed at K-12 teachers, not a one-off Nevada experiment.

The sessions were hands-on. Teachers worked with tools like ChatGPT and Codex and practiced applying them to the parts of the job that eat the most time outside of teaching. That included building lesson plans, working through grading, and writing communication that goes home to families. Those three tasks are where most teachers lose their evenings and weekends, so it is not a surprise that the training started there.

What made the day notable was the reaction in the room. Teachers did not sit quietly and nod. First-grade teacher Madison Bailey pushed back on the idea that AI could take her place, saying she is still the expert in her field. That is a direct challenge to the loudest version of the AI story, the one that says software is going to make classroom teachers optional.

Instructional coach Adrienne Avelar framed the goal differently. She said the point is making sure students are still learning. That is a simple line, but it is the whole ballgame. A tool that saves a teacher four hours a week is a win. A tool that quietly lets a student skip the thinking part of an assignment is a loss. Avelar's framing puts the burden on the adults in the building to tell the difference.

It is worth being precise about the two tools named in the coverage, because they do different jobs. ChatGPT is the general purpose assistant most people have already heard of. It drafts text, summarizes material, answers questions, and can be pushed to generate practice problems or reading passages at a given grade level. Codex is oriented toward code. In a K-12 setting that is less about teaching students to program and more about giving teachers a way to build small tools, automate repetitive work, or handle spreadsheets and data without needing an IT department.

The format also matters. A day-long event at a resort with hundreds of attendees is a different animal than a 45-minute after-school in-service. Teachers had time to actually open the tools, break things, and ask questions. Anyone who has sat through a rushed technology rollout knows that the difference between a demo and real practice time is usually the difference between a tool that gets used and a tool that gets forgotten by October.

FOX5 Vegas covered the event and reported the attendance figure, the host organizations, the tools covered, and the teacher reactions described above. Details beyond what FOX5 reported, including any district-level agreements, costs, or follow-up sessions, were not part of that coverage. Anything you read elsewhere claiming a specific Clark County School District policy came out of this event should be treated carefully until a district source confirms it.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Southern Nevada is home to one of the largest school systems in the country. When 225 educators in this valley get trained on the same set of tools on the same day, that reaches a lot of classrooms very quickly. Even if you never hear the words "Skills Jam" again, you may notice the effects in your child's backpack this fall.

Start with the parent-facing side. If AI is helping draft the newsletter, the progress update, or the note about a missing assignment, those messages may get more frequent and more polished. That is mostly good. More communication from school is something parents ask for constantly. But it also means you should read those messages the same way you always have, and ask questions when something does not match what you are seeing at home.

Then there is the grading question, which is where parents tend to get uneasy fast. Nobody wants a machine assigning a grade that follows a kid into the next school year. The training covered grading as one use case, and the teachers in the room made clear they see themselves as the final call. That is the standard parents should expect and should feel comfortable asking about at conferences.

There is also a fairness angle that hits Clark County harder than it hits a smaller district. Our schools serve families across a huge range of incomes, home internet situations, and home languages. A tool that helps a teacher translate a note into a family's home language is a real equalizer. A tool that assumes every student has a laptop and stable Wi-Fi at home is not. How districts handle that gap will shape whether AI narrows or widens the differences between schools in Summerlin, in North Las Vegas, and in the older neighborhoods east of the Strip.

Student data privacy deserves its own mention. Any time a school tool touches student names, grades, or behavior notes, there are rules about where that information can go and who can see it. Most parents never think about this until something leaks. The right question is not whether teachers are allowed to use AI, it is what information they are allowed to put into it. A teacher using a chatbot to brainstorm a science unit is very different from a teacher pasting a roster of student names and scores into an outside service.

Finally, teacher retention. Southern Nevada has fought staffing shortages for years, and burnout is a big piece of that. If AI genuinely gives teachers back time on lesson prep and paperwork, that is not a small thing. It could be one of the more practical retention tools the region has tried.

Retention connects straight back to housing, which is the part of this I watch most closely. Stable, well-staffed schools are one of the top reasons families pick one Clark County neighborhood over another. When a campus loses teachers every year, parents notice, ratings slip, and buyer interest follows. Anything that keeps good teachers in Southern Nevada classrooms has a long tail that eventually reaches home values, even if it takes a few years to show up.

Background and History

AI did not arrive in schools this month. Generative tools went mainstream with students first, which is why the early conversation in districts across the country was mostly defensive. The question was how to catch cheating, whether to block certain sites on school networks, and how to rewrite academic honesty policies. That reactive phase dominated the first couple of years.

What happened at Green Valley Ranch reflects the next phase. Instead of asking how to keep AI out, the training asked how teachers should use it themselves. That is a meaningful shift. It moves the adults from playing defense to setting the terms, which is generally how technology gets absorbed into a profession without wrecking it.

The choice of hosts also says something. OpenAI Academy is the education and training arm tied to the company behind ChatGPT, and Legacy Traditional Schools is a charter network with campuses in the Southwest, including Nevada. A training run by a tool maker and a school operator together is going to be enthusiastic about the tools. That is not automatically a problem, but it is the context, and it is why the skepticism voiced by teachers in the room is worth taking seriously.

There is a longer history here too. Every generation of school technology arrived with the same promise and the same disappointment. Overhead projectors, computer labs, smart boards, one-to-one laptop programs, and the sudden all-remote year of 2020 all came with claims that teaching was about to be transformed. Some of it stuck. A lot of it ended up as expensive equipment in a closet. Teachers who have been in Southern Nevada classrooms for fifteen years have watched this cycle several times, and that experience is exactly why healthy skepticism showed up in the room.

It is also worth noting where this happened. Green Valley Ranch Resort sits in the Green Valley area of Henderson, which has become a default location for regional conferences and large professional gatherings. Hosting 225 educators there instead of in a school gym signals that organizers wanted this treated as a serious professional event, not a quick afternoon in-service.

What Happens Next

The most immediate thing to watch is whether the 225 teachers who attended actually use what they learned. Professional development has a long history of great days followed by nothing. The tell will be simple and visible from home: does the volume and quality of teacher communication change, and do assignments start to look different?

The second thing to watch is policy. Individual teachers experimenting is one thing. Formal district or network guidance on what AI may and may not be used for is another. Parents should look for written policies covering grading, student data, and what happens when a student uses AI on an assignment. If your school has not published anything, that is a reasonable question to raise at a parent night.

Third, watch for more events like this one. The AI Skills Jam was described as part of a national K-12 series, which means Southern Nevada is unlikely to be a one-time stop. Whether the next round reaches traditional public school teachers at the same scale it reached this group is the open question.

There is a practical follow-up question as well. Training a teacher on a tool is only useful if the tool is available on the school network and approved for use with school accounts. Plenty of districts block the exact software their teachers just got trained on. Watch whether access catches up to training over the next few months, because that gap is where most technology rollouts quietly die.

Finally, expect the debate to get louder before it gets quieter. Teachers pushing back at a training hosted by an AI company is a preview of the conversations coming to school board meetings and union discussions. This story is early, not finished.

Ryan's Take

I pay close attention to school stories because schools drive housing decisions in this valley more than almost anything else. Buyers ask me about school ratings before they ask me about square footage. Anything that changes how Southern Nevada classrooms operate eventually shows up in where families choose to live.

My honest read on this one is that the teachers came out looking better than the technology did. Madison Bailey saying she is still the expert in her field, and Adrienne Avelar keeping the focus on whether students are actually learning, is exactly the posture I would want from the people teaching my kids. The tools are going to keep improving whether anyone likes it or not. What determines the outcome is whether the adults in the room stay in charge of them, and this group sounds like it intends to.

For families weighing neighborhoods right now, I would not treat AI training as a reason to move or not move. It is too early. What I would do is ask specific questions at your next school visit. How does this campus handle AI in grading? Is there a written policy? Schools that answer clearly are usually well run in other ways too, and that is the signal worth acting on.

What You Can Do

If you are a parent in Clark County or Henderson, the most useful thing you can do is ask direct questions at your next conference or back to school night. Ask whether your child's teacher uses AI tools, and for what. Ask specifically about grading. Most teachers will be happy to explain, and the answer tells you a lot about how thoughtful the campus is.

Ask to see any written policy your school or district has published on AI use by staff and by students. If nothing exists yet, ask when something is coming. Written policy is what protects both students and teachers when something goes wrong, and parent pressure is usually what gets it written.

Talk to your kids about it too. The line between using a tool to understand something and using a tool to avoid understanding it is fuzzy for adults, so it is definitely fuzzy for a middle schooler. A short conversation at the dinner table about when AI helps and when it cheats you out of learning is worth more than any school policy.

If you are a teacher rather than a parent, the practical move is to start small and start with the tasks that do not touch student data. Draft a newsletter. Build a rubric. Rewrite a reading passage at three different levels. Get comfortable with what the tool is good at before you let it near anything that affects a grade. That is roughly the order the training itself followed, and it is the order that keeps you out of trouble.

And if you want a voice in the bigger decisions, school board meetings are open to the public and public comment is available at them. AI policy is heading toward those agendas. Showing up before the decision is made is far more effective than complaining after. Local coverage from outlets like FOX5 Vegas is also worth following, because most of these changes get reported at the event level long before they get formalized into policy.
