Henderson Planning Commission Backs a 276-Unit Apartment Project Next to M Resort

The Henderson Planning Commission has recommended approval of a 276-unit apartment community on a vacant 9.1-acre lot at the southwest corner of Volunteer Boulevard and Gilespie Street, just south of the M Resort Spa Casino. The project is called Volunteer and Gilespie Multifamily, and it would put four five-story buildings on a piece of ground that has sat empty while everything around it filled in.

If you live anywhere near St. Rose Parkway, this one matters. It is 276 new rental doors in a part of Henderson where renters have very few options and where almost every new rooftop over the last decade has been a for-sale house. It also tells you something about where the city thinks density belongs.

What Happened

The Henderson Planning Commission voted to recommend approval of the Volunteer and Gilespie Multifamily project, according to NVBEX, which reported the item on September 1, 2026. The site is a vacant 9.1-acre parcel at the southwest corner of Volunteer Boulevard and Gilespie Street. That location sits immediately south of the M Resort Spa Casino, in the stretch of Henderson that most people navigate by the St. Rose Parkway interchange.

The plan calls for 276 apartment units spread across four buildings. Each building is five stories and stands 59.6 feet tall. There is also an 8,300-square-foot clubhouse. Add it all up and the project totals 328,800 square feet of building area on the site.

Amenities in the plan include a pool, a dog park, a sport court, and a multi-use trail running through the property. That amenity list is fairly standard for new suburban garden and mid-rise apartment communities in Southern Nevada right now, and it tells you the developer is targeting the same renter who would otherwise be shopping a newer rental house in Anthem or Inspirada.

Two technical pieces of this application are worth understanding, because they are the parts that actually required a vote. First, the land carried no zoning designation at all. That happens more often than people expect on parcels that were annexed or held raw for years without a specific development plan attached. Because there was no designation on the books, the developer had to request one, and the request was for High Density Residential. Second, the applicant received an administrative adjustment that reduced the required parking from 469 spaces to 422 spaces, a cut of 47 stalls.

It is also important to be precise about what the commission did. A planning commission recommendation is not a final approval. The commission reviews the site plan, the zoning request, and the technical adjustments, then forwards a recommendation. The Henderson City Council is the body that takes the final action on items like a zoning designation. So the accurate way to describe where this stands is that it cleared its first major public hurdle.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Start with the renters. Southern Nevada rents have cooled off compared to the wild run of a few years ago, but Henderson is still one of the most expensive rental submarkets in the valley. The reason is simple supply and demand. Henderson has terrific schools, low crime numbers relative to the rest of the metro, and a huge share of newer housing stock. What it does not have, in the south end especially, is a deep supply of apartments. When 276 units come online in a submarket like that, renters get something they have not had much of lately, which is choice.

Now think about the people who already own a home nearby. Density next door makes homeowners nervous, and I understand why. The honest answer is that the effect is mixed and it depends heavily on execution. A well-built, professionally managed, amenity-rich apartment community with a pool, a dog park, and a trail generally does not hurt nearby single-family values. What hurts values is deferred maintenance, chronic vacancy, and parking spillover onto residential streets. That is why the parking reduction from 469 spaces to 422 is the detail I would watch most closely if I lived within a few blocks of this site.

Then there is traffic. Volunteer Boulevard and Gilespie Street feed into a corridor that already carries resort traffic, commuter traffic heading to the 15, and everyone driving to the shopping and medical uses along St. Rose Parkway. Adding 276 households means adding a few hundred more vehicle trips at peak hours. It is not catastrophic on its own. It is also not nothing, and it stacks with every other approval in the area.

Finally, consider the renter-to-buyer pipeline, which is the piece almost nobody talks about. A large share of the people who buy homes in Henderson rented in Henderson first. They lease an apartment, they figure out which schools they like, they learn which commute they can tolerate, and then they buy within a few miles. When a city adds quality rental product, it is quietly building its own future buyer pool. That is a real, if slow, benefit to the neighborhoods around this site.

There is one more group affected here, and it is the one people forget. Local employers benefit when housing gets built near jobs. The resort corridor, the medical offices along St. Rose Parkway, and the retail centers in south Henderson all hire from a labor pool that has to live somewhere. When that housing does not exist nearby, employees commute in from North Las Vegas or the far southwest, which means longer drives, more turnover, and more cars on the same roads at the same times. Housing near jobs is not just a renter issue. It is a traffic issue and a hiring issue too.

And for anyone tracking property taxes, more assessed value on a parcel that currently generates very little is a net positive for the local tax base. Vacant land is taxed on the land alone. A 328,800-square-foot improvement changes that math substantially, and those dollars flow into the same pool that funds schools, roads, and public safety across Clark County.

Background and History

The St. Rose Parkway corridor has been one of the most consistently active development areas in Clark County for roughly twenty years. It started as the connective tissue between the 15, the 215, and the master planned communities pushing south and east. Then the hospitals came. Then the retail came. Then Inspirada and Anthem filled in with thousands of houses. The M Resort anchored the western end of the corridor and gave it a landmark.

What did not keep pace was multifamily. For most of that build-out, the economics favored for-sale housing. Land in south Henderson was expensive, builders could sell a house at a strong margin, and apartment developers largely went elsewhere in the valley. The result is a submarket with a lot of households and comparatively few apartment doors. That imbalance is exactly why a 9.1-acre infill parcel with no zoning designation was still sitting vacant in 2026 while everything around it got built.

The zoning piece here is a small window into how Henderson grew. Land that was carried without a designation typically means the city never had a reason to assign one, because no one had brought forward a concrete plan. When a developer finally does, the designation request becomes the moment the community gets to weigh in. High Density Residential is the category that makes a five-story, 276-unit project possible. Without it, the site would be limited to something far smaller.

Parking standards are the other piece of history worth knowing. Cities across the country, including several in Southern Nevada, have been revisiting parking minimums that were written decades ago for a very different set of household habits. Requiring 469 stalls for 276 units works out to roughly 1.7 spaces per unit. The adjustment down to 422 is closer to 1.5 per unit. Whether that is right depends entirely on the unit mix and on how many residents in this location will realistically own two cars. In a car-dependent corridor like St. Rose Parkway, that is a fair question for neighbors to ask.

It is also worth noting how the building form itself has shifted. Five stories at 59.6 feet is taller than the classic three-story Las Vegas garden apartment that dominated the valley for thirty years, but it is nowhere near a high rise. That middle height is where a lot of new Southern Nevada multifamily has landed, because it lets a developer get real unit counts out of a modest parcel without the cost of concrete and steel construction. When you see four five-story buildings on nine acres, you are looking at the current standard playbook for infill land that has become too expensive to build on at low density.

Finally, remember the context of the parcel itself. Nine acres of vacant, unzoned land inside a fully built-out corridor is a rare thing in 2026. Southern Nevada has a genuine land supply problem, since the vast majority of the ground around the valley is federally controlled and only released in specific parcels. That scarcity is a big part of why land like this eventually gets used at the highest density a city will allow. It is not an accident and it is not going to reverse.

What Happens Next

The next formal step is City Council action. The Planning Commission recommendation moves the application forward, and the council takes up the zoning designation and the related entitlements. Anyone who wants to speak for or against the project gets another chance at that hearing, and public comment at the council level carries real weight on projects like this one.

After entitlements, the path is the usual one. The developer works through civil and building plan review with the city, pulls permits, and lines up financing. Construction on a four-building, five-story wood-frame or podium-style community of this size typically runs well over a year once dirt moves, and that is before lease-up. So even in a smooth scenario, this is not a 2026 delivery.

The thing I would actually watch is whether the project pencils. Multifamily construction financing has been the tightest part of the real estate capital markets, and a meaningful number of entitled apartment projects across the valley have been approved and then sat. Entitlement is permission, not a promise. If you see site work start, the project is real. If the parcel is still vacant a year from now, that is a financing story, not a zoning story.

There is a second thing to watch, and it is the unit mix. The public reporting covers the unit count, the building count, the height, and the clubhouse size, but not the breakdown of studios, one-bedrooms, and two-bedrooms. That mix drives everything downstream. A community weighted toward two-bedroom units attracts roommates and small families, which means more cars and more kids in nearby schools. A community weighted toward studios and one-bedrooms attracts singles and couples, which means fewer cars and almost no school impact. When the council packet comes out, that table is the most informative page in it.

Ryan's Take

I have watched south Henderson go from empty desert to one of the most desirable addresses in the state, and the thing that has never quite balanced out is rental supply. Buyers who want Henderson can find a house. Renters who want Henderson often end up in Green Valley, in the southwest valley, or across the county line in a rental house that was bought by an investor. This project is a direct answer to that gap, and I think 276 doors in this specific location will lease fast.

For homeowners nearby, my honest read is that this is more upside than downside, with one caveat. New, amenitized apartments next to a resort corridor tend to bring in working professionals and empty nesters, not the tenant profile people fear. They also add daytime population that supports the retail and restaurants those homeowners use. The caveat is parking. If 422 stalls turns out to be short, the overflow ends up on public streets, and that is the kind of thing that genuinely annoys neighbors for years. If I were speaking at the council hearing, that is the single question I would ask.

The bigger picture is that Henderson cannot keep growing on single-family lots alone. The land is not there and the prices show it. Cities that add rental supply in the right places tend to keep their for-sale market healthier, because renters are not forced to bid on entry-level houses just to have somewhere to live. I would rather see 276 apartments here than see 276 more households competing for the same handful of starter homes in Anthem.

What You Can Do

If you live near Volunteer Boulevard and Gilespie Street, the most useful thing you can do is track the City Council agenda. Henderson posts council and planning agendas online ahead of each meeting, and the packet for a project like this includes the site plan, the elevations, and the staff analysis. Reading the actual packet takes twenty minutes and puts you ahead of ninety percent of the people arguing about it on social media.

If you have a concern, be specific and be early. General opposition to apartments rarely changes an outcome. A pointed question about the parking reduction, about how construction traffic will be routed, about lighting spill onto adjacent lots, or about the trail connection sometimes does. Conditions get added to approvals all the time when a resident raises something concrete that staff had not fully addressed.

It also helps to show up in person, or at least submit a written comment. Planning and council staff log every comment that comes in, and written comments become part of the record even if you cannot make the meeting. If you are organizing with neighbors, one well-written letter signed by twenty households lands harder than twenty separate one-line emails. Include your cross streets so staff can see exactly how close you are to the site.

If you are a renter, put this on your radar for lease timing down the road. New communities almost always run aggressive move-in specials during lease-up. And if you are an owner in the area wondering how added density changes your position, the answer depends on your street, your product type, and your timeline, not on the headline.
