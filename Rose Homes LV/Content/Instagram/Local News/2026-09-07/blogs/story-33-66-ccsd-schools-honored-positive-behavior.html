66 CCSD Schools Honored for Student Behavior Programs, Up From 41 Last Year

Sixty-six Clark County School District schools were recognized this year for their work on positive student behavior, up from 41 schools in 2025. The recognition came from the University of Nevada, Reno's Nevada Multi-Tiered System of Supports Technical Assistance Center.

That is a jump of 25 schools in a single year. Superintendent Jhone Ebert said the district is seeing fewer suspensions, fewer behavior incidents, and more students reporting that they feel safe at school.

If you have kids in CCSD, or if you are shopping for a home and trying to figure out which schools are actually improving, this is one of the more useful pieces of information to come out of the district this year. School climate is hard to measure from the outside. This is one of the few public lists that tries.

What Happened

The Nevada Multi-Tiered System of Supports Technical Assistance Center, which is housed at the University of Nevada, Reno, recognized 66 Clark County schools for their positive behavior work. The center evaluates and supports schools statewide on how they build school climate, handle behavior, and support students who need extra help.

The recognition is tiered. Eleven CCSD schools reached the top Diamond level. Ten earned Platinum. Eighteen earned Gold. Seven earned Silver. Twenty earned Bronze. Together that adds up to the full 66.

Among the eleven Diamond schools were Crestwood, Lois Craig, and Summit View Youth Center. That last one is worth pausing on. Summit View Youth Center serves a very different student population than a typical neighborhood elementary school, and the fact that it landed at the top tier says something about how the framework is applied across different settings.

Superintendent Jhone Ebert tied the recognition to measurable changes rather than just paperwork. She pointed to fewer suspensions, fewer behavior incidents overall, and more students saying they feel safe at school. Those are the three outcomes the whole approach is supposed to produce.

It is worth understanding what the tiers actually represent. These are not popularity awards. Schools have to document what they are doing, show they are using data to make decisions, and demonstrate that the practices are in place consistently rather than on paper only. Moving up a tier generally means a campus tightened its systems and can prove it.

Context also matters for the size of this. Clark County School District is one of the largest districts in the country, with hundreds of schools serving hundreds of thousands of students. Sixty-six recognized campuses is a meaningful group and still a minority of the district. The point is the direction of travel, not that the whole district has been transformed.

News 3 Las Vegas reported the recognitions on September 2, 2026, at the very start of the school year. The timing means the work being honored happened during the prior school year, and the schools involved carry that recognition into the current one.

The growth from 41 schools to 66 is the headline number. It reflects both more schools participating and more schools meeting the standards well enough to be recognized at some level. In a district the size of CCSD, moving 25 additional schools into that category in one year is a real operational lift.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Ask any parent in Clark County what they worry about with schools and you will hear the same short list. Class sizes. Teacher turnover. Safety. Behavior. The first two are budget problems. The last two are climate problems, and climate is what this recognition is about.

Suspensions matter more than people realize. A suspended student is a student who is not in class, which hurts that kid academically. It is also a family scrambling for childcare on a school day, which hurts working parents. Fewer suspensions is not a soft metric. It shows up in household schedules and in whether kids stay on track.

Fewer behavior incidents also changes what a normal school day looks like for everyone else. Every disruption costs instructional minutes for the twenty-five other kids in the room. Cut the number of disruptions and you get back real teaching time without adding a single dollar to the budget. That is why climate work tends to show up eventually in academic numbers too.

Students saying they feel safe matters even more. Kids who do not feel safe do not learn well, and they tell their parents. Parent perception of a school drives enrollment decisions, which drives which neighborhoods families want to live in, which eventually drives home values. That chain is not theoretical in Las Vegas. Agents hear it every week.

For homebuyers, this list gives you something concrete to look at beyond a star rating on a real estate website. Those ratings lean heavily on test scores, which correlate strongly with household income and tell you less than people assume about whether a school is well run. A school that has moved up in behavior recognition tiers is a school where adults changed something and it worked.

For current homeowners, an improving neighborhood school is quietly one of the best things that can happen to your property. Buyers with kids screen by school zone before they screen by anything else. When a zoned school gets better, the pool of buyers willing to look at your street gets bigger.

Teachers feel it too, and teacher retention is one of the biggest levers in the whole system. Staff leave schools where behavior is chaotic and stay where it is manageable. A campus that gets its climate under control has an easier time keeping experienced teachers, and experienced teachers are the single biggest in-school factor in how kids do. That is a quiet compounding benefit for the neighborhood.

And for renters and families deciding whether to stay in Clark County at all, this is a counterweight to a lot of negative headlines. CCSD gets criticized constantly, sometimes fairly. A measurable improvement across 66 campuses is a real data point on the other side of the ledger.

Background and History

Multi-tiered systems of support, often shortened to MTSS, are a framework rather than a single program. The basic idea is simple. Most students do fine with good universal practices, meaning clear expectations, consistent routines, and adults who teach behavior the same way they teach math. A smaller group needs targeted help. A very small group needs intensive individual support.

Schools that do this well spend a lot of time on the universal layer. They define what expected behavior looks like in the hallway, the cafeteria, and the classroom. They teach it explicitly rather than assuming kids already know. They track data on incidents and use that data to decide where to focus.

The Nevada Multi-Tiered System of Supports Technical Assistance Center at UNR exists to help schools do that work and to evaluate how well they are doing it. The tiered recognition system, from Bronze up to Diamond, gives schools a ladder to climb rather than a single pass or fail.

CCSD has been under pressure on discipline for years. Behavior incidents, staff safety concerns, and the balance between removing disruptive students and keeping kids in class have all been recurring fights in board meetings and in the press. Districts across the country have struggled with the same tension, and the years following pandemic school closures made it worse almost everywhere.

The inclusion of Summit View Youth Center at the Diamond level is a useful window into how broad the framework is. Summit View serves young people in a secure setting, where behavior support is not an add-on but the core of the work. Being recognized alongside neighborhood elementary schools shows the standards are meant to apply anywhere adults and students share a building.

It also helps to remember why this work got serious attention nationally. For years the default response to misbehavior was removal, and the data eventually showed that removal alone did not improve outcomes for the student or the school. That pushed districts toward frameworks that teach and reinforce behavior instead of only punishing it. Nevada's center is part of that broader shift.

What changed to move 41 schools to 66 is not spelled out in a single sentence, and it is likely a mix of things. More schools adopting the framework, more staff trained, better data collection, and more consistent implementation all play a part. What Happens Next

The near-term question is whether this holds. Recognition programs can produce a one-year bump when attention is high and then slide back when the focus moves elsewhere. The number to watch is next year's count. If it goes from 66 to something higher, the framework has taken root. If it drops back toward the 40s, the gain was situational.

The second question is whether the district publishes the underlying numbers. Ebert pointed to fewer suspensions and fewer behavior incidents. Those are district-level data points that CCSD reports through board presentations and state reporting. Parents who want to verify the trend at their own kid's school should be looking for school-level discipline data, not just the district total.

Third, watch how this connects to the other school safety conversations happening in Clark County right now. CCSD police have been stepping up school zone traffic enforcement. Anti-bullying programming has been rolling through high schools. A behavior framework recognition fits into that same broader push around student wellbeing, and the district tends to talk about these efforts together.

There is also the question of whether the schools that were recognized this year keep their tier or climb. Diamond is the top rung, so those eleven campuses are now defending a standard rather than chasing one. Bronze and Silver schools have the most room to move, and their progress next year is the clearest test of whether the support from the UNR center is actually building capacity.

Finally, expect individual schools to promote this. Diamond and Platinum recognition shows up in school newsletters, on marquees, and in principal messages. That is fair. It is also worth remembering that a Bronze school and a Diamond school are both on the list, and the tier tells you a lot about where a campus actually sits.

Ryan's Take

I look at school data all the time because buyers ask about it constantly. The honest truth is that most of the public school ratings people rely on are blunt instruments. They tell you a lot about the income level of the neighborhood and not much about the quality of the leadership inside the building.

This kind of recognition is different, and I like it for that reason. It measures something a school actually controls. A principal cannot change the median household income of the attendance zone. A principal can absolutely change whether the hallways are calm, whether expectations are consistent, and whether kids feel like adults have their back. When a campus climbs these tiers, that is usually a leadership story.

I also think this matters for how people talk about CCSD. The district takes a beating in conversation, and some of that is earned. But when families write off every school in the valley based on reputation alone, they end up making housing decisions on bad information. There are strong campuses in Clark County, and there are campuses that have gotten noticeably better in the last two years. Blanket judgments cost buyers good options.

Here is the practical version for anyone buying a home in Clark County. Do not make a school decision off a single number on a listing site. Look at whether the zoned school appears on lists like this one, ask which tier, and then go walk the campus during drop-off or pick-up. Five minutes of watching how a school handles a busy morning tells you more than an hour of reading reviews.

What You Can Do

Start by finding out whether your zoned school is on the list and at what tier. School offices and principals will tell you if you ask, and many will mention it in newsletters. If your school is not on the list, that is not automatically a bad sign, but it is a reasonable question to bring to a principal or a school organizational team meeting.

If you want to see the underlying trend, look for discipline and climate data at the school level. Nevada reports school performance information publicly, and CCSD board meetings regularly include discipline data presentations. Board agendas and materials are posted online before meetings, and public comment is open to anyone.

Talk to your kid, too. The single best climate indicator you have access to is whether your own child says they feel comfortable at school, knows which adult they would go to with a problem, and can describe what the school expects of them. Those three answers tell you more than any award list.

Parents can also plug in directly. Most CCSD campuses have a school organizational team, and those teams have real input into school plans and budgets. Behavior and climate work depends heavily on staffing and training, both of which are budget decisions. Showing up to those meetings is the most direct way to influence what happens at your kid's school.

If you are house hunting, build school research into your process early rather than at the end. Confirm the zoned schools for any address you are serious about, since CCSD zones do not always match what a listing says. Then look at climate indicators alongside test scores. A school moving in the right direction is often a better bet than a school coasting on an old reputation.
