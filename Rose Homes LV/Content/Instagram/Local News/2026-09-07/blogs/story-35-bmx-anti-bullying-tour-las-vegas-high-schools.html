Pro BMX Riders Launch a National Anti-Bullying Tour at Las Vegas High Schools

A group of professional BMX riders kicked off a nationwide anti-bullying tour right here in Las Vegas, rolling into high school gyms and parking lots with ramps, stunt bikes, and a two word message: you matter. Clark County got the first stop of a push that is headed to schools across the country.

The riders performed live stunt shows for high school students during the opening weeks of the 2026-27 school year. One of the stops was Veterans Tribute Career and Technical Academy, where rider Oscar Marquez jumped his bike clear over a teacher who volunteered to lie down on the pavement. The kids lost it. That was the point.

If you are a parent in Clark County, this is a story worth two minutes of your time. Bullying is consistently one of the things families worry about most when they pick a school, and by extension when they pick a neighborhood. A program that gets teenagers to actually sit still and listen is not a small thing.

What Happened

Professional BMX riders brought a stunt show and an anti-bullying message to Las Vegas high schools in early September 2026. The Las Vegas Review-Journal covered the launch on September 2. Las Vegas was the first stop of a tour that is designed to travel to high school assemblies nationwide.

The format is simple and it works. The riders set up ramps. They run a live freestyle demonstration. Between tricks, they stop and talk to the students directly about bullying, about mental health, and about the idea that every single kid in that crowd matters to somebody. The performance earns the attention, and then the riders spend that attention on the message.

At Veterans Tribute Career and Technical Academy, rider Oscar Marquez jumped over a teacher as part of the demonstration. That kind of moment is the hook. A teenager who came to the assembly ready to be bored suddenly has a story to tell at lunch, and the message that came with the stunt rides along with it.

Assemblies like this one are usually short, which is part of the design. A high school bell schedule does not have room for a long program, and teenagers do not have patience for one. The riders get a tight window, and they spend it on spectacle and a single clear idea rather than a long list of rules. That focus is why the message travels out of the gym and into the hallway.

The tour is deliberately timed. Organizers aimed at high school assemblies during the first weeks of the school year. That is when school culture gets set. The social groups form, the pecking order gets established, and the freshmen figure out where they fit. Hitting that window is a smarter play than showing up in April after the patterns are already locked in.

Veterans Tribute Career and Technical Academy is a Clark County School District magnet high school focused on career and technical education. Students there study fields like law enforcement, fire science, and health care. It is a school built around students who already have a direction, and it drew one of the first stops on the tour.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Ask any parent shopping for a home in Summerlin, Green Valley, Centennial Hills, or Southern Highlands what they care about, and school quality lands near the top of the list every time. But "school quality" is not just test scores. It is whether a kid feels safe walking into the building on a Monday morning.

Clark County School District is one of the largest school districts in the country. That size brings real advantages, including magnet programs, career academies, and specialty schools that a small district could never afford to run. It also means a single student can feel invisible in a building with a couple thousand classmates. Programs that put a name and a face to the words "you matter" push directly against that.

There is also a straight line between school climate and daily life at home. A student who dreads going to school is a student who is not sleeping well, not eating well, and not learning much. That shows up at the dinner table. Parents feel it long before a report card confirms it.

The neighborhood angle is real too. High schools in Clark County anchor their communities. Friday night games, band competitions, booster clubs, and career academy showcases pull families together across whole zip codes. When a school builds a reputation as a place where kids look out for each other, that reputation follows the attendance zone. Buyers notice. So do sellers who list a home and mention the school by name.

And this one has a little civic pride attached. A national tour picked Las Vegas as the launch city. Our schools get plenty of rough national coverage. Being the first stop for a program built around student dignity is a nice change of pace, and it is worth saying out loud.

Think about the practical side of it too. Clark County families move a lot. Job changes, lease endings, and new construction closings shuffle students between campuses in the middle of the year all the time. A kid who transfers into a high school in October walks into a room where everybody already knows everybody. School culture decides whether that kid gets a seat at the lunch table or eats alone for a semester. In a valley that grows and churns as fast as ours does, that matters more here than it does in a town where nobody ever leaves.

Renters feel this even more sharply than owners. Roughly speaking, a family renting a home in Las Vegas is far more likely to change addresses in a given year than a family that owns, and every move can mean a new attendance zone. If you are one of those families, the culture at the receiving school is the single biggest variable in how the transition goes for your kid. It is worth asking about before you sign a lease, not after.

Background and History

Anti-bullying programming in schools is not new. What has changed over the last decade is the delivery. The old model was a guest speaker at a podium in a gym with bad acoustics, talking to a crowd of teenagers who were mostly looking at the floor. Retention was terrible and everybody knew it.

The newer model uses spectacle first. Skateboarders, BMX riders, motocross athletes, and stunt performers have all become vehicles for school assembly messaging for one simple reason. Students will not tune out a person doing a backflip on a bicycle twenty feet in the air. Once the crowd is genuinely watching, the speaker has earned a few minutes of real attention that a podium never gets.

Clark County has been leaning into school climate work on the district side too. Earlier this month, the Nevada Multi-Tiered System of Supports technical assistance center at the University of Nevada, Reno honored 66 Clark County schools for their positive behavior programs. That is a jump from 41 schools the prior year. The direction is clear. More CCSD campuses are building formal structures around behavior and school culture, not just discipline after the fact.

Career and technical education academies like Veterans Tribute occupy a specific spot in that landscape. These are choice schools. Students apply, families commit to the commute, and the campus tends to build a strong identity around the program. That culture is a real asset, and schools like this often become the test sites for new programming because the buy-in is already there.

It also helps that action sports have deep roots in Southern Nevada. The valley has public skate parks, a long history with BMX and motocross, and open desert riding within a short drive of almost any neighborhood. A stunt bike is not an exotic thing to a Las Vegas teenager. Plenty of them own one. That familiarity is part of why the format lands here, and it is probably part of why organizers chose Las Vegas to launch.

The broader shift worth understanding is that schools stopped treating bullying purely as a discipline problem. For years the response was punitive and reactive. A student reported something, an administrator investigated, and somebody got suspended. What research and practice both pushed districts toward is prevention. Build a culture where the behavior is less likely in the first place, teach students what to do when they see it, and give them a way to report that does not make them a target. Assemblies are only one piece of that, but they are the piece that reaches every student in the building on the same morning.

What Happens Next

The tour continues to other high schools around the country after the Las Vegas launch. Whether additional Clark County campuses get a stop this school year has not been announced in the coverage available. [NOT VERIFIED: additional CCSD stop dates and the full school list]

For families who want to know if their school is getting a visit, the practical move is to watch your school's own communication channels. Individual CCSD high schools post assembly schedules through their websites, their student newsletters, and their social accounts. Assemblies like this are usually announced by the campus itself, not by the district.

The bigger thing to watch is whether the message sticks past the applause. One assembly does not change a school. What changes a school is what teachers, counselors, and student leaders do in the weeks after. If a campus pairs the show with a peer mentoring program, a reporting system students actually trust, or a student-led club, the assembly becomes the kickoff for something. If not, it becomes a fun morning that everyone forgets by October.

There are a few concrete signals to watch on your own campus this fall. Does the school publish how a student can report a problem anonymously? Does the counseling office have enough staff to actually respond? Does the student government run anything of its own around school climate? Those are the things that outlast a one day event, and they are all questions a parent can ask at a back to school night without making it weird.

Ryan's Take

I sell houses for a living, and I can tell you that almost nobody buys a home in Clark County without pulling up the school zone first. But here is what I have learned from working with families for years. The number on a state report card tells you very little about what a kid's day actually feels like. Parents figure that out from other parents, from the booster club, from the group chat, and from how their kid acts when they get in the car at 2:30.

So when I see a program built around getting teenagers to hear the words "you matter" from somebody they think is cool, I do not roll my eyes at it. School culture is part of what makes a neighborhood a place people want to stay in, and staying put is what builds equity. Families who like their school do not move every three years. That stability is good for the kids and it is good for home values on the street. Those two things are more connected than people realize.

What You Can Do

If you want to know what is actually happening on your campus, start with the people who run it. Every CCSD high school has an administration team and a counseling office, and both will tell you what programs are in place if you ask directly. Ask what happens when a student reports being bullied, and ask who follows up. The answer tells you a lot.

Know the district's reporting tools too. Clark County School District maintains channels for students, parents, and staff to report bullying, harassment, and safety concerns, and every school has staff assigned to respond. Look up how your campus handles it now, while nothing is wrong, so you are not searching for it at ten o'clock on a school night. Write the phone number down. Tell your kid it exists.

Get involved with the school organizational team at your child's campus. Every CCSD school has one, and it includes parent seats. These teams weigh in on the school's plan and budget priorities. Showing up to those meetings is one of the few ways a parent gets a real vote on what a school spends its time and money on.

And if your teenager comes home talking about an assembly, take the opening. Ask what the riders said, not just what tricks they did. Those conversations are usually short and awkward, and they still matter. The whole point of a message like this is that a kid hears it more than once, from more than one adult who means it.

Volunteering is the other lever. Clark County high schools run on booster clubs, athletic volunteers, career day speakers, and parents who show up to chaperone. If you have a trade, a business, or an hour on a Thursday, a CTE campus like Veterans Tribute will find a use for you. Schools with a deep bench of involved adults tend to be the schools where students feel seen, and that is not a coincidence.

Finally, if you are house hunting and school culture is high on your list, go look. Drive to the campus at dismissal. Walk into the front office and ask for a tour. Go to a football game. You will learn more about a school in twenty minutes of watching how students treat each other than you will from an afternoon of reading rating websites. I tell every client with kids the same thing, and the ones who do it never regret it.
