Arbor View Automotive Teacher Surprised With Las Vegas Grand Prix Tickets

The Las Vegas Grand Prix surprised Brogen Ripley, the teacher who runs Arbor View High School's automotive and technology program, with two tickets to the 2026 race. It is a small gift with a big point behind it: the people teaching Clark County kids how to work on cars deserve a seat at the biggest car event this city has.

FOX5 Vegas reported the surprise on August 31, 2026. Ripley leads the automotive program at Arbor View, a comprehensive high school in northwest Las Vegas, where students learn engine work, diagnostics, and the fundamentals of a trade that pays real money in this valley.

Here is why a feel good story about a teacher and two race tickets is worth reading all the way through. Career and technical education is one of the clearest, fastest paths from a Clark County high school desk into a job that supports a mortgage. That path runs directly through teachers like Ripley, and there are not enough of them.

What Happened

The Las Vegas Grand Prix honored Brogen Ripley with two tickets to the 2026 running of the race. Ripley leads the automotive and technology program at Arbor View High School in the northwest valley. FOX5 Vegas covered the surprise on August 31, 2026, at the start of the new school year.

The recognition was aimed squarely at career and technical education. Arbor View's automotive program is one of the CTE offerings inside the Clark County School District where students spend real class time with tools in their hands. They learn brake and suspension work, engine diagnostics, electrical systems, and shop safety. That is not an elective in the traditional sense. It is job training with a high school transcript attached.

Ripley is the person who keeps that shop running. In a CTE program, one teacher usually handles the curriculum, the equipment, the safety compliance, the industry relationships, and the student competitions. It is closer to running a small business than teaching a single subject, and it is often done on the same salary as any other teacher in the building.

Arbor View High School sits in the Centennial Hills part of northwest Las Vegas and serves families from a wide swath of that side of the valley. It is a large comprehensive campus with athletics, arts, and a menu of CTE pathways alongside its traditional academics. The automotive program is a signature piece of that lineup.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Clark County's economy has a well known lopsidedness. Leisure and hospitality accounts for roughly a quarter of local employment, while manufacturing sits at a small fraction of that. For decades, the advice given to a Las Vegas kid who did not want a four year degree was to get into a casino job. That still works for a lot of people. But it is not the only path anymore, and the trades are the other big one.

Automotive technicians, diesel mechanics, HVAC techs, electricians, and plumbers are in steady demand across this valley, and the demand is not going anywhere. Every new subdivision in the northwest, every warehouse in North Las Vegas, and every apartment tower going up near the Strip needs skilled hands to build it and more skilled hands to keep it running afterward. Those jobs cannot be outsourced and they cannot be done remotely.

For a family, this is a housing question as much as a career question. A young adult who comes out of a high school CTE program with certifications and a first job in the trades is on a realistic timeline to buying a home in Las Vegas without a four year degree and without student loan debt. That is a different financial starting line than a graduate carrying tuition debt into an entry level office job.

It also matters for where families choose to live. Not every CCSD high school offers the same CTE pathways. A family whose kid is obsessed with cars will look hard at whether the zoned high school has an automotive shop, and northwest Las Vegas neighborhoods inside the Arbor View attendance zone have an answer that a lot of zones do not. Attendance zone shopping is a real thing in this market, and it is not only about test scores.

Then there is the Grand Prix itself. Formula One's arrival changed the November calendar in this town in a way that touches residents whether they buy a ticket or not. Road closures, hotel rates, short term rental demand, and construction schedules all bend around race weekend. A gesture that connects the race back to a high school shop class is a reminder that the event lives in a community, not just on a broadcast.

There is one more piece worth naming. Teacher retention in Clark County is a persistent challenge, and CTE teachers are especially hard to keep because the private sector can pay them more to do the same work with none of the paperwork. A shop teacher who can run a diagnostic bay can walk into a service department and earn a living tomorrow. Every year a program keeps a teacher like Ripley is a year the students get continuity instead of a long term substitute. Public recognition does not fix pay, but it is not nothing either.

Background and History

Career and technical education in Nevada has gone through a real revival. For a long stretch, shop classes were cut across American high schools in favor of college prep tracks. Budgets got tight, liability concerns grew, and equipment aged out. Districts quietly closed shops and never reopened them.

That trend reversed over the last decade and a half. Employers made a lot of noise about not being able to find skilled workers. States responded with funding and with an expanded definition of what counts as a successful high school outcome. Nevada now runs CTE programs across a long list of fields including automotive technology, construction, health science, culinary, information technology, and public safety.

Clark County has leaned into the model with dedicated career and technical academies alongside CTE pathways inside comprehensive high schools like Arbor View. The academy model gives students an entire school built around a career theme. The pathway model, which is what Arbor View runs, keeps the student at their neighborhood school while giving them a sequence of courses in a trade. Both work. The pathway model reaches more students because it does not require a family to commit to a long commute.

Arbor View has been in the local news for other reasons recently too. A volunteer crossing guard outside the campus told reporters that distracted drivers nearly hit her multiple times in a single day, which put a spotlight on school zone safety in the northwest valley. Schools this size sit at the center of their neighborhoods, for better and for worse.

The Las Vegas Grand Prix, for its part, has built a community outreach arm since the race arrived. Recognizing a public school automotive teacher fits that pattern. The race is an enormous machine built on automotive engineering, and the connection to a high school shop class is not a stretch. It is arguably the most natural link the event has to local education.

It helps to remember how quickly the northwest valley filled in. Much of the area around Arbor View was raw desert within living memory. Homes, schools, and retail arrived in waves through the 2000s and kept coming, and each wave brought families who needed a high school. Campuses out there absorbed enormous growth in a short window. When a school grows that fast, the specialty programs are usually the last thing to get funded and the first thing to get squeezed. That a full automotive shop survived and thrived through that growth says something about the people running it.

Nationally, the labor picture behind all of this is not subtle. The skilled trades workforce skews older than the workforce as a whole, and a large share of experienced technicians will retire over the next decade. Employers have been saying for years that they cannot replace them fast enough. High school programs are the front end of that pipeline. Every student who finishes an automotive sequence with a certification is a small answer to a very large problem.

What Happens Next

Ripley will be at the 2026 Las Vegas Grand Prix with a second ticket in hand. The larger question is what the recognition does for the program. Attention like this tends to bring two useful things to a school shop: equipment donations and industry partners willing to take student interns.

Families who want their student in an automotive pathway should be looking at course registration windows for the coming semesters. CTE sequences are built to be taken in order, so getting into the first course early matters. A student who starts the sequence as a freshman or sophomore has room to finish certifications before graduation. A student who starts as a senior usually does not.

Watch also for how the district handles CTE funding in upcoming budget cycles. Shop programs are expensive. Lifts, diagnostic equipment, and consumable supplies cost real money, and equipment has to keep up with vehicles that get more computerized every model year. A program teaching on twenty year old technology is not preparing anyone for a modern service bay. Public attention on a program is one of the more effective ways to protect its budget line.

On the race side, the Las Vegas Grand Prix will publish its full 2026 schedule, ticketing details, and road closure plans through official channels as the weekend approaches. Residents in the resort corridor and the neighborhoods that feed into it should plan around those announcements the same way they have in prior years.

Students in the program have their own next steps to think about. Industry certifications, dual credit agreements with the College of Southern Nevada, and apprenticeship openings with local dealerships and fleets all have their own timelines and application windows. A junior who maps that out now graduates with credentials. A senior who waits until spring graduates with a diploma and a hobby. The difference between those two outcomes is mostly planning, and it is the kind of planning a shop teacher spends a lot of unpaid hours helping students do.

Ryan's Take

I have sat at a lot of closing tables in this valley, and the buyers who surprise people are often the ones in the trades. A 26 year old auto technician with five years in the bay and a clean credit file is a strong buyer in Las Vegas. No tuition debt, a skill that travels, and income that does not evaporate when a convention gets canceled. I have watched that profile buy in Centennial Hills, in the northwest, and out toward Skye Canyon while friends with four year degrees were still renting.

That is why a story about a shop teacher getting two race tickets is not fluff to me. Programs like the one at Arbor View are quietly building the next round of Clark County homeowners. When I look at a neighborhood's long term health, I am looking at whether the people who live there can afford to stay, and whether their kids will be able to afford to stay. A high school that hands a student a trade is doing more for that than almost anything else on the campus. Good on the Grand Prix for noticing, and good on Ripley for showing up every day to run the shop.

What You Can Do

If you have a student who likes working with their hands, find out exactly which CTE pathways your zoned high school offers. Call the counseling office and ask for the CTE course sequence by name. Ask which industry certifications students can earn before graduation, and ask whether the program places students in internships or apprenticeships. Those three answers tell you whether a program is real or just a class.

If you own a shop, a dealership, a fleet, or a contracting business anywhere in the valley, contact a CTE program directly and offer something. Tools you no longer use, a shadow day for a few students, a guest talk about what you actually pay a first year tech. Programs like Arbor View's run on those relationships, and most business owners never realize how easy it is to help.

Parents can also push at the school organizational team level. Every CCSD campus has one, with parent seats, and it weighs in on how the school spends its discretionary money. If you want the shop to get new equipment, that is one of the rooms where you say so.

And if you are simply a fan of the race, remember that the event's community work is a real part of what it does here. Following the Las Vegas Grand Prix community announcements is how you find out about student programs, local hiring pushes, and neighborhood events tied to race weekend.
