Boulder City High Dedicates a Home Football Game to a 4-Year-Old

Boulder City High School's varsity football team dedicated a home game to 4-year-old Grayson Armstrong, who was there in the stands with his parents, Jessica and Rhett. The Eagles opened their season with a 21-0 win over Eldorado on August 28 under new head coach Chris Render.

The Boulder City Review told the story on September 3, 2026, under the headline "A game for Grayson." It is a small town doing what small towns still do well, which is turn out for one family on a Friday night.

Boulder City has around 15,000 residents and one public high school. When something happens to a family there, it does not stay in that family. It moves through the whole town, and the football team is one of the places where the town gathers.

What is clear is the shape of the gesture. A high school football team put a four year old at the center of one of the biggest nights on its calendar, and the community showed up for it. Dedications like this typically involve some combination of a jersey, a spot on the sideline, a pregame introduction, and a stadium full of people who came to make a kid feel like the most important person there. Boulder City did that on the last Friday in August.

Season openers carry their own weight at a school like this. The summer conditioning is over, the roster is finally set, and the whole town has been waiting since November for football to come back. Adding a dedication to that night made it something the players and the community will both remember, which is more than most openers manage.

A 21-0 result gave the night a clean ending. Nobody plans for that part, but a shutout win in a season opener under a brand new head coach is the kind of outcome that makes a dedicated game feel like it landed.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

If you live in Summerlin or Henderson or the northwest and you are wondering why a Boulder City football game is worth your attention, the answer is that this is what people mean when they say they want a small town feel. Everyone says they want it. Boulder City actually has it, and this is what it looks like in practice.

Boulder City is inside Clark County, about a half hour southeast of the Las Vegas Strip on the way to Hoover Dam and Lake Mead. It has its own city government, its own police department, and its own school system operating within the Clark County School District. It also has a size that lets a single high school serve the entire town. One high school means one football team, one graduating class, and one set of Friday nights that belong to everybody.

Compare that to a comprehensive high school in the Las Vegas Valley with a couple thousand students in a zone that keeps changing. Those schools do good work and many have wonderful communities inside them. But the odds that an entire city shows up for a four year old they have never met are simply higher in a town of 15,000 than in a metro of 2.3 million.

For homebuyers, this is a real and often overlooked category of value. People shop Boulder City specifically for this. They give up the convenience of a valley address, they accept a longer drive to work, and in exchange they get a town where the grocery store clerk knows their kids' names. That trade is not for everyone. For the people who want it, there is not really a substitute inside Clark County.

There is also a lesson here that travels. You do not need a small town to build this. Any high school in the valley can dedicate a game, name a night, or organize around a family. What Boulder City has is not magic. It is a habit. Communities that practice showing up get good at showing up, and any neighborhood can start practicing.

Families across Clark County also share the same practical worry underneath this story. Serious illness in a young child is expensive and exhausting, and it hits households that are otherwise doing everything right. Medical costs are one of the more common reasons a family's housing situation changes suddenly, and it is one of the least talked about. That is not the point of this particular story, but it is worth naming, because the neighbors who show up with meals and rides are doing something that has real financial value to a family in the middle of it.

And for anyone raising kids in this valley, there is a simple takeaway. The institutions that hold a community together are mostly the ones nobody thinks about until they are needed. A high school football program, a booster club, a church, a youth league. They look like extracurriculars right up until the week your family needs them. Then they look like infrastructure.

Background and History

Boulder City exists because of Hoover Dam. The federal government built it in the early 1930s to house the workers on the dam project, and it was a government town for decades before it incorporated as a city in 1960. That origin still shapes the place. The street grid, the historic district, and the civic architecture all trace back to that era.

Two local rules make Boulder City different from anywhere else in Clark County. First, gambling is not allowed within city limits, which is remarkable in this county and is a deliberate choice the community has defended for generations. Second, Boulder City has a growth control ordinance that limits how many new residential building permits can be issued. The city decided a long time ago that it did not want to become another valley suburb, and it wrote that decision into law.

The housing market consequences of that second rule are significant. Boulder City has very limited new construction compared to the rest of Clark County. Inventory is thin. Homes there tend to be older, on larger lots, with a character you cannot find in a master planned community built in 2005. When a good one lists, it does not usually sit for long.

The city also sits at the doorstep of Lake Mead National Recreation Area, and it is the last real town before the Arizona border on the way to Hoover Dam and the bridge. Tourism moves through, but the town itself never turned into a tourist product. That balance is a big part of the appeal.

Against that backdrop, Boulder City High School is more than a school. It is the town's shared institution. Football season, graduation, homecoming, and the spring sports calendar structure the local year. A new head coach is genuine local news. So is a season opener. So is a game dedicated to a four year old.

High school football in Nevada is organized by the Nevada Interscholastic Activities Association, which sorts schools into classifications by enrollment. That system is why Boulder City does not line up against the largest schools in the valley on a weekly basis. It also means the Eagles play a lot of games against schools their own size, where the crowds know each other and the rivalries have decades behind them. Eldorado, the opponent in the season opener, is a Clark County School District high school in the east valley.

Coaching changes matter more at a school this size, too. At a large program, a new head coach inherits a deep roster and a full staff. At a school like Boulder City, the head coach is often teaching in the building, knows the players' families, and shapes the culture of the program directly. Chris Render taking over and opening with a shutout is a strong first note, and the way he chose to open the season says something about what he wants the program to be.

For the Armstrong family, whatever they are walking through continues after the stadium lights go off. That is the honest part of stories like this one. A dedicated game is a beautiful night, and it does not solve anything. What it does is tell a family, in front of everyone, that they are not carrying it alone. In a town this size, that message tends to keep showing up afterward in the form of meals, rides, and neighbors who check in.

If the community organizes anything further to support the family, it will most likely be announced through the Boulder City Review and through local channels rather than through valley wide media. That is the normal pattern for this town. If you want to know what Boulder City is doing, you read Boulder City's paper.

The rest of the fall calendar in Boulder City fills in around the football schedule. Homecoming, senior night, and the postseason all sit ahead. Those nights are the town's regular gathering points, and after an opener like this one, the stands will likely be a little fuller than usual.

Ryan's Take

I get asked about Boulder City more than people would guess. Usually it is a buyer who has spent a few years in the valley and wants something quieter, or a family with young kids who is tired of feeling anonymous. My honest answer is that Boulder City is not a compromise version of Las Vegas. It is a different product. Limited inventory, older homes, bigger lots, a real downtown, and a community that operates at a human scale.

Stories like this one are the actual reason people pay for that. Nobody buys a house because of a football game. But people absolutely buy a house because of the kind of place where a whole town turns out for a four year old and his parents on a Friday night. That is not a feature you can put in a listing description, and it is the thing that keeps families in a place for thirty years. Good on Boulder City. Good on those kids and their new coach. And to the Armstrong family, from all of us across the county, we are pulling for you.

What You Can Do

The simplest thing is to go to a game. Boulder City High School home football games are open to the public and tickets are inexpensive. It is a thirty minute drive from most of Henderson and well under an hour from most of the valley. Bring your kids. Buy something from the booster stand. Small programs run on gate receipts and concession sales, and your twenty dollars goes further there than almost anywhere else you could spend it.

If you want to support a local family going through something hard, do it through legitimate, verified channels only. Community efforts around a specific child are frequent targets for scams, and the safest route is always the one the family or the school has publicly confirmed. Check the Boulder City Review and official school communications before you send money anywhere.

Boulder City also runs a full calendar of community events beyond football, from its historic downtown festivals to Lake Mead recreation. If you have never spent a weekend afternoon there, it is an easy day trip from anywhere in the valley and it will tell you more about the town than any listing photo will. Park downtown, walk the old streets, and get lunch. That is the town's actual pitch.

Closer to home, look at what your own school could do. Dedicating a night, naming a game, or organizing around a family in your attendance zone takes a coach, an athletic director, and a handful of parents who are willing to make calls. That is it. If Boulder City can do it with 15,000 people, a valley high school with 2,500 students can absolutely do it too.

And if you are curious about Boulder City as a place to live, understand the market before you fall in love with it. Growth controls keep supply tight, so timing and preparation matter more there than in a valley subdivision where three similar homes list every week. Know what you are looking for and be ready to move when it comes up.
