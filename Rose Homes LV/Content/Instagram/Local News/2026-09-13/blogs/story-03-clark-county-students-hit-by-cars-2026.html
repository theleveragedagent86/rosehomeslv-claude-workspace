45 Clark County Kids Have Been Hit by Cars in the First 22 School Days

Forty-five Clark County schoolchildren were struck by vehicles in the first 22 school days of the 2026-27 year. That is roughly two kids every single school day, and the largest group of them, 23 in all, were riding e-scooters when they were hit.

The Las Vegas Sun reported the count on September 10, 2026. The rest of the breakdown looks like this: 10 kids were on foot, seven were on bikes, three were on e-bikes, and two fell into an other category. Drivers were found at fault in about 60% of the incidents.

If you live near a school in Las Vegas, Henderson, or North Las Vegas, this is not an abstract statistic. It is the intersection you drive through at 7:15 in the morning. It is the sidewalk your kid takes home. And it is one of the quiet factors that shapes how families shop for neighborhoods in this valley.

What Happened

The number comes from tracking done through the 22nd school day of the 2026-27 school year. Clark County schools started back in August, so this window covers roughly the first five weeks of classes. In that stretch, 45 school-age children in Clark County were hit by a vehicle.

The mode of travel matters here, because it is the part of the story most people get wrong. E-scooters accounted for 23 of the 45, which is more than half. Ten students were simply walking. Seven were on traditional pedal bikes. Three were on e-bikes. Two were listed as other.

Put the electric categories together and you get 26 of 45 incidents involving an e-scooter or an e-bike. That is 58% of all the kids struck. Traditional walking and biking, the two ways most of us got to school growing up, made up 17 of the 45.

The fault data is just as important. Drivers were found at fault in about 60% of these incidents. That means in roughly six out of ten cases, the person behind the wheel made the mistake. It also means that in about four out of ten cases, they did not. Both of those numbers deserve attention, and neither one cancels the other out.

There is a reason the e-scooter number jumps off the page. These devices are fast, quiet, and easy to buy. Many of them move 15 to 20 miles per hour with no pedaling and no engine noise. A driver checking a crosswalk for a walking kid is scanning at walking speed. A scooter arriving at three or four times that speed enters the frame in a completely different way.

Think about the geometry for a second. A child walking covers about four feet per second. A child on an e-scooter at 18 miles per hour covers about 26 feet per second. A driver rolling up to a stop sign who glances left, glances right, and then starts moving has burned maybe two seconds. In that window the walker has moved eight feet. The scooter rider has moved more than 50. That gap is the entire story in one sentence.

The 22 school day marker also matters for anyone trying to put this in context. This is not a full-year total and it is not a multi-year study. It is a snapshot of the opening stretch of one school year, which is exactly why it is getting attention. Nobody expected the first five weeks to produce a number this high.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Clark County is built wide. Our arterial roads are six and seven lanes across, speed limits on them run 40 to 45 miles per hour, and block lengths between signals are long. That layout was designed to move cars efficiently across a fast-growing valley. It was not designed around a 13-year-old on an electric scooter.

So when you drop a modern e-scooter into a street grid like Charleston Boulevard, Eastern Avenue, Rainbow Boulevard, or Lake Mead Boulevard, you get a mismatch. The kid is moving at bike speed or faster, but the road treats them like a car, and the driver's eye treats them like a pedestrian. Nothing in that equation is set up to work.

For parents, the practical impact is immediate. If your child rides a scooter to a middle school or high school in Spring Valley, Enterprise, Green Valley, or Centennial Hills, the odds in this data set are not comforting. This is the single most common way Clark County kids got hurt in traffic over the first five weeks of school.

For drivers, the impact is a legal and financial one on top of the human one. Sixty percent driver fault means most of these incidents came with a citation, an insurance claim, and in some cases far worse. Your morning commute through a school zone is the highest-risk 90 seconds of your day, and most of us treat it like the lowest.

For homeowners and buyers, this lands on the neighborhood question. Walkability sells. Families ask about school routes, sidewalks, crossing guards, and whether a kid can get to campus without crossing a major arterial. Those questions used to be soft preferences. In a valley posting these numbers, they are becoming a real part of how buyers compare two otherwise similar homes.

There is a renter angle too. A large share of Clark County families with school-age kids rent, and renters generally have less control over where they live relative to a campus. If you are signing a lease in a complex that sits across a major road from the zoned elementary, you are signing up for that crossing every weekday for a year. That is worth checking before you sign, not after.

And there is a commuter angle that has nothing to do with kids at all. Clark County runs on shift work. Plenty of valley residents are heading to a Strip or resort corridor job right as bell times hit, and their route cuts through three or four school zones. Those drivers are not distracted parents on a short trip. They are people on a schedule moving through an environment that changes character twice a day.

Background and History

Nevada has been wrestling with pedestrian safety for a long time. The state has consistently ranked among the worse performers nationally for pedestrian deaths per capita, and Clark County carries most of that load because most of the state's population lives here. The valley's road design, its 24-hour economy, and its share of visitors all feed into that.

What changed in the last few years is the vehicle mix. E-scooters and e-bikes went from novelty to normal in a very short window. They got cheaper, batteries got better, and they became a common birthday and holiday gift for kids in the 11 to 16 range. A whole category of road user appeared on Clark County streets faster than the rules, the infrastructure, or driver habits could adjust.

Nevada law has tried to keep pace. The state has rules distinguishing electric bicycles by class and setting expectations for where different devices can operate, and local jurisdictions have layered their own ordinances on top. The practical problem is that most families buying a scooter at a big-box store never see any of that. There is no license, no test, and often no helmet included in the box.

Schools have pushed safety campaigns for years. Crossing guard programs, Safe Routes to School work, flashing school zone beacons, and speed enforcement details around campuses are all established tools in Clark County. They were built around walkers and bike riders. The 23 e-scooter incidents in this count suggest the tools have not fully caught up to who is actually on the road at 7 a.m.

It is also worth noting that CCSD is the fifth largest school district in the country, and it is handling this alongside a $51.6 million budget reduction this fall after enrollment fell by more than 10,400 students. Safety staffing, crossing guard coverage, and campus supervision all live inside budgets that are getting tighter, not looser.

Another piece of background that gets overlooked is bus eligibility. Districts across the country set walk-zone boundaries, and students inside those boundaries are expected to get themselves to campus. In a district the size of CCSD, that puts a very large number of kids on foot, on bikes, and now on scooters every morning by design. The scooter is not a toy for many of these families. It is transportation.

Layer in the valley's growth pattern and it gets clearer. Clark County added neighborhoods fast, and schools often opened after the rooftops did. That sequencing means plenty of campuses sit on or near arterials rather than tucked inside residential grids, because the arterial parcel was what was available. Those siting decisions from ten and twenty years ago are part of what today's crossing data reflects.

What Happens Next

The school year is only five weeks old. If the current pace holds across a roughly 180-day calendar, the annual total would land in a range nobody in this valley wants to read. That is the number local agencies will be watching, and it is the number that tends to trigger policy response.

Expect three things to move. First, enforcement. School zone speed details and crosswalk operations tend to ramp up when counts like this get public attention, and Las Vegas Metro, Henderson Police, and North Las Vegas Police all run them. Second, education. Look for more targeted messaging aimed specifically at e-scooter and e-bike riders rather than general pedestrian safety. Third, infrastructure. Beacons, high-visibility crosswalk striping, and signal timing changes at the worst intersections are the usual next step.

There is also a legislative angle worth watching. Nevada lawmakers have shown appetite for rules around electric devices, helmet requirements, and minimum ages, and a data set like this one is exactly the kind of thing that ends up in a bill draft. Nothing is filed on this specific point that I can point to today, so treat that as a reasonable expectation rather than a confirmed plan.

In the shorter term, the number to watch is whether the pace slows as the year settles in. Early-year crash counts often run hot because routines are new, routes are unfamiliar, and drivers have not readjusted to school traffic yet. Whether that holds true here will say a lot about how much of this is a scooter problem versus a start-of-year problem.

One more thing to watch is whether the reporting itself gets sharper. Right now we have counts by mode of travel and a fault percentage. What would actually help families is location data. Which corridors, which intersections, which schools. That level of detail is what turns a scary headline into a map a parent can use, and it is a fair thing for residents to ask for.

Ryan's Take

I have walked a lot of Clark County neighborhoods with buyers, and school routes come up constantly. A family will love a floor plan, love the price, and then drive the route to the middle school and go quiet. That drive tells them something the listing photos never will. A home three blocks from campus with an interior-street walk is a different product than a home three blocks from campus where the walk crosses a six-lane arterial.

Here is the part I think gets missed. This is not really a story about reckless kids or distracted drivers, though both exist. It is a story about a road network built for one set of assumptions meeting a new set of vehicles that do not fit those assumptions. Older parts of the valley with tighter grids and slower interior streets often handle this better than newer areas with big arterials and long blocks. That is worth knowing whether you are buying in an established Green Valley neighborhood or a newer community out in the southwest.

If you are house hunting with kids, drive the route at the actual time your kid would travel it. Not at 2 p.m. on a Saturday. At 7:10 on a Tuesday. You will learn more in ten minutes than in an hour of research, and it costs you nothing.

What You Can Do

Start with your own route. If your child rides an e-scooter or e-bike, ride or drive the full route with them once. Look for the specific spots where they cross a wide road, where a right-turn lane cuts across their path, and where parked cars or landscaping block a driver's view. Those three situations account for a huge share of close calls, and most of them can be engineered around by shifting the route one block.

Put a helmet on the kid. It is the cheapest risk reduction available and it is the one most commonly skipped on scooters, because a scooter does not feel like a bike. Add a front light and a rear light even for daytime riding. Visibility is the whole game when the driver's eye is scanning for pedestrians at walking speed.

Then talk to your school. CCSD campuses have principals, parent organizations, and school community partnership groups that can request crossing guard coverage, flag problem intersections, and push for signage. If a specific corner near your school scares you, say so in writing to the school and to your jurisdiction's traffic engineering division. Clark County, the City of Las Vegas, Henderson, and North Las Vegas all take resident reports on traffic safety, and documented complaints are how intersections get on the fix list.

If you drive, the ask is simple. Treat the 20 minutes on either side of bell times as a different kind of driving. Phone down, speed at or under the posted school zone limit, and a real look both directions before any right turn on red near a campus. Sixty percent driver fault is a number we can move.

Finally, share what you learn. If you find a crossing near your school that works well, or one that clearly does not, tell the parents around you. Neighborhood groups and school parent pages move this kind of information faster than any agency can. Five weeks into the year is early enough that a better route still changes the rest of the school year for a lot of kids.
