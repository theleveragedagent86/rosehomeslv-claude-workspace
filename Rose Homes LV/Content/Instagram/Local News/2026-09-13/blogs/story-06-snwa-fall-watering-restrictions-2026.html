Mandatory Fall Watering Rules Are On in Clark County and Sunday Watering Can Cost You

The Southern Nevada Water Authority's mandatory fall watering schedule started September 1 and runs through October 31. If you own a yard anywhere in Clark County, your sprinklers are now limited to three assigned days a week, and watering on Sunday is off the table entirely.

This is not a suggestion or a conservation campaign. It is a mandatory schedule, and running your system outside your assigned days can trigger water-waste fees on your bill. FOX5 Las Vegas reported the change on September 7, and a lot of valley homeowners still have their irrigation timers set to the summer pattern.

That is the whole problem in one sentence. Summer in Southern Nevada allows watering any day except Sunday. Fall does not. If you never touched your controller after Labor Day, there is a good chance your system is watering on days you are no longer allowed to water.

What Happened

The Southern Nevada Water Authority moved the region into its mandatory fall watering schedule on September 1, 2026. The schedule runs through October 31, 2026, and it applies across SNWA member areas, which covers Las Vegas, North Las Vegas, Henderson, Boulder City, and unincorporated Clark County served by the Las Vegas Valley Water District.

Under the fall rules, outdoor landscape irrigation is limited to three assigned days per week. Your assigned days are tied to your address, not to your preference. Two houses on the same street can have different groups, which is exactly why so many people guess wrong and assume their neighbor's schedule is theirs.

Watering on Sunday is prohibited. That part of the rule is the one most people know, because the no-Sunday restriction stays in place year round in Southern Nevada. What changes seasonally is how many other days you get. Summer is the most permissive window. Fall narrows it to three days. Winter narrows it further.

The enforcement piece is what makes this a story instead of a reminder. Watering outside your assigned days can trigger water-waste fees. Those fees show up on your water bill, and they are applied by the water purveyor that serves your address. FOX5 Las Vegas covered the start of the mandatory period on September 7, 2026, and framed it the same way: the rules are on now, and the fines are real.

It helps to understand what the assigned day groups actually are. Southern Nevada divides addresses into groups, and each group gets a set of days that rotate across the week so the whole valley is not irrigating at the same hour. During the three-day seasons, a group might land on Tuesday, Thursday, and Saturday, while the group next door gets Monday, Wednesday, and Friday. Nobody gets Sunday. The reason for the spread is basic system engineering. Pressure and treatment capacity are finite, and a valley where everyone waters on the same morning is a valley with a delivery problem.

Two practical notes that catch people. First, hand watering with a hose that has a shutoff nozzle is generally treated differently from automatic sprinkler irrigation, so a homeowner watering a single shrub by hand is not in the same category as a system dumping water across a lawn. Second, drip irrigation for trees and shrubs is generally treated differently from spray irrigation for turf. Before you rely on either of those, confirm the current specifics with your water provider, because the details are set locally and can change.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

If you own a home with any living landscape in Clark County, this rule is active right now and it has a price tag attached. That is a different thing from the water conservation messaging most of us have tuned out over the years. A fee on your bill is immediate and personal.

The biggest group at risk is homeowners who set their irrigation controller once and never touched it again. Smart controllers that pull the regional schedule automatically are fine. Older mechanical timers and basic digital boxes are not. If you moved into your home with an existing system and never opened the panel, you are in the group that needs to check this week, not next month.

Renters are affected too, but usually indirectly. In most Clark County rentals the tenant pays the water bill, which means a water-waste fee lands on the tenant even when the landlord installed and programmed the irrigation. If you rent a house with a yard, this is worth a conversation with your property manager about who is responsible for the controller settings.

Then there is the money side that nobody talks about until it shows up. Outdoor irrigation is the single largest use of water at most valley homes with turf. Cutting from a summer pattern to a three-day fall pattern usually lowers a water bill, not raises it, as long as you also shorten run times instead of just cutting days. Homeowners who drop to three days but leave each cycle running at the summer duration often see almost no savings and end up with the same soggy spots.

New buyers feel this differently than long-time owners. Somebody relocating to Summerlin, Mountains Edge, or Inspirada from a state with normal rainfall often arrives with no mental model for any of it. They see a green yard at the showing, they close, and then they get a letter about their watering days and a bill that is higher than they planned for. I would rather a buyer know all of that before they write the offer than after they get the keys.

There is also a neighborhood dimension. Homeowners associations across the valley set landscape standards, and some of those standards were written when water rules were looser. If your HOA expects green turf in a common area or on your front yard, and the watering schedule will not support it, that tension is real. Several valley associations have already worked through turf conversions on common areas. If yours has not, expect that conversation to come up.

Background and History

Southern Nevada has run a seasonal watering schedule for years, and the structure is the same every year even when the exact dates shift. The region uses four seasons for irrigation: winter, spring, summer, and fall. Summer is the widest window, winter is the narrowest, and spring and fall sit in the middle at three assigned days a week. The Sunday restriction stays in place across all of them.

The reason is the Colorado River. Southern Nevada draws the large majority of its water supply from Lake Mead, and Lake Mead's levels have been the defining water story of the American Southwest for two decades. Nevada has the smallest Colorado River allocation of the seven basin states, which is why Southern Nevada built its conservation program around outdoor use, where most residential water actually disappears into the air and the ground.

That focus on outdoor use is also why Nevada took a step no other state had taken. In 2021 the Nevada Legislature passed Assembly Bill 356, which requires the removal of nonfunctional turf in the Las Vegas Valley, meaning decorative grass in places like street medians, office park frontages, and entrances to housing developments. The law does not target residential backyards. It targets grass nobody walks on. The compliance deadline in that law is the end of 2026. Alongside the mandates, SNWA has run a turf removal rebate program for years, commonly known as Water Smart Landscapes. Homeowners and associations that replace grass with desert landscaping receive a rebate calculated per square foot of turf removed. The per-square-foot rate has been raised more than once over the life of the program. [NOT VERIFIED: the current per-square-foot rebate rate and any 2026 program caps.] The point of the program has stayed consistent, which is that permanently removing turf saves more water than any schedule ever will.

One more piece of history matters for understanding why the valley treats outdoor water this way. Southern Nevada recycles nearly all of the water that goes down an indoor drain. Water from sinks, showers, and toilets is treated and returned to Lake Mead, and the region earns return-flow credit for it. Water sprayed on a lawn is gone. It evaporates or soaks past the roots and it never comes back. That single distinction is why a long shower is a smaller regional problem here than an overwatered front yard, and why every conservation rule in the valley points outside the house.

The seasonal schedule is the near-term tool and the turf conversion is the long-term tool. The fall restrictions that started September 1 are the near-term tool doing its job during the shoulder season, when temperatures drop but a lot of irrigation controllers are still behaving like it is July.

What Happens Next

The fall schedule runs through October 31, 2026. After that, Southern Nevada shifts into the winter schedule, which is the most restrictive window of the year and typically drops residential irrigation to a single assigned day per week. If you adjust your controller once for fall and then forget, you will be out of compliance again on November 1.

That is the rhythm worth learning. Four schedule changes a year, and the two that catch people are the fall change and the winter change, because both arrive when the weather still feels warm enough to justify watering. The September 1 and November 1 dates are the two to put on a calendar.

Watch for enforcement activity through October. Water waste is typically identified through patrols and through resident reports, and the usual pattern is a warning on a first observation followed by escalating fees for repeat violations. If you get a notice, treat it as the free lesson it is and fix the controller the same day.

Fall is also the practical window for a turf conversion if you have been thinking about one. Planting and hardscape work go easier in October than in July, contractors are coming off the summer rush, and a yard converted this fall is established before next summer's heat. If you wait until spring you are competing with everyone else who waited until spring.

On the bigger picture, keep an eye on the nonfunctional turf removal deadline at the end of 2026 and on how Lake Mead's levels track through the winter. Those two threads drive whether the valley's watering rules loosen, hold, or tighten in 2027. Nothing about the current schedule suggests a loosening is coming.

Ryan's Take

I look at this as a homeowner cost issue more than a conservation issue, because that is how it actually shows up for my clients. A water-waste fee is annoying. Buying a home with a large grass yard and not understanding the ongoing water cost and the schedule you are locked into is a much bigger deal, and it is the version of this I see more often.

When I walk a listing with a buyer and the backyard is wall to wall turf, that is a line item, not a feature. It is a monthly water bill, a mowing cost, and a landscape that has to survive on three days a week right now and one day a week starting in November. Meanwhile a well done desert conversion in the same neighborhood costs almost nothing to maintain and looks sharp year round. Buyer preferences in this valley have moved toward the second one, and I do not think that reverses.

I will also push back gently on one thing I hear a lot, which is that desert landscaping means rock and nothing else. That was true of a lot of 1990s conversions and it is why some people hate the look. Good desert landscaping in 2026 is layered. It has shade trees, flowering shrubs, texture, and lighting, and it holds up better in August than turf does. If the only version you have seen is a gravel rectangle, go look at a few newer conversions before you decide.

For sellers, this cuts both ways. If you have already converted, say so in the listing and say what it saves. If you still have turf and it is struggling under the restrictions, get it healthy before photos or price it honestly, because a stressed yard in listing photos reads as deferred maintenance to a buyer even when the house is perfect inside.

What You Can Do

Start with your assigned days. Do not guess and do not copy your neighbor. Look up your address through your water provider, which for most of the valley is the Las Vegas Valley Water District, and for Henderson and North Las Vegas is the city utility that serves you. Your assigned group is tied to your address and it is the only version that matters if you get a notice.

Then open your irrigation controller and actually change it. Set it to your three assigned days, confirm Sunday is off, and shorten your run times for the season. A common approach for fall is to keep the same weekly total water but split it into shorter, more frequent cycles so the water soaks in rather than running off into the gutter. If your system has a rain sensor or a seasonal adjust feature, use it. If you have a smart controller that can pull the regional schedule, turn that on and you stop thinking about this forever.

If you are considering a turf conversion, look into the SNWA Water Smart Landscapes rebate before you hire anyone, because the rebate application typically has to be approved before the grass comes out. Removing the turf first can disqualify the job. Same advice for HOA properties: get the association's approval and the rebate pre-approval lined up together.

Finally, if you see broken sprinklers flooding a sidewalk in your neighborhood, that is worth reporting. Most water waste in the valley is not someone being defiant. It is a cracked head or a stuck valve that nobody has noticed yet, on a house where the owner would fix it in an afternoon if they knew.
