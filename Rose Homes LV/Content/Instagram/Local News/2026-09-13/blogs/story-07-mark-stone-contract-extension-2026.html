Mark Stone Signs a Two-Year Extension at $9 Million a Year

The Vegas Golden Knights are keeping their captain. On September 12, 2026, the team signed Mark Stone to a two-year contract extension that runs through the 2028-29 season at an average annual value of $9 million. General manager Kelly McCrimmon announced the deal.

That means the player who lifted the Stanley Cup for this city will be wearing the gold and black into his late thirties. For a franchise that has never had a losing season, keeping the face of the team is a big deal. For a city where the arena and the entertainment corridor drive a huge share of the jobs, it is bigger than hockey.

What Happened

Kelly McCrimmon, the general manager of the Vegas Golden Knights, announced on September 12, 2026 that the team had signed captain Mark Stone to a two-year contract extension. The deal carries an average annual value of $9 million and runs through the end of the 2028-29 season. Stone is 34 years old.

Stone arrived in Las Vegas in 2019. Since then he has become the most productive player in franchise history. He leads the Golden Knights in career points, career goals, and postseason scoring. No other player in the short history of this team has put up those numbers in a Vegas sweater.

The timing matters. The announcement landed right in the middle of the club's ramp-up to the 2026-27 season. Rookie camp was already underway, and the team had just signed forward Braeden Bowman to a six-year extension two days earlier on September 10. Two significant contract announcements in three days is a front office telling the fan base what the plan looks like.

A two-year term is worth noticing on its own. Stone will be 36 when the extension runs out. Short-term deals for players in their mid-thirties are a normal way for teams to manage salary cap risk. You get the production without committing to a long tail of years when the player may not be the same. At $9 million a year, Vegas is paying real money, but they are paying it for a defined window rather than a decade.

It is also worth saying plainly that the deal is an extension, not a new signing. Stone was already under contract. This adds years on the back end. So the captaincy, the locker room, and the top of the lineup stay exactly where they have been since he got here.

For fans who track these things, the numbers tell the story of why the team paid. Career points leader. Career goals leader. Postseason scoring leader. Those are three separate franchise records held by one player who is still on the roster. When a general manager decides whether to extend a veteran, that kind of production history is the strongest argument in the room, and it is the argument McCrimmon clearly accepted.

The deal also arrived without the drama that often surrounds captain contracts elsewhere in the league. There was no public standoff, no trade speculation that dragged on for months, and no reported request to move. Vegas announced it, the news went out, and the fan base spent the rest of the day arguing about the dollar figure rather than about whether the player wanted to stay. That is a quieter outcome than this organization usually produces.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Here is the honest version. A hockey contract does not change your mortgage payment. But it is one small piece of something that does matter to almost everyone who lives in Clark County, and that is the health of the visitor economy.

T-Mobile Arena sits right in the middle of the resort corridor. Forty-one regular season home games, plus preseason, plus whatever playoff runs the team puts together, fill that building. Every one of those nights means people in restaurants, people in rideshares, people in hotel rooms, and people working shifts. A lot of Las Vegas households are one or two steps removed from that activity. Bartenders, servers, valets, security, housekeeping, arena staff, and the businesses that supply them all feel it.

Winning teams sell more of those nights than losing teams do. The Golden Knights have never had a losing season and they won a Stanley Cup in 2023. That track record is why the building stays loud. Keeping the captain who leads the franchise in points is part of protecting that. It does not guarantee anything, but it is the opposite of a rebuild, and a rebuild is what empties an arena.

There is a second layer for homeowners and buyers. Employment stability is what holds up housing demand. Las Vegas learned the hard way in 2008 what happens when the visitor economy stalls. Anything that keeps the entertainment corridor busy and predictable is a small vote in favor of steady local jobs, and steady local jobs are what let people qualify for loans and make payments. That connection is indirect, but it is real.

If you live near the corridor, in the high rise condos along the Strip, in the neighborhoods just west of it, or anywhere the arena traffic flows, you already know the rhythm of a game night. A team locked in through 2029 means that rhythm is not going anywhere soon.

There is also a relocation angle that comes up in my business more than people expect. Buyers moving here from other states ask about what there is to do. A city with a Stanley Cup winning hockey team, an NFL team, a WNBA team, a new PWHL team, and a Formula 1 race every November answers that question differently than the Las Vegas of fifteen years ago. When a family is deciding between Henderson and a suburb in another state, the entertainment calendar is not the deciding factor, but it is on the list.

And for renters and landlords near the corridor, event nights are part of the math. Short-term rental demand, parking pressure, and traffic all spike around big arena dates. If you own a unit in one of the buildings near the Strip, the Golden Knights schedule is effectively part of your occupancy calendar from October through spring.

Background and History

The Golden Knights came into the league in 2017 and immediately did something no expansion team had done. They went to the Stanley Cup Final in their first season. That run happened weeks after the October 1 shooting, and the team became a place for the city to gather. That origin story is a big reason Vegas hockey has the emotional pull it has here.

Stone was not part of that first team. He came over in 2019 and was named the first captain in franchise history later that same year. He wore the C through the years that followed, including the 2023 season that ended with the Stanley Cup. He has been the constant while plenty of other names came and went.

The Golden Knights front office has built a reputation for being aggressive. They move on from players quickly, they take on big contracts, and they operate close to the cap ceiling. Fans have watched popular players get traded with little warning. That history is part of why a Stone extension is notable. This is the organization saying it wants continuity at the top of the roster instead of another shake-up.

The hockey footprint in Southern Nevada has grown alongside the NHL club. The Henderson Silver Knights play at Lifeguard Arena and the America First Center in Henderson. City National Arena in Summerlin is the practice facility and a busy community rink. Youth hockey programs that barely existed here a decade ago now have waiting lists. All of it traces back to the NHL team being good and being visible.

Stone's own history in Vegas is tied up in that growth. He arrived the season before the pandemic shortened everything, stayed through the bubble playoffs, and was on the ice when the franchise finally broke through in 2023. Fans who have been here since 2017 have watched him go from the new guy to the longest running symbol of the team. That is a short history by the standards of most NHL cities, and it is exactly why it feels significant here.

What Happens Next

The extension covers the 2027-28 and 2028-29 seasons, so it does not change anything about the roster this year. Stone was already under contract for the coming season. Training camp opens, the schedule starts, and the captain is in the same place he has been.

The real thing to watch is the salary cap. At $9 million a year, Stone represents a large share of what Vegas can spend. Combine that with the six-year, $3.4 million per year deal for Braeden Bowman announced September 10, and the front office is locking in a meaningful block of future cap space. Every dollar committed is a dollar not available for a trade deadline addition or a free agent. Fans who follow the cap closely will be doing that math for the next two offseasons.

The other thing to watch is simply health and production. Stone will be 35 in the first year of the extension and 36 in the second. Players at that age can still be excellent, and Stone has been the most productive player this franchise has had. Whether the deal looks like a bargain or a burden in 2029 comes down to how those two seasons go. Nobody knows that yet, and anyone telling you they do is guessing.

In the near term, the 2026-27 season starts with a roster that has its captain, its recent extensions, and a prospect group that got its first look at the Rookie Faceoff in September. The regular season home schedule at T-Mobile Arena is the next date that matters for most locals.

Watch for how the front office handles the rest of the roster from here. Vegas has a pattern of making one loud move per season, and a big extension usually sets the shape of what comes next rather than ending the conversation. If more money gets committed long term, the trade deadline options narrow. If the team stays flexible, they will be in the mix again in February. Either way, the Stone deal is now one of the fixed points the rest of the plan has to work around.

Ryan's Take

I look at this the way I look at any news that touches the Las Vegas economy. The question is always whether it makes the next five years here more predictable or less predictable. This one makes it a little more predictable, and predictable is good for real estate.

People who are not from here sometimes underestimate how much pro sports changed the identity of this valley. Before 2017 we had no major league team. Now we have the Golden Knights, the Raiders, the Aces, PWHL Las Vegas coming, and Formula 1 every November. That is not a small shift. It is part of why relocation buyers take Las Vegas seriously as a place to live rather than just a place to visit. When I show homes to people moving here from California or the Midwest, sports and entertainment come up constantly, usually right after schools and taxes.

Does a captain's extension move home prices? No. The median price in Southern Nevada is $475,000 right now and it moved down 1% year over year because of rates and inventory, not because of hockey. But the arena, the corridor, and the jobs attached to them are part of the floor under this market. Keeping the team good keeps that floor solid. I will take that.

The other thing I would say to sellers and buyers reading this is a reminder about scale. One contract, one development, one road project. None of them individually decide what your home is worth. What decides it is the sum of a hundred small things going the right direction over years. Pro sports arriving here was one of those things, and it has been going the right direction since 2017. Locking up the captain is a continuation of that, not a headline you should trade on.

What You Can Do

If you are a fan, the practical step is the schedule. T-Mobile Arena is the home building for Golden Knights games, and single game tickets and season packages go on sale through the team's official channels. Locals often find better value in weeknight games against non-rivalry opponents. If you have never been, the walk from the parking structures on game night is its own experience.

If you have kids who are getting into hockey, the valley has more options than most people realize. City National Arena in Summerlin runs youth programs and learn to skate sessions. Lifeguard Arena in Henderson is the Silver Knights practice home and hosts community skating. Both are worth a call if your child has been asking. The pipeline that produced Braeden Bowman started with rinks like these.

If you are house hunting and the entertainment corridor is part of your plan, be specific about what you want. Living close to T-Mobile Arena means high rise condos or the neighborhoods just off the Strip, and that comes with traffic, event nights, and short-term rental neighbors in some buildings. Living in Summerlin or Henderson means a drive to games but a very different day to day. Neither is wrong. They are just different trade-offs, and it helps to walk the area on an event night before you decide.

One more practical note for condo buyers near the Strip. Read the HOA documents carefully and ask specifically about short-term rental rules, guest parking, and how the building handles event night traffic. Two units in the same tower can feel completely different depending on which floor they are on and which direction they face. If the arena and the corridor are part of the appeal for you, make sure the building you pick actually delivers that instead of just being close to it.
