New Vegas Homes Closed $106,930 Above Resale While Builder Permits Fell 25%

A brand new single-family home in Southern Nevada closed at a median price of $581,930 in July 2026, while an existing single-family home sold for a median of $475,000 in August. That is a gap of $106,930 between new and used in the same valley, in the same season.

The July new-home figures come from Las Vegas-based Home Builders Research, reported by the Las Vegas Review-Journal. The August resale figure comes from Las Vegas REALTORS, also reported by the Review-Journal. Put side by side, they describe two markets moving in opposite directions. Resale prices slipped about 1% from a year ago. New home prices rose 2.1%.

And here is the part that most coverage skipped. Builders are not building their way out of that gap. Through the first seven months of 2026, Southern Nevada builders pulled 4,776 new-home permits. That is down 25% from the same stretch of 2025. A permit pulled today is a finished house many months from now, which means a 25% cut in 2026 becomes a supply problem in 2027.

What Happened

Home Builders Research released its July numbers in late August. Builders logged 735 net home sales in Southern Nevada in July. Net sales means newly signed contracts minus cancellations, so it is a real-time read on buyer demand. That total was up 28% from June, which sounds like a comeback, but it was still down 7% from July 2025.

Builders also pulled 620 new-home permits in July, up about 1.5% from June and down 23% from a year earlier. They closed 706 sales in the month, down 12% year over year. The month-to-month bounce was real. The year-over-year direction was not good.

The price numbers are where the story gets interesting. Buyers paid a median of $535,114 in July for all newly built homes in the valley, which includes condos and townhomes, and that was up 2.9% from a year earlier. For new single-family houses specifically, the median closing price was $581,930, up 2.1%.

Now flip to the resale side. Las Vegas REALTORS reported that the median price of an existing single-family home sold in Southern Nevada in August was $475,000. That was down 1% from August 2025 and $15,000 below the all-time high of $490,000 set in May and June of this year. The median price of a condo or townhouse sold in August was $299,900.

Total local sales fell 11.9% in August compared to July. Las Vegas REALTORS reported 2,252 existing homes, condos, and townhomes sold in the month. Single-family sales were down 1.7% from August 2025 and condo and townhome sales were down 7.4%.

Inventory is building on the resale side. There were roughly 7,590 single-family homes listed for sale without any offer at the end of August, up 5.3% from a year earlier. Another 2,714 condos and townhomes sat listed without an offer, up 6%. Las Vegas REALTORS put the sales pace at just over four and a half months of supply.

"Local home prices have been pretty stable this year, and really for the past two years or so," Las Vegas REALTORS President George Kypreos said in a news release. "It's not surprising to see sales slowing down a bit, especially considering how mortgage rates have been rising recently, and that can be a drag on the housing market."

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Start with the obvious one. If you are shopping right now, the choice between a new build and a resale house is not a small preference anymore. It is a six-figure decision. $106,930 is roughly the price of a Las Vegas condo. It is a down payment on a second property. At current rates it is hundreds of dollars a month on the payment for the life of the loan.

That premium buys real things. A new house comes with a warranty, current building codes, a fresh roof, a fresh HVAC system, and no deferred maintenance from the last owner. Nobody is arguing that a 2026 build and a 2004 build are the same product. The question is whether the gap between them is worth six figures to you personally, and for a lot of local buyers the honest answer is no.

Second, the permit slowdown matters to people who are not buying anything. If you already own a house in Henderson, North Las Vegas, or the northwest valley, new construction is your competition when you go to sell. Fewer permits in 2026 means fewer brand new homes competing with your listing in 2027. That is a quiet tailwind under resale values, and most homeowners have no idea it is happening.

Third, this is an affordability story before it is a market story. The valley added 620 permits in July while the resale median sat at $475,000 and the new-build median sat at $535,114. Neither of those numbers works for a household earning the local median wage without significant help. Supply is the lever that moves affordability over time, and the lever got pulled the wrong way this year.

Fourth, the leverage question. Right now a resale buyer in Clark County has more negotiating room than they have had in a while. Over 7,500 single-family homes are sitting without an offer. Supply is over four and a half months. Sellers who priced to the May peak are getting a cold reception. That is a genuinely good moment for a buyer with a stable job and a real down payment.

But leverage built on soft demand is not the same as leverage built on abundant supply. If builders keep pulling back and demand recovers even modestly when rates ease, that four and a half months of supply can tighten fast. Buyers who are waiting for a better market may be waiting through the better market.

There is also a renter angle here that rarely makes the headlines. Every house that does not get permitted this year is a household that stays in a rental another year, and that demand lands squarely on the valley's apartment and single-family rental stock. Clark County's rental market and its for-sale market are not separate systems. When one tightens, the other feels it within a couple of quarters.

And for anyone holding a home they bought in 2021 or 2022 at a low rate, the permit numbers are an argument for patience. The locked-in rate problem is real and it is not resolving quickly. But the supply side of the equation is shifting in your favor while you sit on that rate, which is a better position than most homeowners realize they are in.

Background and History

The 2026 pullback did not come out of nowhere. It follows a rough 2025. Southern Nevada builders pulled 9,734 permits last year, a 20% decline and the lowest annual total since 2016, according to Home Builders Research. They closed 9,990 home sales in 2025, also down 20% from the prior year.

"It is a positive sign moving into 2026 after the lowest annual total in a decade," Home Builders Research President Andrew Smith said of the market's footing heading into this year. Seven months in, the recovery has not arrived. Permits are down another 25% and closings are down another 20%.

Builders have responded to the affordability squeeze by changing the product rather than the price tag. VEGAS INC reported in May that Las Vegas-area builders are cutting square footage, paring down finishes, and leaning on financing incentives to reach buyers sidelined by mortgage rates above 6.5%. The trend is national. The National Association of Home Builders reported the median size of a new single-family home fell to 2,176 square feet in the third quarter of 2025, and new-home size has generally been shrinking since 2015 as affordability worsened.

NAHB also reported in January that 40% of builders cut prices and 65% used sales incentives. There is a reason builders reach for incentives before price cuts. A recorded sale at a lower price becomes a comparable sale that sits in the data for the rest of that community and drags down every future closing there. A rate buydown or a closing cost credit does not show up in the comps the same way. The buyer gets relief, the community keeps its price floor.

Nevada has tried to address supply directly. Gov. Joe Lombardo and the Legislature passed Assembly Bill 540, the Nevada Housing Access and Attainability Act, backed by a $133 million appropriation. It created the Nevada Attainable Housing Account, aimed at spurring construction and expanding homeownership access for households earning up to 150% of the area median income. Tina Frias, CEO of the Southern Nevada Home Builders Association, called it "a step in the right direction, but it's a piece of a much larger supply challenge."

What Happens Next

Watch the monthly permit count first. Home Builders Research reports it every month and the Review-Journal covers it. Permits are the leading indicator for everything else in this story. If monthly permits stabilize in the 600 to 700 range through the fall, the 2027 supply picture stays thin but manageable. If they slide back under 600, the new-home shortage next year gets sharper.

Watch the resale inventory number too. About 7,590 single-family homes were listed without an offer at the end of August, up 5.3% year over year. That number normally softens heading into the holidays as sellers pull listings. If it keeps climbing through the fall anyway, sellers are going to have to get serious about price, and the gap between new and resale widens further.

Watch mortgage rates, because they move both sides of this at once. Las Vegas REALTORS specifically flagged rising rates as a drag on August sales. Lower rates pull buyers off the sidelines and take pressure off builders to discount. Higher rates do the opposite and push builders deeper into buydowns and credits to keep traffic moving through the models.

And watch what the big builders open next. New community openings tell you where the permit money is actually going, and in this valley that has been Henderson, North Las Vegas, and the upper northwest. Those openings, and the starting prices attached to them, are the clearest public signal of where builders think the affordable price point is.

Finally, watch what happens with the state money. The Nevada Attainable Housing Account is funded and the program is new, so the first round of projects it touches will tell us whether $133 million actually moves the needle on units built or just moves the conversation. In a valley pulling roughly 600 permits a month, it does not take many funded projects to show up in the data, but it does take projects that actually break ground.

The one thing I would not do is wait for a single dramatic headline to tell you the market turned. This shift is happening in monthly permit counts and inventory reports, not in a crash or a boom. By the time it is obvious, the pricing will already reflect it.

Ryan's Take

I do not think most buyers understand how much of the new-construction premium they are paying for things they will never notice. Some of that $106,930 is a genuinely better house. Some of it is a builder protecting a price floor inside a community so the last twenty closings do not wreck the first hundred. Those are different things and they are bundled into one number on a contract.

What I keep telling clients this fall is that the resale side is where the negotiation is right now. Over 7,500 single-family homes are sitting without an offer. A seller who has been on the market since July is a different negotiating partner than a builder's sales office with a corporate pricing floor and a quarterly target. If the house does not have to be brand new, the resale market is handing buyers real concessions today.

I also want to be fair to the builders here, because the pullback is rational. Construction costs are up, financing is expensive, and nobody wants to be sitting on finished spec inventory in a market where closings are down 20% year over year. Pulling fewer permits is what a disciplined company does when demand is soft. It just happens to be the exact opposite of what a valley with an affordability problem needs.

The flip side, and I say this to sellers constantly, is that the permit pullback is genuinely good news for people who already own here. Your competition in 2027 is being decided right now by builders who are choosing not to pull permits. That is not a guess, it is 4,776 permits through July against a 25% drop from the same stretch last year. Fewer new rooftops next year is a floor under your equity.

What You Can Do

If you are comparing new construction to resale, do the math on the same floor plan size, not the same monthly payment. A builder's buydown can make a $581,930 house feel like a $475,000 house for the first few years. It does not change what you owe, what you insure, or what you pay in property taxes. Ask what the payment looks like in year four when the buydown is gone.

If you are buying new, bring your own representation to the first visit, before you sign a registration card at the model. The sales agent in that office works for the builder, which is their job and not a criticism. Having someone on your side of the table costs you nothing at closing and changes how the contract, the walkthrough, and the warranty period get handled.

If you are selling a resale home this fall, price to August data, not to the May peak. The median went from $490,000 in May and June to $475,000 in August. Sales fell 11.9% in a single month. A listing priced to the spring is going to sit next to 7,590 other homes that are also sitting, and the first thirty days on market are the only thirty days that carry real urgency with buyers.

If you are shopping new construction, also compare square footage against what the same builder was selling two or three years ago in the same community. New homes are getting smaller nationally, down to a median of 2,176 square feet, and that trend is showing up in Las Vegas floor plans too. A lower starting price on a smaller plan is not the same thing as a price cut, and it is worth knowing which one you are actually being offered.

And if you are just watching from the sidelines, follow the permit number each month. It is the single most useful housing statistic in Clark County right now, and almost nobody outside the industry tracks it. Home Builders Research publishes it monthly and the Review-Journal covers the release, so it costs you nothing but a few minutes to stay ahead of the people who only read the median price headline.
