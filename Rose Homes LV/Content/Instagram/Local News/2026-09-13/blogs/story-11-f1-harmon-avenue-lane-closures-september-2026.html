F1 Track Work Is Closing Harmon Avenue Lanes Overnight Through September 18

Two lanes of Harmon Avenue are closed overnight from 9 p.m. to 6 a.m., Sunday through Friday, through September 18, 2026. The closures are the first visible step of track preparation for the Formula 1 Heineken Las Vegas Grand Prix, which runs November 19 through 21.

Crews started the week of September 7 with track lighting installation and bridge work. If you live, work, or drive near the resort corridor, this is your heads up. The overnight work is the beginning of a build that stretches across the next two months, not a one week inconvenience.

What Happened

FOX5 Las Vegas reported on September 7, 2026 that road prep for the 2026 Las Vegas Grand Prix was officially underway. The first phase covers track lighting installation and bridge work along the circuit route. That work is happening on Harmon Avenue, one of the streets that makes up the race course through the resort corridor.

The schedule is specific. Two lanes of Harmon Avenue close at 9 p.m. and reopen at 6 a.m. The closures run Sunday night through Friday morning. Saturday nights are clear. The current window ends September 18, 2026.

Race organizers did something useful this year. They launched an interactive tracking map so residents and businesses can see exactly which segments are closed and when. They also set up a text alert system, so you can get a message on your phone instead of finding out about a closure when you are already sitting in it.

The race itself is scheduled for November 19 through 21, 2026. That means the gap between the first lane closure and the checkered flag is a little over two months. Everything between now and then is build out: lighting, barriers, grandstands, pedestrian bridges, paving work, and the temporary structures that turn public streets into a race circuit.

It helps to understand what overnight lane closures actually mean on a street like Harmon. This is not a residential side street. Harmon Avenue is a working east and west connector in the middle of one of the busiest tourist zones in the country. Even at 11 p.m. on a Tuesday, it carries rideshare drivers, hotel shuttles, delivery trucks, casino employees heading to and from shift changes, and residents who live in the condo towers and apartment communities just off the Strip.

Taking two lanes out of that mix for nine hours a night does not shut the road down. It squeezes it. Traffic still moves, but it moves slower, and the slowdown ripples out to the cross streets that feed into Harmon.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

The people most affected are not tourists. They are the roughly tens of thousands of Clark County residents who work the resort corridor on swing and graveyard shifts. If your shift ends at midnight and you head home through Harmon, your drive just got longer. Add ten or fifteen minutes to your commute and plan for it, because the alternative is being late and frustrated.

Second group: anyone who lives in the high rise and mid rise condo buildings near the track route. Buildings along and near Harmon, Koval Lane, and the Strip sit inside the construction footprint. Residents there deal with light spill from work crews, equipment noise, and access changes at their own garage entrances. Some buildings handle this well with advance notices to owners. Others do not, and residents find out the hard way.

Third group: small businesses in the corridor. Restaurants, bars, tattoo shops, convenience stores, and service businesses off the Strip depend on late night traffic. When two lanes disappear and drivers reroute, the businesses on the closed stretch lose walk in and drive by customers. This is a real and repeating cost for operators in that zone, and it is one of the reasons F1 traffic is such a sore subject with locals.

Fourth group: emergency response and delivery logistics. Fire, ambulance, and police routes through the corridor have to adjust. So do freight deliveries to hotels, which often happen overnight precisely because daytime traffic is worse. Every delivery window that gets pushed adds cost somewhere, and that cost eventually shows up in prices.

Renters in the corridor deserve a mention too. A lot of the apartment inventory just east of the Strip houses hospitality workers who chose the location specifically for a short commute. For them, the trade off they made is being tested. A ten minute drive that stretches to twenty five for two months is the kind of thing that pushes people to look at Henderson or the southwest valley at lease renewal time, and I do see that pattern in the fall.

Here is the part that matters most for the next two months. September is the light phase. The closures get more involved as the race gets closer, because the amount of physical infrastructure that has to be installed grows. Anyone planning a move, a home sale showing schedule, or regular travel through the corridor between now and late November should assume the disruption increases, not decreases.

There is also a practical point about home sales in the corridor. If you are listing a condo near the route this fall, showing times matter more than usual. An evening showing that runs into a 9 p.m. lane closure gives a buyer a bad last impression of the drive home. Morning and early afternoon showings are the smarter play right now, and any listing agent working that submarket should be building the closure schedule into the showing calendar rather than finding out about it from an annoyed buyer.

Background and History

Formula 1 returned to Las Vegas in November 2023 after a long absence, and the event has run annually since. The circuit is a street course, which means it uses existing public roads rather than a purpose built track. That is the entire reason these closures exist. A permanent facility would not require shutting down city streets. A street circuit does, every single year.

The first year drew heavy local criticism. Construction stretched for months, businesses along the route reported lost revenue, and residents dealt with barricades, detours, and noise. The event delivered real economic activity for the region, but the way the build out was handled became a lasting sore spot for people who live here year round rather than visit for a weekend.

Since then, organizers have adjusted. Build out timelines have tightened compared to that first year, more work has moved to overnight windows, and communication tools have improved. The interactive tracking map and text alert system launched with this phase are a direct response to the complaint that residents never knew what was closed until they hit the cones.

That is genuine progress. It does not eliminate the disruption, but information changes how disruption feels. Knowing on Sunday that Harmon loses two lanes Monday through Friday nights lets you plan. Finding out at 11:40 p.m. while you are already late does not.

It is worth remembering why the race is here at all. Las Vegas competed hard for the event because a global motorsports weekend fills hotel rooms in what used to be a softer part of the November calendar. The economic case has always rested on visitor spending, room rates, and international television exposure for the destination. None of that benefit lands evenly, though. The hotels and the operators on the route see the upside. The resident who commutes across Harmon at midnight in October sees the cost and none of the revenue.

That imbalance is the honest core of the local debate, and it is why the same argument resurfaces every September. Locals are not against the race. Most of the people I talk to think it is good for the city overall. What they object to is absorbing months of construction for a three day event they may never attend. Better communication tools help, because they reduce the feeling that the corridor is being run for visitors while residents figure it out on their own.

The bigger context is that Clark County is in a heavy infrastructure cycle right now. HOV lane work continues on I-11 and U.S. 95 in the northwest valley. A $37.7 million rebuild of Lake Mead Boulevard is running through the rest of 2026 in North Las Vegas. Add F1 track prep in the resort corridor and you have three major traffic pressure points in one valley at the same time. If your commute touches any of them, this fall is going to test your patience.

What Happens Next

The current Harmon Avenue closure window runs through September 18, 2026. After that, expect the work to shift to other segments of the route rather than stop. Track lighting and bridge work are early stage items. Barrier installation, grandstand construction, pedestrian bridge work, and paving typically follow as the race date gets closer.

Race weekend itself is November 19 through 21. The days immediately before and after are the heaviest closure period of the entire cycle, because the circuit has to be fully sealed off for the event and then dismantled afterward. If you have flexibility in your schedule, that is the window to avoid the corridor entirely.

Between now and then, the single most useful thing to watch is the interactive tracking map. Closure segments change week to week. A map that is accurate today may not describe next month's conditions. Check it before you plan a route through the corridor, especially for anything time sensitive like an airport run, a medical appointment, or a home showing.

There is a teardown phase too, and people forget about it. Once the cars leave, the barriers, grandstands, lighting rigs, and temporary structures all have to come down. That work produces its own round of closures in the weeks after the race, right in the middle of the holiday season. If your December plans involve the resort corridor, do not assume the road is back to normal on November 22.

Also watch for announcements about daytime work. Most of this phase is overnight, which is the least disruptive option. If any segment moves to daytime hours, that changes the calculation significantly for commuters and businesses, and it would be worth knowing in advance.

Ryan's Take

I get asked about this every single year by buyers looking at condos near the Strip, and my answer has not changed. F1 is a real factor in that submarket, but it is a seasonal one, and it cuts both ways. For roughly two and a half months a year, you deal with construction, closures, and noise. For race weekend, you may be sitting on a short term rental opportunity that produces more income in three days than a normal month.

What I tell clients is to be honest about which kind of owner they are. If you are buying a Strip area condo as a primary residence and you work a normal schedule, drive the route in September and again in November before you commit. Visit at 10 p.m., not at noon on a Sunday. If you are buying as an investor and the short term rental math works in your building's rules, the same disruption that annoys residents is part of why the rent spikes.

For the rest of the valley, I want to be clear that this story does not touch your home value. Summerlin, Henderson, Centennial Hills, Mountains Edge, and North Las Vegas are not affected by Harmon Avenue lane closures in any meaningful way. Resort corridor construction is a hyperlocal condition, not a valley wide one, and I would not let a headline about F1 traffic shape a decision about a house ten miles away.

The mistake I see is buyers who only tour during quiet months and get blindsided in the fall. The corridor is a different place in October than it is in May. That is not a reason to avoid it. It is a reason to go in with your eyes open, and to price the inconvenience into what you are willing to pay.

What You Can Do

Start with the two tools organizers built for exactly this situation. Sign up for the text alert system so closure notices come to your phone, and bookmark the interactive tracking map so you can check segments before you drive. Both are free, and both remove the main complaint locals have had about F1 traffic, which is not knowing.

If you commute through the corridor on a swing or graveyard shift, map an alternate now rather than improvising at midnight. Tropicana Avenue, Flamingo Road, and Sands Avenue are the main east and west alternatives depending on where you are coming from. Pick one, drive it once during your actual commute hour so you know how it really flows, and keep it in your back pocket for the rest of the fall.

If you own or rent in a building near the route, ask your HOA or property manager directly what the construction schedule means for your garage access and your building entrances. Do it now, not in November. Management companies that get asked early tend to communicate better, and you will have a much easier time planning deliveries, moves, and guest parking with that information in hand.

Rideshare and delivery drivers can turn this into an advantage. The drivers who learn the closure pattern first are the ones who keep their trip times down while everyone else guesses. Nine hours a night of altered traffic across two months is a lot of learning opportunity, and the map makes it easy.

Business owners in the corridor should document the closure dates that affect their block. If you need to adjust hours, staffing, or delivery windows, having the schedule written down makes those decisions cleaner. It also gives you something concrete to point to if you want to raise concerns with county officials or race organizers.
