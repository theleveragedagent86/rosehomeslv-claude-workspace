KB Home Opens a 1,500-Home North Las Vegas Community Starting in the $300,000s

KB Home opened the first phase of Sandstone in North Las Vegas on September 4, 2026, a community planned for more than 1,500 houses on nearly 300 acres, with prices starting in the $300,000s. That starting price sits well below the $475,000 median for an existing single-family home in Southern Nevada, which makes this one of the largest chunks of entry-level supply the valley has seen in years.

The timing is what makes it interesting. Sandstone is landing right as the local market cools off. Prices slipped, sales slowed, and buyers finally have some breathing room. A builder dropping 1,500 planned homes into that moment changes the math for a lot of people who thought they were priced out of Clark County for good.

What Happened

KB Home opened Sandstone to the public on September 4, 2026. The builder describes it as its largest new Southern Nevada community in a decade. The site covers nearly 300 acres north of the 215 Beltway and the North Fifth Street interchange in North Las Vegas, a part of the valley that has absorbed a lot of the region's growth over the past several years.

The full plan calls for more than 1,500 homes. That is not a single release. A project that size gets built out in phases over years, with each phase priced to the market conditions of its moment. The first two neighborhoods are open now and they are called The Landings and The Reserves. Prices in this opening phase start in the $300,000s.

For context on how unusual that price point is right now, the median price of an existing single-family home sold in Southern Nevada in August 2026 was $475,000. That figure comes from Las Vegas REALTORS and it was down 1.0% from August 2025, sitting $15,000 below the all-time high of $490,000 set in May and June of this year. So a new-build community opening with a three in front of the starting price is roughly $150,000 under the typical resale house in the valley.

The location matters as much as the price. North Las Vegas has been one of the busiest building corridors in Clark County. In the first quarter of 2026, six submarkets including Summerlin West, Lake Las Vegas, Cadence in Henderson, Skye Canyon, and North Las Vegas took 74% of all new single-family home permits issued in Clark County. Builders go where the land is, and North Las Vegas still has land.

One thing worth being clear about: a starting price is a starting price. In new construction, the advertised number usually reflects the smallest floor plan, on a standard lot, with a base level of finishes. Lot premiums, upgrades, and elevation choices move that number. Anyone reading "$300,000s" should treat it as the floor of the range, not the average price a buyer will actually sign for.

It also helps to understand what "first phase" means in a project like this. The land is entitled and graded for the full 1,500-plus homes, but the builder releases lots in small batches, sometimes only a handful at a time. That is how national builders manage risk. They can slow releases if demand softens and speed them up if buyers line up. So the pace of the buildout at Sandstone will tell you as much about the health of the North Las Vegas market over the next few years as any single monthly price report will.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

The biggest reason this matters is supply at the bottom of the market. For the last several years the hardest thing to find in Clark County has been a decent house that a first-time buyer or a working family could actually afford. Inventory under $400,000 has been thin, and what did come up often needed work or sat in an area buyers did not want. Sandstone puts new product directly into that gap.

Second, it matters for anyone who already owns a home in North Las Vegas. When a builder opens 1,500 planned homes a few minutes from your street, you are now competing with brand new inventory whenever you go to sell. Builders can offer things a private seller cannot, like rate buydowns, closing cost help, and a warranty on everything in the house. If you are planning to list a resale home in that part of the valley in the next couple of years, that competition is real and you should price accordingly.

Third, it matters for renters. Clark County's rental market has stayed tight, and a big share of valley households are renting because buying felt out of reach. A community opening in the $300,000s does not fix affordability by itself, but it gives renters a target that is at least reachable. With a starting price in that range, the monthly payment conversation looks different than it does at $475,000, especially once you factor in what builders are willing to do on financing.

Fourth, growth brings traffic, schools, and services with it. Fifteen hundred homes is a lot of new households. That means more cars on North Fifth Street and the 215 Beltway, more kids in nearby Clark County School District schools, and eventually more demand for the grocery stores, gas stations, and medical offices that follow rooftops. Some of that is good news for existing residents and some of it is a real adjustment. Both things are true at the same time.

There is one more angle that does not get talked about enough. New construction changes what a buyer's monthly cost actually looks like, and not always in the direction people assume. A new house usually comes with lower maintenance costs and better energy efficiency than a 1990s resale, which helps. But it often comes with a higher HOA, and in some parts of Clark County it comes with a Special Improvement District assessment attached to the parcel that funded the streets and utilities. Those line items are real money every month, and they belong in the comparison before anyone decides a new build is cheaper than a resale at a similar price.

Background and History

North Las Vegas has been through a full cycle in the last two decades. It was one of the fastest-growing cities in the country before the 2008 crash, then it was one of the hardest hit places in the entire nation when the market turned. Whole subdivisions sat unfinished. The city itself came close to serious financial trouble. Anyone who lived here through that remembers it.

What brought the city back was a combination of cheap land, industrial growth along the northern edge of the valley, and the buildout of the 215 Beltway, which made the northern submarkets commutable to jobs across the valley. Over the last several years North Las Vegas has become one of the main places builders go when they need to deliver a home at a price a normal buyer can pay.

That is the backdrop for Sandstone. KB Home calling this its largest Southern Nevada community in a decade tells you something about where the builder sees demand. Big builders do not commit to 300 acres and 1,500 planned homes on a hunch. They commit when they believe there is sustained demand at a specific price point over a multi-year horizon, and the price point they are aiming at here is clearly the entry level.

The other piece of background is what has been happening to prices. Southern Nevada's median existing single-family price ran up to an all-time high of $490,000 in May and June 2026, then slipped to $475,000 in August. Total sales of existing homes, condos, and townhomes in August came in at 2,252, down 11.9% from July. Single-family sales were down 1.7% year over year and condo and townhome sales were down 7.4%. That is a market taking a breath, not a market falling apart, but it is a real shift from the run-up.

It is also worth remembering how much of the valley's housing story has been written by builders rather than by resale owners. Las Vegas has always been a new construction town. When resale inventory tightens up, the builders are the release valve, and when the builders pull back, the whole market feels it. A project the size of Sandstone is the builders leaning in, not pulling back, and that is a reasonably confident signal about where this part of North Las Vegas is headed over the next five to seven years.

The affordability picture deserves one more note. Buyers priced out of the $475,000 single-family median have had two realistic lanes, and both of them have been getting a little easier. One is the condo and townhome market, where the Southern Nevada median in August 2026 was $299,900. The other is entry-level new construction in the outer submarkets, which is exactly what Sandstone is. Neither lane is perfect, but having two of them open at once is better than the single-lane market buyers dealt with during the run-up.

What Happens Next

The Landings and The Reserves are open now, so the near-term question is how fast that first phase sells and what KB Home learns from it. Builders watch traffic through their model homes week by week. If the $300,000s price point pulls strong interest, expect later phases to come out priced a little higher. If traffic is soft, expect incentives to get more generous before the base price moves.

Watch the end of each sales quarter. Buyer agents in the valley report builders negotiating an extra $8,000 to $22,000 in incentives beyond the published package, and that flexibility shows up most in the final 30 days of a quarter when a division is chasing a closing target. That pattern is not specific to Sandstone, but it applies to almost every new community in Clark County right now, and it is worth timing around.

Also watch the infrastructure around the site. North Las Vegas has significant road work underway, including a $37.7 million roadway, utility, and traffic system rebuild along Lake Mead Boulevard between Simmons Street and Losee Road that is scheduled to continue through the rest of 2026. Corridor work like that is disruptive while it is happening and usually a long-term positive for the surrounding blocks once it is finished.

The last thing to watch is financing. Freddie Mac's national average 30-year fixed rate was 6.76% as of September 10, 2026, while Nevada quotes the same week ran closer to 7.125% in the Monitor Bank Rates Las Vegas survey. Those two numbers are measured differently, a national survey average versus an advertised local snapshot, so they are not a perfect comparison. But the gap is a good reminder that a local quote is the only number that matters for your budget.

Ryan's Take

I have been telling buyers for two years that the affordability problem in this valley is a supply problem at the bottom. Sandstone is a direct answer to that. Fifteen hundred planned homes starting in the $300,000s in a valley with a $475,000 median is meaningful new inventory for exactly the buyer who has been shut out. I would rather see it built than not built.

My honest caution is about how people shop new construction. The single most expensive mistake I see is a buyer walking into a model home alone, signing with the on-site agent, and assuming the advertised incentive package is the whole offer. It usually is not. That on-site agent works for the builder, not for you, and bringing your own representation costs you nothing out of pocket. In a cooling market with builders competing for a smaller pool of buyers, the negotiating room is wider than most people think.

The other thing I would tell a North Las Vegas homeowner: pay attention. If you are thinking about selling in the next two years, you are going to be selling into a market where a national builder a few minutes away can offer a brand new house with a rate buydown. That does not mean you cannot sell. It means you need to be sharp on price and honest about condition from day one.

What You Can Do

If you are a buyer, go see it, but go prepared. Register with your own agent before your first visit to the sales office. Most builders in Clark County, including the national ones, require your agent to accompany you on that first registration for representation to apply. If you tour alone first and come back later with an agent, you may have given up your right to representation on that purchase. This is the single easiest mistake to avoid and it costs you nothing to get right.

Ask specific questions when you are there. What is the base price versus the price of the model you just walked through? What are the lot premiums? What is the HOA, and is there a Special Improvement District or LID assessment on the parcel? What is the incentive package, and does it require using the builder's preferred lender? Get the estimated closing timeline in writing, and get an independent inspection even on a brand new house.

Get a local preapproval before you shop, not after you fall in love with a floor plan. Ask a Nevada lender for an actual quote on your credit profile and your down payment, and compare it against whatever the builder's preferred lender offers with the incentive applied. Sometimes the builder's package genuinely wins because the buydown is worth more than a slightly better base rate. Sometimes it does not. You cannot know which without both numbers side by side.

If you already own in North Las Vegas, keep an eye on what Sandstone actually closes at, not what it lists at. Closed prices in a new community set the comps that appraisers use in the surrounding area. And if you drive North Fifth Street, the 215 Beltway, or Lake Mead Boulevard, plan for construction traffic to be part of your commute for a while.
