Vegas Condos Went Up While Houses Went Down

Condo and townhome prices in Southern Nevada went up in August 2026 while single-family house prices went down. The median price of a condo or townhome sold in the valley was $299,900, up 0.6% from August 2025, according to Las Vegas REALTORS data reported by KSNV News 3. In the same month, the median price of an existing single-family home fell 1.0% to $475,000.

That is a split in the same report, in the same month, in the same valley. Two lanes of the Las Vegas housing market moved in opposite directions. For a buyer who has been priced out at $475,000, that $299,900 number is the most useful figure in the whole August report. It is also the number almost nobody is talking about, because the headlines all went to the single-family side.

What Happened in the August 2026 Numbers

Las Vegas REALTORS released its August 2026 data on September 9, 2026. The report covers existing homes, condos, and townhomes sold through the Multiple Listing Service in Southern Nevada. It is the same monthly report that local news outlets use for their housing headlines, and it is the closest thing the valley has to an official scoreboard.

The single-family number got the attention. The median price of an existing single-family home sold in August was $475,000. That is down 1.0% from August 2025 and $15,000 below the all-time high of $490,000, which the valley set back in May and June of 2026. Sales volume fell too. A total of 2,252 existing homes, condos, and townhomes changed hands in August, with total sales down 11.9% from July. Single-family sales were down 1.7% year over year.

The condo and townhome number went the other way on price. At $299,900, the August median for condos and townhomes was up 0.6% compared to August 2025. It is a small gain. It is not a boom. But in a month where the bigger, more expensive half of the market slipped, an increase of any size on the attached-housing side is worth noticing.

There is one more piece of the condo picture that matters just as much as the price. Condo and townhome inventory without offers rose 6.0% year over year to 2,714 units. That means more attached homes are sitting on the market waiting for a buyer than there were a year ago. At the same time, condo and townhome sales were down 7.4% year over year. So you have more choices available and fewer people competing with you to grab them.

Put those three facts together and the shape of it gets clear. Prices held and ticked up slightly. Available supply grew. Buyer activity slowed. That is not the profile of a market that is running away from buyers. That is the profile of a market where a prepared buyer has room to negotiate.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

The gap between $475,000 and $299,900 is about $175,000. That is not a small difference in monthly payment. For a first-time buyer, a single person, a young couple, or anyone who has watched the valley median climb past what their paycheck can carry, that gap is the entire question of whether buying is possible at all this year.

Most people in Clark County who feel locked out of homeownership are looking at the single-family number and stopping there. They see $475,000, they run rough math on the payment at today's Nevada mortgage rates, and they decide it does not work. Then they stop looking. What they are missing is that the attached-housing lane is a real, functioning, well-supplied part of the same market, and it sits at a completely different price point.

This matters for current owners too, just in a different direction. If you own a condo or townhome in the valley, your value did not follow the single-family slide this month. Your segment held steady and edged up slightly year over year. That is useful to know if you have been putting off a sale because you assumed the whole valley was falling. It also matters if you were planning to sell your condo and move up to a house. When the lower rung holds and the upper rung dips, the distance between them shrinks, and move-up math gets a little friendlier.

For renters, the story is about options. Rising condo and townhome inventory means more units competing for the same pool of buyers. Some of those units belong to owners who tried to sell, did not get their number, and will end up renting them out instead. More attached supply in the valley tends to show up eventually as more rental choice as well, even when the original owner never planned it that way.

There is a psychological piece to this as well. A lot of valley residents have spent two years reading that Las Vegas housing only goes one direction, and that story has made people passive. They assume the decision is out of their hands. The August data says otherwise. When one segment of a market cools and another holds, the buyer who knows the difference gets to act, and the buyer who only reads headlines gets to wait. That is a choice, not a market condition.

And for families comparing neighborhoods, the condo lane opens up locations that a $475,000 budget might not reach in a detached house. Attached housing exists close to the resort corridor, near the Henderson employment centers, around the medical district, and in established parts of the valley where new detached construction simply is not happening anymore. Location per dollar is often the real argument for a condo, not just the sticker price.

Background and History

Condos and townhomes have never tracked single-family homes perfectly in this valley. They move on their own rhythm, and the August 2026 split is a good reminder of that.

The record median for Southern Nevada condos and townhomes is $315,000, set in October 2024. So the August 2026 figure of $299,900 is still below that peak, by roughly $15,000. The attached market has been working its way back toward its own high rather than setting new ones every quarter. Compare that to the single-family side, which hit $490,000 in May and June of 2026 before sliding to $475,000 in August. Two segments, two different peaks, two different timelines.

Part of why they behave differently comes down to who buys them and how. Attached homes in Clark County draw a heavier share of first-time buyers, downsizers, investors, and part-time residents. That mix reacts to interest rates and to the local job picture in its own way. Single-family demand in the valley leans more on move-up buyers and on families relocating here, and that group is more sensitive to what is happening with their current home equity.

Financing is the other big difference, and it is one that trips people up. Condo purchases often involve an extra layer of review, because lenders look at the health of the homeowners association and the building itself, not only at the borrower. Some complexes are approved for certain loan programs and some are not. That approval status can change over time, and it can quietly shrink the buyer pool for a specific building without affecting the neighborhood next door. It is one reason condo pricing and condo inventory can drift away from the single-family trend for months at a stretch.

The other long-running factor is investor interest. Attached homes in Southern Nevada have always been a popular target for buyers who want a rental unit, because the entry price is lower and the exterior maintenance is handled by the association. When investor appetite rises, condo prices firm up even if owner-occupant demand is flat. When investors step back, condo inventory builds fast. That swing is a big part of why the attached median can gain 0.6% in a month when the detached median is losing 1.0%.

Supply history matters here too. Southern Nevada built a large amount of attached housing during the 2000s, then very little for years after the crash. A lot of today's condo and townhome stock in the valley is a couple of decades old, sitting in established associations with established reserve funds and established maintenance schedules. That is not a bad thing. It just means the quality range between one complex and another can be wide, and averages across 2,714 available units hide a lot of variation.

What Happens Next

The next Las Vegas REALTORS report will cover September 2026 and will land in early October. That is the number to watch, and the question is simple. Does the condo and townhome median hold near $299,900 while single-family keeps easing, or do the two segments start moving together again?

Fall is normally the slower half of the year for Southern Nevada housing. Families who wanted to be moved before the school year started are already done. Sales counts usually cool from here into the holidays, which means the 11.9% month-over-month drop from July to August is partly the calendar doing what the calendar always does. What is not seasonal is the year-over-year comparison, and on that measure condo and townhome sales were down 7.4% while prices still nudged up.

Inventory is the piece most likely to shift the balance. If that 2,714 unit count of condos and townhomes without offers keeps climbing through the fall while buyer activity stays soft, sellers on the attached side will start meeting buyers on price, and the small year-over-year gain could flatten or reverse. If inventory stalls or pulls back as unsold listings expire, the $299,900 median has a decent chance of holding into winter.

Watch the record too. The condo and townhome median would need to climb about $15,000 from $299,900 to retake the October 2024 high of $315,000. At the current pace of 0.6% a year, that is not happening soon. A buyer today is not buying into a segment that just set a record. That is a meaningfully different position than the single-family side occupied a few months ago at $490,000.

Mortgage rates will decide a lot of it. Nevada borrowers have been quoted meaningfully above the national headline average, and condo loans sometimes carry their own pricing adjustments on top of that. Any real move in rates this fall will show up in the condo lane quickly, because the buyers there are the most payment-sensitive group in the valley.

Ryan's Take

I have watched a lot of buyers give up on Las Vegas in the last two years because they looked at one number and decided the answer was no. The August report is the clearest argument I have seen all year that the one-number approach costs people money.

Here is the honest version. A condo or a townhome is not the same product as a detached house on its own lot. You share walls. You pay an HOA fee every month, and you need to read the association's budget, reserve study, and rules before you fall in love with the unit. Some Las Vegas complexes are well run and well funded. Some are not, and that shows up later as special assessments or as trouble getting financing when you go to sell. That homework is not optional, and it is the single biggest thing I push condo buyers on.

But when the homework checks out, the math right now is genuinely attractive. You have 2,714 attached homes sitting without an offer, which is 6.0% more than last year. You have fewer buyers competing. You have a price point about $175,000 below the single-family median. In this market, that combination is the closest thing Clark County has to an open door for a first-time buyer, and I would rather someone walk through it with their eyes open than sit on the sidelines waiting for a $475,000 house to get cheap.

What You Can Do

Start with a local mortgage quote, not a national headline. Ask the lender specifically whether they finance condos, what their condo approval process looks like, and how their pricing changes on an attached unit versus a detached house. Getting that answer first saves you from falling for a unit in a complex your loan cannot touch.

Next, ask for the association documents early in the process, not at the end. You want the current budget, the reserve study, the rules and restrictions, and the minutes from recent meetings. Look for planned major projects like roofs, paint, plumbing, or parking lot work, and look for whether the reserves can actually cover them. Also check the rental cap and the pet rules if either one applies to you, because those clauses are where people get surprised after closing.

Then compare total monthly cost, not just list price. A $299,900 unit with a modest HOA fee that covers water, trash, exterior maintenance, and insurance can carry a very different monthly number than a $299,900 unit in a complex with high dues and few inclusions. Run both against what a detached home at the valley median would actually cost you each month. Sometimes the condo wins by a mile. Sometimes it is closer than you expect. You will not know until you put the numbers side by side.

It also helps to shop by complex, not just by unit. Two listings at the same price in two different associations can be very different purchases. Drive the property at night and on a weekend. Look at the parking, the landscaping, the common areas, and whether deferred maintenance is visible from the sidewalk. A well-kept complex tells you the association is collecting and spending its dues responsibly, and that protects your resale value more than any single upgrade inside the unit.

Finally, watch the monthly Las Vegas REALTORS release. It is public, it is local, and it is the only data set that tells you what is actually happening in Clark County instead of what is happening nationally.
