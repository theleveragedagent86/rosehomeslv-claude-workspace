CCSD Breaks Ground on a $150 Million Pre-K Through 8th Grade Campus

The Clark County School District broke ground on a new $150 million James Cashman campus that will serve students from prekindergarten all the way through 8th grade in one building. The project replaces a school building that is roughly 60 years old, and it is an unusual move for a district that almost always keeps elementary schools and middle schools separate.

That combination matters. A consolidated pre-K through 8 campus means a family could enroll a four year old and not change schools again until high school. For parents who have spent years juggling two drop-off lines at two different bell times, that is a real quality of life change. For anyone who owns a home zoned to this campus, it is also a long-term value story.

Here is what the district announced, why a nine-figure school build is landing in the same season as district-wide budget cuts, and what a brand new campus usually does to the homes around it.

What Happened

On September 9, 2026, the Clark County School District held a groundbreaking ceremony for the future James Cashman prekindergarten through 8th grade campus. The Las Vegas Sun reported the project carries a price tag of about $150 million. That places it among the largest single school construction projects the district has launched in recent years.

The most notable part of the announcement is the grade configuration. CCSD runs one of the largest school systems in the country, and the standard model here is an elementary school for kindergarten through 5th grade, then a separate middle school for 6th through 8th. A single campus that carries a student from prekindergarten to 8th grade is uncommon for the district. When CCSD does build one, it is usually because the site, the enrollment pattern, or the age of the existing buildings makes consolidation the smarter option.

The campus is a replacement project, not a brand new school on empty desert. The building being replaced is about 60 years old. That puts its original construction somewhere in the mid-1960s, back when the valley population was a small fraction of what it is today. Buildings from that era were designed for a different student, a different curriculum, and a very different set of expectations around technology, air conditioning, security, and accessibility.

The other detail worth circling is the program design. According to the Sun, the new campus puts a stronger focus on vocational education for younger students. That means career and technical style learning is being pushed down into the middle grades instead of waiting until high school. In practice that usually looks like hands-on labs, applied science and engineering spaces, and coursework that connects classroom work to actual trades and careers.

CCSD has not published a public opening date that we could verify for the finished campus [NOT VERIFIED]. Large district construction projects of this size typically run two to three years from groundbreaking to the first day of class, so families in the zone should plan around that general window rather than a firm date.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

School quality is one of the strongest forces in the Las Vegas housing market, and most buyers underestimate how strong it is. When a family calls me about a neighborhood, the school question usually comes up in the first five minutes. It comes up before the question about the yard, before the question about the HOA, and sometimes before the question about the price.

A new building changes the answer to that question. A 60-year-old campus carries baggage that has nothing to do with the teachers inside it. Older buildings have aging HVAC, dated layouts, smaller classrooms, tighter parking, and security features that were designed for a different era. Buyers walk the campus, or they drive past it, and they make a judgment in about ten seconds. A modern campus with new construction removes that objection entirely.

The pre-K through 8 structure adds a second layer of appeal. Parents in Clark County deal with a lot of transitions. A child starts at one school, moves to another for middle school, then moves again for high school. Each move resets the social network, the commute, the after-school routine, and the parent-teacher relationships. A campus that holds a student for ten straight years cuts one of those resets out completely. For a two-income household with more than one kid, that is not a small thing. It is one drop-off instead of two.

The vocational focus matters for a different group of families. Not every Clark County student is aiming at a four-year university, and not every parent wants that path. Las Vegas runs on hospitality, construction, health care, logistics, and the trades. A middle school that starts exposing kids to applied and technical work earlier is pointed directly at the local economy. Families who work in those industries tend to notice that, and it is the kind of thing that comes up in neighborhood Facebook groups long before it shows up in a listing description.

There is also a plain financial angle. Homes zoned to newer, better-regarded campuses tend to hold value better in a softening market. Southern Nevada just posted a median existing single-family price of $475,000 in August 2026, down about 1% from a year earlier. In a market that is flattening, the neighborhoods with a clear, specific advantage are the ones that keep their buyer pool. A brand new $150 million campus is about as specific an advantage as a neighborhood can get.

Renters feel this too. A large share of Clark County households rent, and school assignment follows the address the same way it does for owners. Families who rent near an aging campus often move the year their oldest starts kindergarten. A new campus gives those households a reason to stay put, which is good for landlords and good for neighborhood stability. Lower turnover on a street tends to show up in better upkeep and stronger comps over time.

Background and History

To understand why this project stands out, you have to look at the rest of the district's year. In the same week the district was celebrating a nine-figure groundbreaking, it was also announcing a $51.6 million budget reduction for the fall. Of that, $29.1 million comes out of individual school budgets and $22.5 million comes out of the central office through a 2.5% across-the-board cut. The district pointed to an enrollment drop of more than 10,400 students year over year as the reason.

Those two headlines look contradictory, but they are not. School districts in Nevada run capital construction money and operating money on separate tracks. Capital dollars come from bond programs and dedicated property and sales tax revenue that voters approved specifically for building and fixing schools. Operating dollars pay salaries, supplies, and programs. Money set aside to build a campus cannot legally be redirected to pay teachers. So CCSD can break ground on a $150 million school and cut $51.6 million from operations in the same month without anyone being confused about the math.

What the enrollment decline does affect is the logic behind consolidation. When a district is losing students, keeping a large number of separate, half-empty, aging buildings open is expensive. Each one needs its own principal, its own office staff, its own custodial team, its own utilities, and its own maintenance budget. Combining grade levels onto one modern campus reduces that overhead over the long run. A pre-K through 8 model is one of the cleaner ways to do it without closing a neighborhood school outright.

This is also not the only major school build in the valley right now. A separate $92.9 million, 108,000-square-foot elementary school is under construction in Skye Canyon at North Shaumber Road and Skye Canyon Park Drive, with an August 2027 opening and a naming vote scheduled for October. Put those two projects together and CCSD has roughly a quarter billion dollars of new school construction going up at the same time it is cutting operating costs. That is the real picture of the district in 2026. It is spending on buildings while shrinking on staff.

It is worth remembering how the valley got here. Clark County grew explosively from the 1990s through the 2000s, and CCSD built schools as fast as it could to keep up. Many of those campuses went up quickly on tight budgets. Meanwhile the buildings from the 1960s kept getting patched rather than replaced. Replacement projects like this one are the district finally catching up on the oldest end of its inventory, and there are more of those buildings still standing than most residents realize.

The pre-K piece is newer than it looks. Adding prekindergarten seats to a campus reflects a statewide push to expand early childhood education access in Nevada, which has historically ranked near the bottom nationally for pre-K availability. Putting those classrooms inside a neighborhood campus instead of a separate center makes them easier for working parents to actually use.

What Happens Next

The immediate next phase is site work and foundation construction. Anyone living near the campus should expect the normal construction pattern: heavy equipment early in the morning, truck traffic on the surrounding streets, temporary fencing, and dust control measures. Clark County construction projects of this size typically run standard daytime hours, but the first several months are the loudest and dustiest stretch.

After that, watch for CCSD to publish the attendance zone boundaries for the finished campus. This is the single most important document for any homeowner in the area, and it is the one most people miss. A new school does not automatically change which campus your address feeds into. The boundary map does. If your home ends up inside the new pre-K through 8 zone, that is a meaningful addition to your listing. If it ends up just outside, it is not. Boundaries for new schools are usually released and voted on well ahead of opening day, and there is often a public comment window before the board votes.

The other thing to track is the CCSD Board of School Trustees agenda. Construction contracts, change orders, boundary adjustments, and naming decisions all run through public board meetings. The Skye Canyon naming vote scheduled for October is a good example of how these items surface with little warning and get decided quickly. If you care about the outcome, the agenda is public and the meetings take public comment.

Finally, keep an eye on the vocational program details as they are released. The district has said the campus will emphasize career and technical learning for younger students, but the specific pathways, lab spaces, and partnerships have not been detailed publicly yet [NOT VERIFIED]. Those details will determine whether this is a genuine draw for families or just a line in a press release.

One more practical item for neighbors: during a multi-year build, traffic patterns around the site will shift more than once. Construction entrances move as the project progresses, and temporary bus routing or parent drop-off changes are common at replacement campuses where students are still attending nearby. Check with the school office at the start of each semester rather than assuming last year's route still works.

Ryan's Take

I have watched new school openings move neighborhood demand in this valley for years, and the pattern is consistent. A brand new campus does not create a housing boom on its own, but it removes objections, and removing objections is most of the job in a slower market. In 2026, with the valley median down about 1% year over year and August sales off 11.9% from July, sellers need every advantage they can get. A new zoned school is a real one.

What I like about this particular project is the pre-K through 8 structure. Buyers with young kids are doing long-range math whether they say it out loud or not. They are thinking about where their three year old will be in fifth grade. A campus that covers ten years of a child's education answers a question the family has not even asked yet, and that is exactly the kind of detail that turns a maybe into an offer. I would put it in the first paragraph of a listing for any home inside that zone.

The honest caution is timing. Groundbreaking is not opening day, and a two to three year build means the families buying today are buying the promise, not the product. If you are pricing a home right now based on a school that does not exist yet, you will be disappointed. The value shows up as the building goes vertical and again when the doors open, not the week after the shovels hit the dirt.

What You Can Do

Start by confirming what your address is actually zoned to today. CCSD publishes a school zone lookup tool on its website where you enter your address and get your assigned elementary, middle, and high school. Do this even if you think you know the answer, because boundaries get adjusted more often than most homeowners realize. Then save that result so you can compare it when the new campus boundaries are published.

If you want a voice in how this project lands, the CCSD Board of School Trustees meets regularly and posts its agendas in advance. Boundary changes, construction items, and school naming decisions are all board actions with public comment periods. Showing up or submitting a comment during the boundary discussion is far more effective than complaining after the vote. The same goes for the vocational program design, which is still being shaped.

For buyers, use this window. If you are shopping in a neighborhood near the campus, the new school is not fully priced into the market yet because it is two or three years out. That gap is an opportunity. For sellers, the opposite applies. If your home is zoned to an older campus that is being replaced, be straightforward about it in your marketing and lead with the replacement timeline rather than hoping nobody asks.

And if you are simply trying to figure out which Clark County neighborhoods are set up well for the next five years of school changes, that is a conversation worth having before you start touring homes, not after.
