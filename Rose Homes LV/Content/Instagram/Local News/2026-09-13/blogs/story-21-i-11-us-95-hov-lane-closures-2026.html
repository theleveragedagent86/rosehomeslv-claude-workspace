HOV Lanes Stay Closed on I-11 and U.S. 95 Through September

If you drive I-11 or U.S. 95 through the northwest valley, your carpool lane is gone for now. HOV lanes are closed in both directions between Rancho Drive and Rainbow Boulevard through September while crews work on the NDOT and RTC SafeTech Corridor project.

The work covers about five miles of freeway from Summerlin Parkway to the Spaghetti Bowl. That is one of the busiest stretches of road in Clark County, and it is the main artery connecting Summerlin, Centennial Hills, and the rest of the northwest to downtown Las Vegas.

The short version: expect a slower drive right now, and expect a safer and smarter freeway when it is finished. Here is what is happening, why it matters if you live or plan to buy out there, and how to plan around it.

What Happened

The Nevada Department of Transportation and the Regional Transportation Commission of Southern Nevada are running a project called the SafeTech Corridor along I-11 and U.S. 95. The project area runs roughly five miles, from Summerlin Parkway on the west end to the Spaghetti Bowl interchange on the east end.

To do that work safely, crews need room. The HOV lanes, also called carpool lanes or high occupancy vehicle lanes, are the lanes closest to the median. That is exactly where a lot of the work is happening. So NDOT closed the HOV lanes in both directions between Rancho Drive and Rainbow Boulevard. Those closures run through September 2026.

The name SafeTech tells you the goal. This is a safety and technology upgrade to the corridor, not a lane-adding expansion. That means things like improved signage, better traffic monitoring, updated striping and barriers, and technology that helps the agencies see and manage traffic in real time. The idea is to reduce crashes and to move traffic more intelligently on a freeway that already carries an enormous daily load.

For drivers, the practical effect is simple. If you normally use the carpool lane to skip past the slowdowns between Rancho and Rainbow, that option is not available right now. Everyone is funneled into the general purpose lanes. When you take a lane away from a freeway that is already near capacity during rush hour, the remaining lanes absorb the difference. That is why the drive feels heavier than the calendar alone would suggest.

NDOT and RTC have been messaging this project since the spring. The agencies typically post closure schedules ahead of time and adjust them based on weather, materials, and how the work is progressing. Closure windows on projects like this can shift. Always check the current status before you plan a trip around a specific ramp or lane.

It also helps to understand where the segment begins and ends. Rancho Drive sits on the east side near the older central part of the valley. Rainbow Boulevard sits west of that, closer to the neighborhoods that fill in toward Summerlin. The stretch between them is where the 95 narrows psychologically for a lot of drivers, because that is where merges, ramps, and heavy traffic all stack up at once. Taking the HOV lane out of that specific segment is why the closure feels bigger than five miles of orange cones would suggest.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

This corridor is not an obscure stretch of road. I-11 and U.S. 95 through the northwest is the commute for tens of thousands of Clark County residents. If you live in Summerlin, Centennial Hills, Skye Canyon, Lone Mountain, or anywhere along the 215 and 95 corridor, this is how you get downtown, to the medical district, to the Strip, and to the airport.

Losing the HOV lane hits a specific group hardest: the people who were actually carpooling or driving during the HOV-restricted hours to save time. If your household built a routine around that lane, whether that is a shared ride to work, a two-person commute downtown, or a daily run to a school on the other side of the valley, your buffer just disappeared. Add ten or fifteen minutes to your morning and plan accordingly.

There is also a ripple effect on surface streets. When freeway traffic slows, drivers bail out and take Rancho Drive, Decatur Boulevard, Jones Boulevard, and Craig Road instead. Those arterials pick up the overflow. So even if you never touch the freeway, you may notice your neighborhood streets feeling busier at 7:30 in the morning and 5:30 in the evening.

For homeowners along the corridor, there is a noise and dust factor too. Freeway construction runs with heavy equipment, and some of the work happens overnight because that is when traffic is lightest. If your backyard faces the 95, you have probably already heard it. That is temporary, and it is the price of getting the work done without shutting the freeway down in the middle of a weekday.

Families feel this in ways that go beyond work. School drop-off, youth sports, doctor appointments, and weekend trips to the Strip or the airport all run through this corridor. A parent who used to make it from Centennial Hills to a downtown office in twenty-five minutes now needs to rethink the whole morning. Small delays compound when you have two kids at two different schools and a start time you cannot move.

Renters feel it too. Rental demand in the northwest is strong partly because the area offers newer homes at a lower price per square foot than the central valley, with a commute people consider acceptable. When that commute gets harder, some renters start looking closer in. That does not flip the market overnight, but it is the kind of pressure that shows up in leasing conversations before it shows up in any report.

The longer view is more positive. A safer, better-instrumented corridor is a real asset for the northwest valley. Commute quality is one of the top three things buyers ask me about when they are looking at Summerlin and Centennial Hills. Anything that makes the drive downtown more predictable helps property values in every neighborhood that feeds into this freeway.

Background and History

U.S. 95 has been the northwest valley's lifeline for decades. As Summerlin, Centennial Hills, and the communities around them grew, the freeway grew with them, but never quite fast enough. Anyone who lived here through the 2000s remembers the years of widening work along the 95, and then the beltway buildout that was supposed to take pressure off it.

The I-11 designation is more recent. Interstate 11 is the long-term corridor concept meant to connect Phoenix to Las Vegas and eventually north toward Reno and beyond. Portions of existing Las Vegas freeway, including stretches of U.S. 95, carry the I-11 shield today. That dual designation is why you will see both numbers used for the same asphalt in project documents and news coverage.

Clark County's growth is the underlying story here. The valley added population faster than almost any metro in the country over the last thirty years, and the northwest was one of the biggest growth zones. Master planned communities filled in, schools went up, and the daily flow of cars from the northwest toward the center of the valley became one of the heaviest commute patterns in Southern Nevada.

Building new lanes is expensive and slow, and there is not unlimited room in the right of way. That is part of why agencies across the country have shifted toward technology-first approaches. Better sensors, ramp metering, dynamic message signs, and improved incident response can squeeze more usable capacity out of a freeway that already exists. That is the thinking behind a SafeTech style corridor upgrade.

HOV lanes themselves have a complicated history in Southern Nevada. They were added to encourage carpooling and to give buses and multi-passenger vehicles a reliable option during peak periods. Drivers have argued about them ever since, with some saying the lanes sit underused while the general lanes crawl, and others pointing out that the whole point is to reward people for taking a car off the road. Whatever side you land on, the lanes are part of the corridor's design, and taking them offline temporarily changes how the freeway behaves.

The Spaghetti Bowl on the east end of the project deserves its own mention. That interchange, where I-15, U.S. 95, and I-515 all tie together, has been the subject of its own multi-year rebuild effort. Anything that improves flow and safety on the approach to it helps the entire valley, because a backup there does not stay there. It spreads.

What Happens Next

The HOV lane closures between Rancho Drive and Rainbow Boulevard are scheduled to run through September 2026. Once the crews finish that phase, the carpool lanes in that segment should reopen. Work on other parts of the five mile corridor may continue after that, with different lanes and ramps affected at different times.

Watch for phased changes rather than one clean finish line. Projects this size move segment by segment. You may find the HOV lane back open between Rancho and Rainbow while a new restriction pops up closer to the Spaghetti Bowl or out toward Summerlin Parkway. Nighttime and weekend closures are common during the striping and barrier phases.

There are a few specific things worth watching. One is whether the HOV lane reopens with the same rules it had before, or whether the agencies use the project as a chance to adjust hours or eligibility. Another is how the new traffic technology performs once it is live, especially during incident response when a crash shuts down a lane. The third is simply how the corridor handles the next big event weekend, because Las Vegas measures its roads against convention traffic and stadium traffic as much as against a normal Tuesday.

The best move is to set expectations for the rest of the year rather than the rest of the month. Anyone commuting this corridor should assume some level of active work through 2026 and plan for occasional surprises. When the full corridor upgrade is complete, the payoff is a freeway with better safety features and better real-time traffic management on one of the valley's most important routes.

Ryan's Take

I show homes in Summerlin and Centennial Hills constantly, and commute time comes up in almost every conversation. Buyers moving from California or the Midwest ask how long it takes to get downtown. Buyers already living here ask whether the drive is getting worse. Right now, honestly, it is a little worse. Construction does that.

But I would not let a temporary closure change a long-term decision. If you love a house in Skye Canyon or Providence and the only hesitation is that the 95 is torn up this fall, remember that you are buying the neighborhood for the next five or ten years, not for September. A corridor that comes out of this project safer and better managed is a plus for every northwest neighborhood that depends on it. Road work is disruptive in the moment and quietly valuable afterward.

The practical advice I give clients is to actually drive the commute before you write an offer. Do it at 7:30 on a Tuesday morning, not at noon on a Saturday. That tells you what you are really signing up for. And if the current construction is skewing your test drive, drive it twice and give yourself an honest picture of what normal looks like.

One more thing worth saying plainly. I have never seen a northwest valley listing lose value because of a road project that ended. I have seen plenty of buyers talk themselves out of a great house over a temporary inconvenience, then watch that same house appreciate while they kept renting. Be realistic about the commute, but keep the construction in the right size box.

What You Can Do

Start with information. NDOT posts current lane and ramp closures for Southern Nevada projects, and the RTC publishes traffic and transit updates for Clark County. Checking before you leave takes thirty seconds and can save you a frustrating detour. Local news outlets also run weekly road work roundups that are worth a scan on Sunday night.

Second, build in a buffer. If you have a hard start time downtown or an early appointment at the medical district, leave ten to fifteen minutes earlier than you would have last year. That single change removes most of the stress. If your employer offers flexible start times, shifting your commute even thirty minutes off peak makes a noticeable difference on this stretch.

Third, know your alternates. Rancho Drive, Decatur Boulevard, and the 215 beltway all give you options depending on where you are headed. RTC bus routes serve much of the northwest corridor as well, and for some downtown commutes transit is genuinely competitive when the freeway is backed up. Try a route on a low-stakes day so you know whether it works before you need it.

Fourth, speak up if something is not working. NDOT and RTC both take public comment, and the RTC board meets regularly with time set aside for residents. If a detour is routing heavy traffic through a residential street near a school, that is exactly the kind of feedback agencies can act on quickly. Clark County and the City of Las Vegas also field complaints about surface street impacts tied to freeway work.

Fifth, drive like there is work happening, because there is. Construction zones in Nevada carry doubled fines, and the people standing behind those barriers are a few feet from moving traffic. Slow down, put the phone away, and give yourself room to merge. The most common crashes in a work zone are rear-end collisions caused by drivers who did not expect the car ahead to stop.

Finally, if you are house hunting along this corridor, factor the construction into your timeline but not into your fear. Ask your agent about what is planned nearby, not just what is happening today. Knowing the road map for the next few years is part of knowing the neighborhood.
