Mascots and Superheroes Hit 9 Summerlin-Area Schools With a "Slow Your Roll" Message for Drivers

On September 9, mascots and costumed characters stood outside nine west valley schools holding signs that asked drivers one simple thing. Slow down in the school zone. It was the visible face of the "Class is Back, Slow Your Roll" School Zone Safety Initiative, and it landed in Summerlin neighborhoods right at the hour when the most cars and the most kids share the same stretch of road.

At William Lummis Elementary School, the lineup was hard to miss. Chance from the Vegas Golden Knights, Aviator from the Las Vegas Aviators, Stomper from the Athletics, McGruff the Crime Dog from the Las Vegas Metropolitan Police Department, and ReRide from Professional Bull Riders all turned out. The idea is not subtle, and it is not supposed to be. A giant Gila monster holding a sign gets your foot off the gas faster than another yellow sign you have driven past a thousand times.

The campaign is run by Howard Hughes Communities and Summerlin, with the Las Vegas Metropolitan Police Department, the Clark County School District Police Department and PedSafe Vegas taking part. For west valley parents, this is the rare safety push you can actually see from the driver's seat on a Tuesday morning.

What Happened

The event took place on September 9, 2026, across nine schools in the west valley. Costumed characters and mascots fanned out to school zones and held signs telling drivers to slow down. The message on the campaign is "Class is Back, Slow Your Roll," and the timing lines up with the stretch of the calendar when school traffic patterns are still new and drivers are still adjusting.

William Lummis Elementary School drew the biggest roster of characters. Chance, the mascot for the Vegas Golden Knights, appeared alongside Aviator from the Las Vegas Aviators and Stomper from the Athletics. McGruff the Crime Dog represented the Las Vegas Metropolitan Police Department, and ReRide came from Professional Bull Riders. Five recognizable characters at one elementary school is a lot of visual noise pointed at a single behavior, which is the whole point.

The initiative is organized by Howard Hughes Communities and Summerlin. Partners include the Las Vegas Metropolitan Police Department, Clark County School District Police and PedSafe Vegas. That mix matters. One of those groups builds and manages the neighborhoods, two of them write the citations, and one of them studies pedestrian safety for a living. Getting all four pointed at the same problem on the same morning is not a small piece of coordination.

PedSafe Vegas Director Erin Breen is quoted saying that 434 children were hit during the last school year. That figure has been reported at different values at different points. FOX5 Vegas reported 432 in August 2026, and a Clark County task force figure from July 2026 was 427. The underlying count comes from Clark County School District Police and has been restated more than once as records get reviewed. The honest way to say it is that the number sits in the low 430s and it is far too high no matter which version you use.

What the campaign is not is an enforcement sweep. Nobody got a ticket from a mascot. This was awareness work, staged at the exact location and the exact time of day where the risk shows up. The people who needed to see it were already driving past.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

If you live in Summerlin, Spring Valley, or anywhere in the west valley, you already know the morning rhythm. Between roughly 7 and 8 in the morning, arterial roads fill up, neighborhood streets turn into cut-throughs, and school zones collect a mix of drop-off parents, commuters trying to beat the light, and kids walking or riding to campus. Those three groups do not move at the same speed or watch for the same things.

A count in the low 430s of children hit during a single school year is not an abstraction in a county this size. It works out to several every week that school is in session. Most of those are not fatal, and that is worth saying plainly, but a child struck by a car at even 20 miles per hour is a life-altering event for a family and for the driver. The point of a school zone speed limit is that the same collision at a lower speed is usually survivable.

For homeowners, school zone safety is also a neighborhood quality issue. When parents do not feel safe letting a fifth grader walk six blocks to campus, more of them drive. More driving means more cars in the same pickup loop, which makes the zone more dangerous, which pushes more parents into cars. That loop feeds itself. Communities that break it get quieter streets, shorter pickup lines, and kids who can move around their own neighborhood without a chauffeur.

There is a practical angle too. School zone citations in Nevada are expensive, and enforcement near campuses has been climbing. A speeding ticket in an active school zone is not a parking ticket. It is the kind of thing that shows up on your record and on your insurance. If you commute through the west valley during school hours, the cheapest possible outcome is simply slowing down.

Renters and new arrivals feel this most. Someone who moved to Summerlin in July may not know where the school zones start, especially in newer village areas where the street grid is still filling in and campuses sit close to collector roads. A mascot holding a sign is a blunt instrument, but it is a very effective way to tell a newcomer that this particular stretch of road is different at this particular hour.

There is one more group worth naming. Older residents in the west valley walk more than people assume, especially in the cooler months and especially in villages built around trail systems. A crossing that is safe enough for a kid with a backpack is also the crossing that works for a neighbor with a walker. School zone improvements almost never help only children. They lower the speed of the whole street for everyone on it.

Background and History

School zone safety has been the loudest recurring safety story in Clark County this year. The child pedestrian count has been cited repeatedly by different agencies and different outlets, and the fact that the number keeps getting restated says something about how hard it is to track. Crashes near schools get logged by more than one agency, and a case can move between the school district police and the local department depending on where it happened.

PedSafe Vegas has been the steady voice on this beat. The organization focuses on pedestrian safety in Southern Nevada, and Director Erin Breen has been the person most often quoted attaching a number to the problem. That consistency is part of why the low 430s figure has traveled so widely this year, even as the exact count moved.

Summerlin is a natural place to stage a push like this. Howard Hughes Communities develops and manages the master plan, which means it has a direct relationship with the schools, the trail system, the parks, and the people who live there. A master-planned community can organize nine campuses in one morning in a way a scattered set of neighborhoods usually cannot. The village structure that makes Summerlin easy to navigate also makes it easy to coordinate.

Mascot programs are also not new here. Las Vegas is unusual in how many professional sports properties operate in one metro area, and those clubs have built real community programs around school visits and youth outreach. Pulling Chance, Aviator, and Stomper together with McGruff the Crime Dog and ReRide is a use of that bench that costs a family nothing and reaches kids and parents at the same time.

It is also worth understanding why school zones are treated differently from any other stretch of road. A school zone is not just a place where children are present. It is a place where children are present in large numbers, at a predictable hour, moving in unpredictable ways, often at a height where a driver in a tall vehicle cannot see them over the hood. Every one of those factors makes the same road more dangerous at 7:30 in the morning than it is at 2 in the afternoon. The reduced speed limit exists because that combination is unusually unforgiving.

The other piece of history here is the shift in what families drive. Vehicles in Southern Nevada have gotten taller and heavier over the last two decades. A taller front end means a longer blind zone directly in front of the bumper, and more mass means a longer stopping distance from the same speed. None of that is a moral failing on the part of anyone who owns a truck or an SUV. It just means the margin for error in a school zone is smaller than it was when a lot of today's parents were kids walking to their own campus.

What Happens Next

The September 9 event was a single morning, but the underlying problem runs the length of the school year. School zone risk does not fade after the first month. It shifts. Days get shorter through fall, which means more drop-offs and pickups happen in low light. Holiday traffic thickens the arterials in November and December. Spring brings more kids walking and riding once the weather turns pleasant again.

Enforcement is the part to watch. The Las Vegas Metropolitan Police Department and Clark County School District Police were both partners in this campaign, and both agencies have been active around school zones this year. Awareness events like this one usually run alongside stepped-up patrols, though the specific enforcement schedule following this event has not been published. [NOT VERIFIED]

For parents in the nine participating schools, the practical next step is the walking route itself. If your child walks or bikes, the days right after a safety push are a good time to walk the route with them once and look at it like a driver would. Where does a car turning right have to look left? Where does a hedge or a parked truck block the view of the crossing? Those are the spots that matter.

Whether the campaign repeats at the same nine schools or expands to other parts of the valley has not been announced. [NOT VERIFIED] Given that Howard Hughes Communities and Summerlin anchor it, the most likely pattern is a recurring back-to-school push tied to the start of each year.

One thing to watch over the next few months is whether the reported child pedestrian count for this school year comes in below the low 430s. That is the real scoreboard. Awareness campaigns are easy to run and hard to measure, and the only measurement that counts is fewer kids hit. If the number moves down, this kind of coordinated back-to-school push becomes much easier to justify at more campuses across the valley.

Ryan's Take

I show homes all over the west valley, which means I am in these school zones constantly, usually at the worst possible hour. I can tell you that the drivers causing problems are almost never doing anything dramatic. They are going 32 in a 15 because they are late, or they are looking at a phone in a pickup line, or they are making a U-turn in the middle of a block because the loop was full. It is ordinary distraction, repeated by a hundred people in the same 20 minutes.

That is exactly why a campaign like this works better than it sounds like it should. A sign you have seen five hundred times stops registering. A seven foot tall Gila monster in a Vegas Golden Knights jersey holding that same message does register, and it registers on the people who actually drive that road. Buyers ask me about schools constantly. They rarely ask about the street outside the school, and they should, because the walkability and the safety of that first quarter mile is a big part of what daily life in a neighborhood actually feels like.

The other thing I would say to sellers is that this kind of community work is part of what you are actually selling. When a buyer asks me why homes in a master-planned community hold their value, the honest answer is not the gate or the paint color. It is that somebody is still paying attention. Trails get maintained. Parks get programmed. And on a Tuesday in September, somebody organizes five mascots to stand outside nine schools because kids are getting hit by cars. That is a community with a functioning nervous system, and buyers can feel it even when they cannot name it.

What You Can Do

Start with the easy one. Leave five minutes earlier on school days. Nearly every school zone speeding problem is a time problem, and five minutes of margin removes the pressure that makes people roll through crossings and take chances on turns.

Know where the zones are on your own route. School zone limits in Nevada are in effect during posted hours, and those hours are marked on the sign. If you drive the same road daily, take one trip to actually read the sign instead of assuming. This is especially true in newer Summerlin villages where campuses have opened in the last few years and the traffic pattern around them is still settling.

If your school needs crossing guards or volunteer help at drop-off, that is where a single parent makes the biggest difference. Contact your school office directly to ask what they need. Schools also field complaints about specific dangerous intersections, and those reports are how a crossing gets restriped, a sign gets moved, or a patrol gets assigned.

Talk to your kids about the crossing itself, not just the rule. The habit worth building is looking at the driver, not the light. A child who makes eye contact with a driver before stepping off the curb is a child who catches the car that is not going to stop.
