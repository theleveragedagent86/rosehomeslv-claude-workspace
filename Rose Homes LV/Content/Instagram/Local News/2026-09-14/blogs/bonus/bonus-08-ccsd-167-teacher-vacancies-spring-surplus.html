CCSD Started This School Year With 167 Teacher Vacancies, Down From 348

The Clark County School District opened the 2026 school year with 167 teacher vacancies, less than half of the 348 empty positions it had on day one the year before. District staff shared that number with the Nevada State Board of Education on September 9, 2026, as part of a staffing update.

For families across the valley, that is the headline worth knowing. Fewer open jobs on day one means fewer classrooms starting the year with a rotating cast of substitutes. But the same presentation carried a second set of numbers that tell a very different story. Falling enrollment pushed 707 positions into the district's spring surplus process, which moved hundreds of teachers out of the schools where they had been working.

So this is good news and complicated news at the same time. Clark County has more classrooms covered and fewer teachers staying put. Here is what the state board heard, and what it means if you live here, own a home here, or have a kid in one of these buildings.

What Happened at the September 9 State Board Meeting

The Nevada State Board of Education met on September 9, 2026. Clark County School District staff presented a staffing update, and the first number they gave was the day one vacancy count for licensed teaching positions. That count came in at 167. The prior year, the same count was 348.

That is a drop of 181 positions year over year. In plain terms, CCSD had roughly half as many teacher jobs sitting open on the first day of school as it did the year before. For a district the size of Clark County, that is a meaningful swing.

The district also reported on special education, which has been the hardest area to staff for years. CCSD listed 90 licensed special education vacancies on day one, down from 163 the year before. Special education roles require extra licensing and endorsements, and the pool of qualified candidates is smaller than it is for general classroom teaching. Cutting that number nearly in half is the kind of detail that does not make the evening news but matters enormously to the families it touches.

Then there is coverage. CCSD reported a 99.3 percent fill rate for substitutes and temporary coverage. That figure measures how often a requested substitute or temporary assignment actually gets filled by a person. A fill rate near 100 percent means that when a teacher is out, somebody shows up. It does not mean the person who shows up is a licensed teacher in that subject, and it does not measure how long a temporary assignment lasts. It measures whether the seat at the front of the room has an adult in it.

The second half of the presentation dealt with the spring surplus cycle. This is the process CCSD uses when a school's projected enrollment drops and the school no longer needs as many positions as it had. In the 2026 Spring Surplus Cycle, 707 positions were identified for surplus. That is 707 jobs that went away at the school where they existed.

Of the employees attached to those positions, 337 could not find a new position through the spring transfer process. The district reported that 103 of those employees later selected qualifying positions, 14 resigned, and 154 licensed professionals were placed into attrition vacancies, meaning open jobs created when other employees retired or left. The numbers presented to the board account for most but not all of the 337. The status of the remainder was not detailed in the presentation summary. [NOT VERIFIED]

One more piece of context helps make sense of the surplus figures. The 707 positions and the 337 employees are two different counts. A surplused position does not always have a person attached to it. Some of those 707 were already empty, so eliminating them moved no one. The 337 figure is the one that counts actual employees who finished the spring transfer window without a placement.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Start with the obvious one. If your child's school started the year with every teaching position filled, your child had a teacher on day one. That teacher can build a routine, learn the kids' names, and plan a full year. A classroom running on substitutes cannot do any of that in the same way, no matter how good the substitute is.

Cutting day one vacancies from 348 to 167 means roughly 180 more classrooms across Clark County started the year with a permanent teacher than started that way last year. Spread across the valley, that reaches neighborhoods from Summerlin to Henderson to North Las Vegas and out into the unincorporated areas. It is not evenly spread, and it never is, but the direction is clearly better.

The special education number matters even more for the families it affects. When a special education position sits open, the students assigned to that teacher still have legally required services in their plans. The school has to cover those services somehow, usually by stretching the staff it does have. Dropping from 163 open positions to 90 takes pressure off a system that has been carrying too much of it.

Now the harder part. The surplus numbers mean hundreds of teachers across Clark County did not return to the same school this fall. A teacher who spent five years at a campus, who knew the families, who ran the after school club, may now be on the other side of the valley. That churn is invisible in a vacancy count because the position is filled. It is very visible to the parents and kids who walk into the building.

There is also a commute story here. Clark County is enormous. A teacher surplused from a school near her home and placed at a school twenty miles away has a different daily life than she had in May. Some of those teachers make it work. Some of them decide the drive is not worth it and leave the district, which then creates a new vacancy somewhere else.

For renters and homeowners, school staffing is one of the quiet inputs into neighborhood desirability. Families ask about schools constantly. They ask about test scores and boundaries, but what they usually mean is whether the school feels stable. A building that keeps its teachers year after year feels stable. A building that reshuffles a chunk of its staff every spring does not.

That is not a small thing in a valley where school quality is one of the top questions on almost every buyer's list. Families relocating to Clark County from California, Arizona, or the Midwest ask about schools before they ask about square footage. When the district can say it started the year with half as many empty classrooms as last year, that answer travels. It shows up in relocation conversations, in neighborhood Facebook groups, and eventually in what people are willing to pay to live inside a particular boundary.

The flip side is worth saying too. Staffing news moves slowly into home values, if it moves at all. Nobody is repricing a house in Mountains Edge because of a vacancy report presented to a state board in September. What these numbers really do is shape the day to day experience of living somewhere, and that experience is what people describe to their friends when those friends ask where they should buy.

Background: Why 707 Positions Went Into Surplus

The surplus process is a function of enrollment. CCSD staffs schools based on how many students each campus is projected to serve. When projected enrollment at a school falls, the school is allocated fewer positions. The positions that go away are declared surplus, and the employees in them enter a transfer process to find another spot in the district.

The district identified falling enrollment as the driver behind the 2026 Spring Surplus Cycle. This is not a Clark County invention. Districts across the country have been working through enrollment declines, and the mechanics are always the same. Fewer students means fewer funded positions means staff moves.

What makes Clark County different is scale. This is one of the largest school districts in the United States. When a district this size runs a surplus cycle, 707 positions is the kind of number that comes out the other side. A smaller district might move a dozen people. Clark County moves hundreds.

It is worth being precise about what surplus is and what it is not. A surplus is not a layoff. The employee keeps a job with the district and goes through a transfer process to land somewhere else. That is why 154 licensed professionals ended up placed into attrition vacancies, which are the openings that appear naturally when other people retire or resign. The district used those natural openings to absorb surplused staff.

That also helps explain the vacancy number at the top of this article. Some of the reason day one vacancies dropped from 348 to 167 is that CCSD had a pool of surplused licensed staff available to fill openings. The two halves of this presentation are connected. Enrollment decline created a supply of teachers who needed placements, and those placements filled seats that would otherwise have been vacant.

What Happens Next

Watch the enrollment counts. Nevada districts report official enrollment early in the school year, and that count drives funding and staffing for the year ahead. If Clark County enrollment keeps sliding, the 2027 spring surplus cycle will look a lot like the 2026 one, and another round of teachers will move buildings next spring.

Watch the vacancy number as the year goes on. Day one vacancies are a snapshot. Teachers resign in October, go on leave in January, and retire in March. The number that matters over a full year is how quickly the district refills those seats, not just how few were open in August. Districts that start strong and drift through the year end up in the same place as districts that started behind.

Watch special education specifically. Ninety open licensed positions is much better than 163, but it is still ninety. That category has been the district's toughest staffing problem for a long time, and a single good year does not settle it.

The Nevada State Board of Education meets regularly and takes these updates on an ongoing basis. Board agendas and materials are posted publicly ahead of each meeting, and CCSD posts its own board agendas as well. If school staffing is something you track, those agendas are where the real numbers show up first, usually before any news coverage.

One more thing to keep an eye on is the substitute fill rate. A 99.3 percent rate is about as high as that measure goes, and it is worth asking whether it holds through flu season and the back half of the year. Fill rates almost always look best in August, when the substitute pool is fresh and demand is lowest. The January version of that number is the honest one.

Ryan's Take

I talk to buyers about schools every single week. Almost nobody asks me about teacher vacancy rates, but that is essentially what they are asking when they ask whether a school is any good. Stability is what people can feel when they walk into a building, and stability comes from having enough adults in the right rooms.

Going from 348 open positions to 167 is a real improvement, and I think it is fair to say so plainly. At the same time, I would not tell a client that Clark County school staffing is solved. Moving 707 positions through a surplus cycle is a lot of disruption, and the families at the affected schools felt it in August even if the vacancy chart looks healthier.

Here is the practical version for anyone buying in the valley right now. Do not pick a neighborhood off a school rating alone. Ratings lag by a year or more and they do not capture staff turnover at all. Talk to neighbors on the street you are considering. Ask whether their kids kept the same teachers, whether the front office feels settled, and whether the school has been growing or shrinking. That conversation will tell you more than any number I can pull up.

And if you are a teacher who got surplused this spring and you are now driving across the valley every morning, I understand why you might be thinking about moving closer to your new school. That decision is worth running the numbers on carefully, because the cost of the move and the cost of the commute do not always land where people assume. I am happy to help you think it through with no pressure attached.

What You Can Do

If you want the primary numbers rather than a summary, the Nevada State Board of Education posts its agendas and meeting materials publicly, and CCSD publishes its own board documents. The staffing presentation discussed here came out of the September 9, 2026 state board meeting, and materials from that meeting are part of the public record.

If you have a student in a Clark County school, the fastest way to understand your own building is to ask your principal directly. Ask how many licensed positions were open on the first day, whether the school was affected by the spring surplus cycle, and how many staff members are new this year. Principals generally know these answers cold, and most will tell you straight.

If you are shopping for a home and schools are driving your search, build your list around specific campuses rather than general areas. Clark County attendance boundaries can shift, and two homes a few blocks apart can feed different schools. Verify the boundary for the exact address before you write an offer, not after.

Finally, if you care about this beyond your own kid, the public comment period at state board and CCSD board meetings is open to anyone. Districts make staffing and budget decisions in public, and the rooms are usually not full. Parents who show up and ask specific questions about surplus, transfers, and special education staffing tend to get specific answers. That is a lot more useful than posting about it later.
