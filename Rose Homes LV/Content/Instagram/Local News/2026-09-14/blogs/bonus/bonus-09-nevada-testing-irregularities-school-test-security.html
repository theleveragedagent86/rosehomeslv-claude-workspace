Nevada Logged 933 Testing Irregularities Out of 1.5 Million Test Sessions Last Year

Nevada schools gave about 1,500,000 state test sessions during the 2025-26 school year, and 933 of them were flagged as testing irregularities. That is roughly 0.06 percent, or about one flagged session out of every 1,600. The Nevada State Board of Education received the annual test security summary on September 9, 2026.

That number sounds big until you put it next to the number underneath it. Nine hundred thirty three is a headline. One out of 1,600 is the actual story. For Clark County parents deciding where to buy a home or where to enroll a child, that difference matters, because state test results feed school star ratings, and star ratings move families and home prices.

Here is what the report found, what it recommends, and how much weight a parent should give it when comparing schools in Las Vegas, Henderson, or North Las Vegas.

What Happened at the September 9 Board Meeting

The Nevada State Board of Education met on September 9, 2026, and one of the items on the agenda was the annual test security summary for the 2025-26 school year. This is a routine report. The state collects reports of anything that went wrong during state testing, sorts them into categories, and brings the totals to the board once a year.

The headline figure was 933 reported testing irregularities. Those came out of roughly 1,500,000 individual test sessions given across Nevada public schools. Doing that math gives about 0.06 percent of sessions.

The report also broke the irregularities down by what actually went wrong. Improper test administration made up 38 percent of the total. That is the largest single category by a wide margin. It covers problems on the adult side of the room, not the student side. Student cheating or misconduct accounted for 24 percent. Testing interruptions accounted for 20 percent.

Add those three together and you get 82 percent of the 933 reports. The remaining share falls into other categories the summary tracks.

Read that breakdown again, because it is the part most people skip. The single biggest bucket is not students cheating. It is adults making mistakes while giving the test. Wrong instructions, a timing error, the wrong materials on a desk, a proctor stepping away. Those are process problems, and process problems have process fixes.

The report came with recommendations. The state suggested better web-based tracking software so irregularities get logged and followed more cleanly. It called for revised training materials for testing staff. It called for more frequent webinars for district staff so the training does not happen once in August and then get forgotten by March. And it called for more on-site monitoring by state staff during testing windows.

Each of those four recommendations targets a different weak point. Tracking software addresses how reports get collected and followed once someone raises a flag. Revised training materials address what proctors are told before testing starts. More frequent webinars address the gap between August training and spring testing. On-site state monitoring addresses what actually happens in the room on test day. Together they are a maintenance plan, not an emergency response.

The board heard a second education item the same day. Staff gave an update on the new Nevada District Performance Framework, the system the state is building to measure how whole districts perform rather than just individual schools. As part of that discussion, the board set a goal of raising the share of Nevada high school students who graduate with a College and Career Readiness diploma or an advanced diploma from 36 percent to 50 percent by the end of the 2030-31 school year.

That is a jump of 14 percentage points over roughly five graduating classes. It is an ambitious target, and it is the kind of goal that only moves if high schools expand the course pathways that lead to those diplomas.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

State test scores are not just a number in a folder. They roll up into Nevada's school star ratings, and star ratings are one of the first things a family looks at when they are choosing a neighborhood.

I see this every week. A buyer narrows a search to three areas, pulls up school ratings on a real estate site, and quietly drops one area from the list. That decision gets made in about forty seconds, and it is based on a rating that came from test data. So the integrity of that test data is not an academic question. It is a housing question.

The good news for Clark County families is that 0.06 percent is a very small share. A rate that low does not distort a school's results in any meaningful way. If a school building gave 5,000 test sessions in a year, a 0.06 percent rate works out to roughly three flagged sessions. Three flagged sessions do not move a star rating.

That is the proportion I want parents to hold onto. The number 933 was written to get attention. The rate is what tells you whether the system is working. A system that catches and logs 933 problems out of 1.5 million sessions is a system that is paying attention, not one that is falling apart.

There is a second reason this matters locally. Clark County School District is the largest district in Nevada by a wide margin, so a large share of those 1.5 million test sessions happened right here in Las Vegas, Henderson, North Las Vegas, and the unincorporated parts of the county. When the state improves proctor training or adds on-site monitoring, Clark County classrooms are where most of that work lands.

And the diploma goal touches families directly. Moving from 36 percent to 50 percent on College and Career Readiness and advanced diplomas means more Nevada graduates leaving high school with a credential that carries weight with employers and colleges. For a household deciding whether to put down roots here or move to another state for schools, that trend line is worth watching.

It also matters to the local economy in a way that eventually reaches the housing market. Employers looking at Southern Nevada for new offices, medical campuses, and advanced manufacturing all ask the same question about the workforce pipeline. A higher share of graduates with a readiness or advanced diploma is a talking point the region can use. More employers bringing higher paying jobs is the single healthiest thing that can happen to a housing market, because it grows demand through income rather than through speculation.

Renters have a stake here too. School quality is one of the biggest factors in where families choose to lease, and in Clark County a large share of households with school age children rent rather than own. A renter comparing two apartment communities in Spring Valley and Green Valley is often really comparing two attendance zones.

Background and History

Test security reporting is not new and it is not unique to Nevada. Every state that gives standardized assessments has a process for logging when something goes wrong, because federal and state accountability systems depend on the results being clean.

The categories in Nevada's summary reflect how testing actually happens in a school. A state test is not one event. It is thousands of separate sessions spread across dozens of rooms, given by regular classroom teachers and support staff who proctor a handful of times a year. Every one of those adults has to follow a script, manage timing, handle devices, and keep materials secure.

That is why improper test administration is the largest category at 38 percent. It is not evidence of bad faith. It is what you get when you ask a very large number of people to execute a detailed procedure a few times a year. The fix for that is training and monitoring, which is exactly what the report recommended.

The 20 percent for testing interruptions has an equally ordinary explanation. Fire alarms, power blips, network drops, and schedule conflicts all interrupt test sessions. Nevada gives most state assessments on computers now, so a connectivity problem in one lab can generate multiple flagged sessions at once.

Student cheating or misconduct at 24 percent is the category people assume is the whole report. It is roughly a quarter of it. Spread across 1.5 million sessions, that works out to a very small number of incidents statewide.

The District Performance Framework discussion has its own history. Nevada has rated individual schools for years through the star rating system. Rating whole districts is a newer idea, and it is meant to hold district central offices accountable for things a single principal cannot control, like staffing, transportation, and program access.

That shift matters in a county the size of Clark. A principal at a single campus cannot fix a bus route, hire a district wide special education coordinator, or open a new career and technical program on their own. A district level framework puts those decisions on the record where families can see them.

It is also worth remembering why any of this reporting exists. Standardized testing carries real weight in Nevada. Results feed school ratings, they inform where the state directs support and funding, and in some cases they factor into graduation requirements for students. When a system carries that much weight, the state has to be able to show the results were collected properly. The annual security summary is that proof of work.

What Happens Next

The recommendations in the summary are next steps, not finished work. Better web-based tracking software, revised training materials, more frequent webinars, and more on-site state monitoring all have to be built, funded, and scheduled before they show up in a classroom.

The practical timeline follows the testing calendar. Training materials get revised over the summer and rolled out before the next testing window. Webinars run during the school year. On-site monitoring happens during the actual testing windows in spring. So the earliest a parent would see the effect of these changes is the 2026-27 testing cycle, with the results reported to the board a year after that.

The number to watch next year is not the raw count. It is the rate and the mix. If the improper administration share drops below 38 percent, the training changes are working. If the total count rises but the improper administration share falls, that most likely means better tracking software is catching more of what was always happening, which is a sign of a healthier system rather than a worse one.

On the diploma goal, the board set a five-year target ending with the 2030-31 school year. That gives the state and districts several graduating classes to move the number from 36 percent toward 50 percent. Expect annual progress updates, and expect College and Career Readiness diploma pathways to get more attention at the high school level in Clark County.

There is one more thing to watch. If the state adds on-site monitoring during testing windows, some of that monitoring will happen in Clark County schools simply because that is where most of the testing happens. Principals and testing coordinators here should expect more state presence during the spring window than they have seen in past years.

Ryan's Take

I read a lot of school reports because my clients ask about schools constantly. This one is a good example of a story where the headline number and the real finding point in different directions.

Nine hundred thirty three flagged sessions out of 1.5 million is a clean rate. If anything, it tells me the reporting system is functioning, because a state with no irregularities reported is a state that is not looking. The categories back that up. The biggest bucket is adults making procedural mistakes, and the state's response is training and monitoring, which is the right response to a procedural problem.

What I would tell a buyer is this. Do not let a single headline number change how you think about a school or a zip code. Test integrity at this rate is not affecting star ratings in any neighborhood in Clark County. Go visit the school, talk to the front office, ask about class sizes and programs, and look at trends over several years rather than one data point. That is a better use of your time than reacting to a number pulled out of a board agenda.

The diploma goal is the part of this meeting I will be following closely. Test security is plumbing. It needs to work, and right now it looks like it is working. Whether half of Nevada graduates leave high school with a readiness or advanced diploma by 2031 is a much bigger question for this valley, and it will show up in wages, in employers, and eventually in home values.

What You Can Do

If you want to follow this yourself, the Nevada State Board of Education posts its agendas and supporting documents online ahead of each meeting, and the annual test security summary is filed as one of those agenda items. You can read the actual report rather than a summary of a summary.

State Board meetings also include public comment. If you have a concern about how testing is handled at your child's school, that is a real avenue, and it carries more weight than a social media post. Board meeting dates are published in advance.

At the school level, ask your principal or school testing coordinator how proctor training works at that campus and what the testing schedule looks like for the year. Most schools will tell you directly. If your child reports that something went wrong during a test session, report it to the school. That is how an irregularity gets logged in the first place, and the reporting only works if people use it.

And if you are choosing a neighborhood, look at more than one year of data. A school's trend over three to five years tells you far more than any single report or rating snapshot.
