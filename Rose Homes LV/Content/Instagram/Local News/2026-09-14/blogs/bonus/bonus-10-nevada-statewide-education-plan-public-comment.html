Nevada Opens Public Comment on a Five-Year Statewide Education Plan Through October 21

Nevada is asking you to read and react to the plan that will guide its schools for the next five years, and the window closes on October 21. The Nevada Department of Education presented a draft of the Statewide Plan for the Improvement of Pupils to the Nevada State Board of Education on September 9, then opened a public comment period that anyone in the state can use.

If you have a kid in a Clark County classroom, teach in one, or just pay attention to how schools are run here, this is the part of the process where your name can actually land on the document. The plan is built around five strategic priorities, and the department says it wants feedback before the final version goes to the State Board and the governor in November.

Here is the short version of how to comment. Read the draft plan posted on the Nevada Department of Education website, then submit your feedback through the department's online survey. If you need accessibility help with the survey, the department lists ndeinfo@doe.nv.gov as the contact for ADA assistance. Do it early, because October 21 is a hard stop and the department has to leave itself time to sort through what comes in.

What Happened

On Wednesday, September 9, 2026, the Nevada Department of Education brought the draft Statewide Plan for the Improvement of Pupils, known as the STIP, to the Nevada State Board of Education. The STIP is a five-year plan focused on improving student outcomes and strengthening alignment across the state's education system. Presenting it to the State Board is the step that starts the public phase.

With that presentation done, the department opened the public comment period. It runs through October 21. The department specifically invited educators, families, students, community members, and other education partners to review the draft and give feedback. That is a wide invitation. You do not have to be a teacher or a school board member to weigh in.

The draft plan is posted on the Nevada Department of Education's website, and feedback is collected through an online survey the department is hosting. The department also published an email address, ndeinfo@doe.nv.gov, for anyone who needs ADA assistance with the survey. That matters, because a comment process only works if people can actually use it.

The draft is organized around five strategic priorities. They are Strong Foundations, Empowering Pathways, Equipped Educators and Leaders, Informed and Connected Families, and Aligned Systems. The department says those five came out of input from Nevadans around the state along with the expertise of school and district staff. Together they are meant to support a vision where, every day, Nevada students are building knowledge of the world, engaging with grade-level content and instruction, solving complex problems, and making progress toward their goals after high school.

Superintendent of Public Instruction Dr. Victor Wakefield tied the plan directly to what he heard on the road. "In my first 100 days, I traveled across Nevada, visiting schools and meeting with students, educators, trustees, and district leaders. I heard a consistent message: we need greater clarity about where we are headed and how we get there," Wakefield said. "This plan responds to that call. It establishes a clear direction for what we want students to experience every day and a focused plan for how we will work together to deliver better outcomes. Now, we are asking Nevadans to help us make that plan stronger."

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Clark County is where most of Nevada's students are. A statewide plan is not an abstract Carson City document when the majority of the classrooms it touches sit in Las Vegas, Henderson, North Las Vegas, and the unincorporated parts of the county. Whatever the final plan says about reading, career pathways, teacher support, or family communication will show up in schools our neighbors send their kids to.

Two of the five priorities are aimed squarely at the people reading this. Informed and Connected Families is about the relationship between schools and the households they serve. Equipped Educators and Leaders is about the adults in the building. If you have ever felt out of the loop on what your child is actually learning, or watched a good teacher leave, those are the priorities where your comment has the clearest home.

There is also a practical reason to care about the plan even if you do not have kids in school. Schools are one of the first things buyers ask about when they look at a neighborhood in Clark County. The quality and the reputation of local schools move demand. A five-year plan that sets clear goals and clear measures is the kind of thing that eventually shows up in school ratings, in enrollment patterns, and in which parts of the valley families compete for.

Public comment periods usually attract the same small group of regulars. Organized voices show up, and everyone else assumes someone will speak for them. When a plan is going to steer the system for five years, that is a lot of ground to hand over. A few thousand Clark County parents and teachers filling out one survey would change the weight of the feedback the department is sorting through in late October.

It also helps that the department opened the door to students. The invitation names students directly, not just adults speaking on their behalf. A high school junior in Henderson who has an opinion about career pathways or about what the last two years of school should look like is exactly the kind of voice a plan about postsecondary goals should be hearing from. That is worth mentioning at your dinner table.

One more thing to keep in mind. This plan sits above your school district, not inside it. Comments about a single campus, a single principal, or a single bus route belong somewhere else. What belongs here is the pattern you keep running into, the thing you would expect any Nevada school to handle well. Frame your comment that way and it fits the document.

Background and History

Nevada's statewide education plan is not a new idea and it is not optional. It is required under NRS 385.930 through 385.934. The law calls for a plan that establishes goals, strategies, and measures of progress for improving student outcomes. In other words, the state is obligated to say out loud what it is trying to do and how it will know whether it worked.

What changed recently is how the plan gets built. Senate Bill 460 from the 2025 Legislative Session revised the development process. Under that change, the Nevada Department of Education prepares a five-year plan and reviews it annually, and it does that work in consultation with the State Board of Education, school districts, and other education boards, commissions, and councils. So the version now out for comment is the first big product of that revised process.

That shift is worth understanding, because it explains the shape of the draft. A one-year plan can be a list of activities. A five-year plan with an annual review has to be built around priorities that survive more than one budget cycle, plus measures the department can check every year. The draft reflects that. It names the strategies the department will pursue, the outcomes it expects, and the measures it will use to track progress. It also commits the department to using the state's resources, information, support, and authority in a coordinated way so schools and districts get real help instead of mixed signals.

The scale involved is easy to underestimate. The Nevada Department of Education works with the state's 17 school districts and the State Public Charter School Authority, and it oversees all pre-K through 12 education in Nevada. Its work reaches nearly half a million children and about 30,000 educators. The department keeps offices in Carson City and in Las Vegas, with the Las Vegas office at 2080 E. Flamingo Road.

It is also useful to know what the STIP is not. It is not a district budget, and it is not a curriculum. It is the state's own statement of direction, plus the accountability piece that comes with it. The draft commits the department to coordinating the state's resources, information, support, and authority so that schools and districts get consistent help. That is the promise a reader should test when they go through the draft.

Dr. Wakefield's framing of the plan came out of his first 100 days on the job, traveling the state and meeting students, educators, trustees, and district leaders. The message he reported hearing was not about any single program. It was about clarity. People wanted to know where the system was headed and how it planned to get there. That is a fair summary of what a STIP is supposed to provide.

What Happens Next

The calendar from here is short and specific. Public comment closes October 21. After that, the department will review the feedback it received and make appropriate revisions to the draft. There is no second comment round announced, so October 21 is the date to plan around.

The final STIP is expected to be presented to the State Board of Education and submitted to the governor in November. That is roughly three weeks after comment closes, which tells you something about how the department intends to work. Feedback that arrives early and is specific has a better chance of being sorted, grouped, and acted on than a flood of comments that lands in the last 48 hours.

Once the plan is final, the annual review built into the revised process becomes the ongoing point of contact. A five-year plan that gets reviewed every year is a plan that can be adjusted, which means the measures it picks now will be the measures people argue about later. If you care about how progress gets counted, the comment period is the cheapest moment to say so.

Keep an eye on the annual review too. Because Senate Bill 460 directs the department to review the five-year plan every year, this will not be a document that gets filed and forgotten. Each year there is a built-in moment where the goals, the strategies, and the measures come back up. That is good news if the first version lands short in a spot that matters to you.

Watch the State Board of Education agenda in November for the item on the final STIP. State Board meetings are public, and the agenda is where you will see what actually changed between the draft and the final version. Comparing the two documents is the simplest way to tell whether the public comment period moved anything.

Ryan's Take

I sell homes in Clark County, and schools come up in almost every conversation with a family. Not test scores in the abstract. Specific questions. Is the elementary school close enough to walk? Does the middle school have the program my kid needs? Will the high school actually get them to a job or a college? Those questions are exactly what a plan like this is supposed to answer at the system level, and it is unusual to get a direct shot at the document before it is locked.

My honest read is that the value here is in the measures, not the priority names. Five priorities with nice titles are easy to agree with. What makes a plan real is what the state promises to count and report every year. If you are going to spend 15 minutes on the survey, spend most of it on that part. Say what you want measured and what you want published, in plain language, about your own school or your own kid's grade level.

The other thing I would say is that this is a low-cost civic act with a long shelf life. You are not going to a meeting, you are not signing up for a committee, and nobody is asking for money. You are reading a draft and answering a survey before October 21. For a plan that runs five years across nearly half a million Nevada students, that is a pretty good trade.

What You Can Do

Start by reading the draft. The Nevada Department of Education has posted the draft STIP on its website. You do not need to read every page. Find the two priorities that touch your life most, likely Informed and Connected Families or Equipped Educators and Leaders for most parents and teachers, and read those sections closely.

Then submit your comment through the department's online survey. Do it this week rather than in October. Comments that arrive early get read while there is still room to revise. If you need ADA assistance with the survey, the department lists ndeinfo@doe.nv.gov as the contact.

Be concrete when you write. A comment that says "communication with families needs work" is easy to file away. A comment that names the grade level, the specific breakdown you experienced, and the measure you would use to fix it is much harder to ignore. The same goes for teachers. Name the support you actually need and the thing you would want the state to track every year.

If you want a simple structure, use three lines. Line one, say who you are and which of the five priorities you are writing about. Line two, describe the problem or the thing that is working, using a real example from your own experience. Line three, name the measure you want the state to publish each year. Three honest lines beat three pages of general frustration every time.

Teachers and school staff have one more angle worth using. Equipped Educators and Leaders is the priority written about you. If the plan is going to name strategies for supporting the adults in the building, the people in the building should be the ones telling the state what support actually looks like on a Tuesday in October. That feedback does not have to come through anyone else.

Finally, pass it along. Tell one other parent at pickup, one teacher, or one neighbor with school-age kids. Then mark November on your calendar and check the State Board of Education agenda for the final plan so you can see what changed.
