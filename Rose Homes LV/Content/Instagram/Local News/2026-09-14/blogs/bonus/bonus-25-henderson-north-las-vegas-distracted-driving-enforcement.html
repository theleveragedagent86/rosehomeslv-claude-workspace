Henderson and North Las Vegas Police Wrap Distracted Driving Crackdowns

Henderson Police stopped 317 drivers in about three weeks this past August and wrote 71 of them a ticket for distracted driving. North Las Vegas Police ran its own campaign at nearly the same time and made 188 traffic contacts. Both departments have now closed out those enforcement pushes, and the numbers are public.

This was not a random week of extra patrols. Both campaigns ran under Joining Forces, a statewide traffic safety program that targets distracted driving, impaired driving, speeding, seat belt use, and pedestrian safety. Nevada funds it, local agencies run it, and the results get reported back.

If you commute on Green Valley Parkway, Craig Road, or any of the big surface arterials that move Clark County around every day, the takeaway is simple. Officers were out, they were looking, and the phone in your hand was near the top of the list.

What Happened

Henderson Police ran its Joining Forces campaign from August 14 through September 1, 2026. Over that stretch officers stopped 317 motorists. The citation breakdown tells you what they were finding on the road.

Officers issued 71 distracted driving citations. They issued 107 speeding citations. They issued 23 citations for intersection violations, the category that covers things like running a red light or blowing through a stop. And they issued 37 other traffic citations on top of that. Add it up and the vast majority of those 317 stops produced paper.

Henderson did not pay for all of that out of its regular patrol budget. The department received $174,500 in grant funding from the Nevada Department of Public Safety Office of Traffic Safety for the 2026 program. That money is what pays for the extra officers and the extra hours that make a saturation campaign possible in the first place.

North Las Vegas Police ran a campaign of its own from August 7 through August 31, 2026. That effort produced 188 traffic contacts, three arrests, 167 citations, and 63 warnings. The department logged roughly 95 overtime hours getting it done.

Those two sets of numbers are worth looking at side by side. Henderson stopped more drivers overall. North Las Vegas wrote 167 citations out of 188 contacts, a very high rate, and it also handed out 63 warnings, which suggests officers were using some discretion on the lower level stuff. The three arrests are a reminder that a traffic stop sometimes turns up something bigger than a ticket.

The Joining Forces program is the connective tissue here. It is not a Henderson thing or a North Las Vegas thing. It is a statewide framework that lets agencies across Nevada schedule coordinated enforcement periods around a shared set of priorities. When several departments run campaigns in the same window, the effect on a region like Clark County adds up fast.

The two campaigns also overlapped in time. North Las Vegas started August 7 and Henderson started August 14, and both were still running through the last week of the month. For anyone whose daily drive crosses both cities, and plenty of people in this valley work in one and sleep in the other, late August meant driving through two active enforcement zones at once.

It is also worth being clear about the language in these reports. A traffic stop and a traffic contact are the same basic thing, an officer pulling a driver over. A citation is a ticket. A warning is a documented stop that does not carry a fine. Henderson reported its results as stops plus citations by category. North Las Vegas reported contacts, citations, warnings, and arrests. Different departments format their wrap ups differently, which is why the two sets of numbers do not line up column for column.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Start with the money. A distracted driving citation in Nevada is not a parking ticket. It carries a fine, it can carry points, and points can move what you pay for car insurance. Seventy-one Henderson drivers found that out in a three week window. Multiply the cost of one ticket by the time and hassle of dealing with it, and the phone glance that caused it looks like a bad trade.

Then look at the speeding number. Henderson wrote 107 speeding citations, more than any other single category in that campaign. That is the quiet story inside this story. Distracted driving got the headline, but speed was the most common thing officers were writing up. On wide, straight, well lit Clark County arterials, it is easy to drift ten or fifteen over without noticing. Enforcement campaigns exist precisely because that drift is so easy.

There is a neighborhood layer to this too. The corridors these campaigns focus on are the same roads that run past our schools, our parks, our shopping centers, and our front doors. Green Valley Parkway is not a highway. Craig Road is not a highway. They are neighborhood roads that carry highway volumes at certain hours. A driver looking at a screen on one of those roads is a risk to people walking to a bus stop or a corner store.

Pedestrian safety is explicitly part of what Joining Forces targets, and that matters in a valley built the way ours is. Clark County has long blocks, wide crossings, and a lot of people who walk or take transit as their main way to get around. The distance between a curb and the far side of a six lane arterial is real. Enforcement that slows traffic down and pulls eyes back to the road is part of how that crossing gets safer.

Commute time is the other piece. Clark County is a driving valley. Most of us do not have a train option, and the trip from a Henderson neighborhood to a job on the north side can eat an hour of a day in each direction. When an arterial gets crowded and drivers get impatient, that is exactly the mix that produces the speeding and intersection violations these campaigns are written up around. The 23 intersection citations Henderson issued are the quiet ones on that list, because an intersection violation is the kind of thing that turns into a collision the one time it does not work out.

Seat belt compliance is on the Joining Forces priority list too, and it is the cheapest safety habit anyone has access to. It costs nothing, takes a second, and every single person in the car can do it. If you have kids who are old enough to buckle themselves, the habit is worth checking rather than assuming.

Finally, there is the household budget angle that most people do not connect to a police press release. Insurance rates in a household are one of those fixed monthly costs that quietly shape what a family can afford. A ticket or two on a driving record can move that number. If you are planning a home purchase, every recurring monthly cost matters when a lender looks at your debt to income ratio. Keeping a clean record is not glamorous, but it is part of staying financially ready.

Background and History

Joining Forces is the umbrella Nevada uses to coordinate traffic enforcement across agency lines. Rather than every department running its own scattered efforts, participating agencies schedule campaigns around shared priority areas. The five named in this round were distracted driving, impaired driving, speeding, seat belt compliance, and pedestrian safety.

The funding comes through the Nevada Department of Public Safety Office of Traffic Safety. Henderson's share for the 2026 program was $174,500. Grant funded enforcement works differently from routine patrol. Departments schedule officers on overtime specifically for the campaign, which is why North Las Vegas reported about 95 overtime hours as part of its results. Those hours are dedicated to the campaign rather than pulled away from regular calls for service.

The reason distracted driving keeps getting its own line item is that it changed shape over the last decade. It used to mean fumbling with a radio or a cheeseburger. Now it means a device that is designed to hold your attention, sitting in the same space where your eyes are supposed to be pointed at the road. Nevada, like most states, responded with a hands free law and with enforcement periods built around it.

Clark County's street grid is part of the background too. This valley grew fast and it grew outward, which produced a network of very wide, very long arterial roads laid out on a mile grid. Those roads move a huge amount of traffic efficiently, and they also make it easy to go faster than you meant to. A road designed to feel open is a road where the posted limit and the comfortable speed do not always match. Enforcement is one of the levers cities have to close that gap, alongside engineering changes like signal timing and crossing improvements.

The timing of these two campaigns was not an accident either. Mid August is when Clark County School District is back in session and the valley's morning traffic pattern changes overnight. School zones activate, bus routes fill in, and thousands of additional short car trips get added to neighborhood streets at the exact hours when kids are walking. Running a distracted driving and pedestrian safety push in that window is about as well aimed as enforcement gets.

Reporting the results publicly is part of the design. A campaign that happens quietly and is never mentioned again does not change behavior after it ends. A campaign whose numbers get published gives every other driver in the valley a reason to think twice next time. That is a big part of why departments put out these wrap up figures at all.

What Happens Next

Both campaigns are over. Henderson's ended September 1 and North Las Vegas wrapped August 31. That does not mean normal traffic enforcement stopped, it means the funded surge period closed and the departments went back to their standard staffing for traffic.

Joining Forces campaigns run in waves throughout the year, so more enforcement periods are likely ahead. Specific future dates for Henderson or North Las Vegas have not been announced. [NOT VERIFIED] Watch each department's public information channels if you want a heads up when the next one starts.

The practical thing to watch for is timing. Enforcement waves tend to cluster around parts of the calendar when roads get busier or riskier. The back to school stretch in August is one of those windows, and both of these campaigns landed squarely inside it. Holiday periods are another typical focus for impaired driving enforcement across the state.

Grant funding is the other thing to keep an eye on. Henderson's $174,500 from the Office of Traffic Safety covers the 2026 program. Grant cycles renew, and the amount a department receives shapes how many campaign hours it can staff. If you ever wonder why enforcement feels heavier some months than others, funding cycles are a large part of the answer.

Also worth watching is whether the published numbers move over time. If a department runs the same kind of campaign next year on the same corridors and writes noticeably fewer distracted driving citations, that is a signal the message is landing. If the count holds steady or climbs, expect the enforcement to keep coming.

Ryan's Take

I drive this valley every single day. Showings in Henderson in the morning, an inspection in North Las Vegas in the afternoon, back across town for a listing appointment. I am on Green Valley Parkway and Craig Road and everything in between more than most people, and I can tell you the phone problem is not subtle. You can spot it at a red light without trying.

So a number like 71 distracted driving citations in one Henderson campaign does not surprise me. What I actually find useful about these reports is what they say about the roads themselves. When a city and a state put $174,500 and a bunch of overtime hours into slowing down traffic on the arterials that run through residential areas, that is a city paying attention to the streets where people live. Buyers ask me about schools and HOA fees all the time. They ask about traffic less often, and they probably should, because the road you back out onto every morning is part of daily life in a home just as much as the kitchen is.

What You Can Do

The easiest step is the most obvious one. Put the phone somewhere you cannot reach it. A mount with navigation set before you pull out of the driveway solves almost every reason people pick a phone up mid drive. Set your music, set your route, then leave it alone until you park.

If you have a teen driver in the house, these numbers are a better conversation starter than a lecture. Henderson stopped 317 people and wrote 107 speeding citations in under three weeks. That is a concrete, local, recent fact, and it lands better than a general warning about being careful.

For neighborhood level concerns, both cities have channels for reporting recurring traffic problems on a specific street. If you have a corner where drivers consistently run the stop, or a stretch where speeds are clearly out of line with the posted limit, report it to your city rather than assuming someone else already has. Enforcement resources get allocated partly on where problems are documented, and grant funded campaigns like these pick target corridors somewhere.

It also helps to think about the trip before you start it. A lot of phone use behind the wheel comes from being rushed, not from being careless. Leaving five minutes earlier removes the reason to check a message at a light or to push the speed on a long green. Five minutes is a small price for not owning one of those 107 speeding citations.

And if you are house hunting, drive the street at the hours you would actually be using it. Nine in the morning on a Saturday tells you almost nothing about what a road feels like at 5:15 on a Tuesday. Go see it for yourself before you commit.
