Grand Prix Road Work Hit Koval Lane First, and the Bus Detour Came With It

Before the Harmon Avenue closures started, crews already shut lanes on Koval Lane at Rochelle Avenue for 2026 Las Vegas Grand Prix road work. That overnight job ran from 9 p.m. on September 2 to 6 a.m. on September 3, and traffic was briefly stopped in all directions around 2 a.m. The Regional Transportation Commission also put a detour in place for Route 119, the Koval and Simmons line, and that detour affected four stops.

If you drive Koval to get to work, or you ride Route 119, that is the part of this story that actually changes your night. We covered the Harmon Avenue overnight closures in F1 Harmon Avenue Lane Closures. This piece is about the Koval Lane side of the work and the bus detour, which that earlier post did not carry.

The race itself is not until November 19 to 21. The road work started in early September. That gap is the whole point. Residents in the resort corridor are living with construction for months before a single car takes a lap.

What Happened on Koval Lane

Road work for the 2026 Las Vegas Grand Prix started in early September. The first piece that hit local drivers was on Koval Lane at Rochelle Avenue. Crews reduced lanes there overnight, starting at 9 p.m. on September 2 and clearing by 6 a.m. on September 3.

During that window, traffic was briefly stopped in all directions around 2 a.m. That is a full stop, not a lane shift. If you rolled up to Koval and Rochelle at the wrong moment that night, you sat and waited. It did not last long, but it was a hard stop rather than a slow crawl.

The second piece is the one most people heard about. From September 7 through September 18, overnight lane reductions run on Harmon Avenue between Las Vegas Boulevard and Koval. Those run from 9 p.m. to 6 a.m. each night, with one lane open in each direction. That stretch of Harmon connects the Strip to Koval, so it is a short segment that carries a lot of local traffic.

The piece that got the least attention is the transit side. The Regional Transportation Commission put a detour in place for Route 119, which runs the Koval and Simmons corridor. Four stops were affected by that detour. Riders who use those four stops had to walk to a different pickup point or plan around a changed route. The specific stop list was not included in the reporting we are working from, so treat the exact stop names as [NOT VERIFIED] and check the RTC site for the current version.

The race weekend itself is November 19 to 21. Everything happening now is preparation, not the event. That is a long runway of work between early September and mid November.

It helps to picture the geography. Koval Lane runs behind the east side of the Strip. Harmon Avenue crosses it and ties Koval back to Las Vegas Boulevard. Rochelle Avenue is a smaller east to west street that meets Koval in the same pocket. So the September work touched a compact set of streets that sit right on top of each other. If you live or work in that pocket, one closure is enough to reroute you.

Notice also what the work did not do. The Harmon segment kept one lane open in each direction. That is a lane reduction, not a full closure. You could still get through. The Koval night at Rochelle was the one with a brief full stop, and even that was around 2 a.m. and short. The difference matters when you are deciding whether to change your route or just leave early.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Thousands of locals work in the resort corridor. Koval Lane and Harmon Avenue are not tourist roads. They are the back way. They are how housekeepers, dealers, cooks, security officers, valet staff, and hotel engineers get to their shift without sitting on Las Vegas Boulevard behind a rideshare line.

Look at the hours on this work. Nine p.m. to 6 a.m. That is not a random window. It is the window picked to keep daytime traffic moving. It is also the exact window when graveyard shift workers are driving. A swing shift ending at 11 p.m. and a graveyard shift starting at 11 p.m. both land inside it. So does the 6 a.m. day shift arrival, right at the edge.

The people least likely to be inconvenienced by 9 p.m. to 6 a.m. work are people who commute at 8 a.m. and drive home at 5 p.m. The people most likely to be inconvenienced are the ones who keep the resorts running overnight. That is a real cost, and it falls on the same households every time this corridor gets worked on.

The bus detour compounds it. Route 119 serves Koval and Simmons. A worker who does not drive, or who shares one car with a partner on a different shift, depends on that route. Losing four stops means a longer walk in the dark, often at the end of a long shift, sometimes in summer heat that has not broken by 10 p.m. It is a small change on paper and a big change in practice.

There is a housing piece too. The apartments and condos east of the Strip, in the pocket bounded roughly by Koval, Harmon, Flamingo, and Paradise, exist largely because people want to live close to resort jobs. A five minute commute is the entire value proposition. When months of construction turn that five minutes into fifteen, the neighborhood's biggest selling point takes a temporary hit.

Parking and pickup change too. A lot of resort workers get dropped off or picked up by a family member at a specific corner because employee lots are far from the door. When a lane disappears on Harmon or Koval, those pickup spots get awkward fast. Cars stack up, someone circles the block, and a five minute handoff turns into fifteen. None of that shows up in a closure notice.

And there is the simple matter of sleep. Nine p.m. to 6 a.m. road work happens outside somebody's bedroom window. The apartments and condos along Koval and Harmon are close to the street. Backup alarms, compactors, and light towers are part of the job. For a household with a kid in school or someone who works days, nine straight nights of that is real.

Background and History

This is not the first year the resort corridor has gone through this. The Las Vegas Grand Prix runs on public streets. That is the appeal for the event and the problem for everyone who lives here. A permanent racetrack gets built once and then it sits there. A street circuit has to be built and unbuilt around a working city, every single year.

That means the same roads keep coming up. Koval Lane, Harmon Avenue, and the connections between them are part of the route between the Strip and the neighborhoods just east of it. Work on them is not a surprise. It is a seasonal pattern now, and residents in the area have learned to read the signs.

What has changed over the last few years is how the work is scheduled. Overnight windows, one lane kept open in each direction, and defined end dates are all signs of a process that has been tuned. The September 7 to 18 Harmon work has a stated finish. The Koval work at Rochelle was a single overnight. These are not open ended closures with no end in sight.

The transit response has also gotten more organized. Announcing a Route 119 detour in advance, and naming how many stops are affected, is better than riders finding out at the curb. It does not make the walk shorter, but it does let people plan.

The tradeoff is the one this valley has been arguing about since the race came back. The event brings money and attention. The prep brings months of cones, lane shifts, and detours to neighborhoods that do not get a cut of the ticket revenue. Both things are true at once, and reasonable neighbors land in different places on it.

One more piece of history worth holding onto. Transit riders have historically been the last group considered when a street project gets planned. A driver can pick a different road. A bus rider cannot pick a different bus. So when RTC announces a Route 119 detour with a stop count attached, that is the agency treating riders as people who need to plan, not as an afterthought. It is a small thing that says a lot about how the process has matured.

What Happens Next

The Harmon Avenue overnight work between Las Vegas Boulevard and Koval runs through September 18. After that date, that specific segment of the schedule is done. One lane stays open in each direction until then, so the road is passable, just slower.

Expect more work between now and the November 19 to 21 race weekend. Building a street circuit is a staged job. Paving, barrier placement, fencing, grandstand construction, and pedestrian bridge work all happen on their own timelines. The announced September work is the opening round, not the whole season.

For Route 119 riders, the detour is the thing to watch. Bus detours tied to construction tend to change as the construction moves. The four stops affected in September may not be the same four stops affected in October. Check before you leave the house rather than assuming last week's plan still holds.

If you drive Koval or Harmon on a night schedule, build in a buffer. A brief all directions stop like the one around 2 a.m. on September 3 is unpredictable by nature. Ten extra minutes covers most of it. Being late to a graveyard shift over a five minute delay is a bad trade when the fix is leaving a little earlier.

Employers in the corridor can help here, and some already do. A shift supervisor who knows Harmon is down to one lane can tell the crew before they leave home. A property manager who knows Koval is getting worked on can post it in the lobby. This information moves faster inside buildings than it does through official channels, and it is free to pass along.

Ryan's Take

I get asked about the resort corridor all the time, usually by someone weighing a condo east of the Strip because the commute would be almost nothing. My honest answer has not changed. The location is genuinely great, and you should go in knowing that this corridor gets torn up on a schedule now.

The thing buyers underestimate is that the work is annual, not one time. If you are buying near Koval, Harmon, Flamingo, or Paradise, you are buying into a neighborhood that hosts a street circuit build every fall. That is not a reason to walk away. It is a reason to ask which side of the building your unit faces, how the parking garage exits, and whether your normal route has an alternate.

On the value side, I have not seen this scare people off. Proximity to resort jobs is still one of the strongest demand drivers in Clark County, and short commutes hold their worth. The people who struggle are the ones who never thought about it and get surprised in year one. The ones who plan for it barely notice.

One practical note for sellers in that pocket. If you are listing this fall, the construction question is going to come up, because buyers read the news too. The best answer is a specific one. Name the streets, name the dates, say the work is overnight and seasonal. A vague reassurance makes people nervous. A clear explanation does the opposite.

What You Can Do

Check the RTC before you ride. Route 119 covers Koval and Simmons, and the detour affected four stops. RTC posts current detours and route changes, and that is the source that stays accurate as the work moves. If you ride that line to a resort job, checking takes thirty seconds and saves you a surprise at the curb.

Know a second way in. If Koval at Rochelle is your normal path, have an alternate ready for the nights it is not. Paradise Road, Flamingo Road, and Tropicana Avenue all connect the same general area. You do not need a perfect backup, just one you have actually driven so you are not figuring it out at 1 a.m.

Leave earlier on work nights. The 9 p.m. to 6 a.m. window is when the cones go out. A brief full stop can happen at any point inside it. Ten or fifteen minutes of cushion turns a stressful night into a normal one.

Tell your neighbors and coworkers. A lot of graveyard shift workers find out about a closure by driving into it. A quick text to the person on your crew who takes Koval is worth more than any official notice. Word of mouth is still how most of this valley learns about a lane closure.

Write the dates down. The Harmon Avenue work between Las Vegas Boulevard and Koval runs through September 18, from 9 p.m. to 6 a.m. The race is November 19 to 21. Those two dates on a calendar or a phone reminder cover most of what you need to know for the next two months.

If the noise or the detour is a real problem for your household, say so to the people who schedule this work. Clark County and the RTC both take public comment, and complaints from residents are what change work windows and stop placement in future years. Construction schedules are not fixed by nature. They get set by people, and people respond to feedback.
