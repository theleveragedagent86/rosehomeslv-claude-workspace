A Free Anime Convention for Teens Packed a Las Vegas Library, and Most Parents Never Heard About It

Teen anime and manga fans packed the Las Vegas-Clark County Library District for Teen animeFEST 2026 on Saturday, September 12. The whole thing was free. No ticket, no badge, no parking fee, no age gate beyond the grade range it was built for, which is sixth through 12th grade.

The Library District puts this convention on every year, and the 2026 edition landed at the Sahara West Library at 9600 W. Sahara Ave. in the 89117 zip code, on the west side of the valley at Sahara Avenue and Grand Canyon Drive. That is a neighborhood branch surrounded by rooftops, not a convention center out by the freeway. Families in The Lakes, Peccole Ranch, Spring Valley and the neighborhoods off Fort Apache and Durango can get there in about ten minutes.

Here is the part that surprises people. The library paid for a voice actor guest, a cosplay contest with prizes, taiko drumming, K-pop dancing, artist workshops, a boba stand and a food truck, and then charged local teenagers nothing to walk in. If you are a parent in Clark County who thinks of the library as a quiet room with books, this article is for you.

What Happened at Teen animeFEST 2026

Teen animeFEST is the Library District's yearly anime and manga convention for teens. The 2026 event ran on Saturday, September 12, and News 3 Las Vegas reported that fans packed the building. The convention was created for students in grades six through 12, so it covers middle school and high school in one event.

The special guest was voice actor Jenny Yokobori. If you have a teenager who plays Genshin Impact, that name will land. Yokobori is the voice behind Yoimiya in that game. She also voices Kuromi and Cinnamoroll for Hello Kitty and Friends, Black Lemonade Cookie in Cookie Run: Kingdom, and both Elice and Eleonora Yumizuru in Fire Emblem Heroes. For a kid who has heard that voice through a headset for hundreds of hours, meeting the person behind it is a real moment.

Organizers built out a full day. The schedule included Anime Jeopardy, artist workshops, a boba stand, a cosplay contest with prizes, and a cosplay panel. Attendees also had face painters and caricature artists, a food truck, games, K-pop dancing and taiko drumming performances. Vendors set up through the day selling anime-inspired merchandise, artwork and collectibles. The Library District described the day as a lively, immersive experience, and that tracks with the schedule.

The vendor floor is worth calling out on its own. Artist tables at a convention are where a lot of teenagers first see that drawing can be a job. A kid who doodles characters in the margins of a math worksheet walks past a row of working artists selling prints and stickers, and the idea stops being abstract. The workshops did the same thing from the other direction, putting instruction in front of kids who had never had a lesson.

The performances kept the day moving. Taiko drumming is loud, physical and impossible to ignore, and K-pop dance sets pull in kids who came for the games and stayed for the music. Add face painters, caricature artists and a boba stand, and you have a schedule that gives a shy 12-year-old about six different ways to participate without having to talk to a stranger first.

Stack that up against what a commercial convention charges for the same list of things. A guest signing, a costume contest with prizes, artist alley vendors and live performances usually come with a ticket price, a parking cost and a merch table markup. Here, the ticket price was zero and the venue was a public building your property taxes already support.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Families

Finding something for a 13-year-old to do in this valley that is safe, free and actually interesting is harder than it should be. Most of what gets marketed here is built for adults with a credit card. A free convention aimed squarely at middle and high schoolers fills a gap that Clark County parents complain about constantly.

Cost is the headline. A family with three kids can walk into Teen animeFEST and spend nothing. Compare that to a paid convention weekend, where badges, parking and food can run past a car payment. For households watching every dollar right now, a free full-day event within driving distance is not a small thing.

There is also the social side. Anime and gaming are how a lot of teenagers find their people. A kid who is quiet at school will talk for an hour about a character design with someone wearing the same costume. Events like this give shy teenagers a low-pressure way to meet others who are into the same thing, in a supervised public building, during daylight hours, with library staff around.

And it is close to home. Sahara West Library sits inside a residential part of the west valley, not on the Strip and not in a tourist zone. Parents can drop off and be back in fifteen minutes. That matters when you are deciding whether your kid gets to go at all.

Transportation is part of that. The branch page links straight to RTC transit directions, and the District even runs a program built around using library services while riding transit. A teenager without a license and without a parent free on a Saturday morning still has a way to get there. In a county this spread out, that is the difference between an event existing and an event being reachable.

Timing helps too. September is when the school year has just settled in and the summer programs have ended. A lot of Clark County families hit a stretch where there is homework but not much else, and weekends get long. Dropping a full convention into the middle of that month is good scheduling, whether the District planned it that way or not.

There is one more piece that does not show up on any flyer. Free public programs are how a community actually meets itself. Parents standing in the same hallway waiting on the cosplay contest end up talking. Kids from different middle schools end up in the same Anime Jeopardy round. That is how a neighborhood stops being a collection of houses and starts being a place.

Background: What the Library District Actually Does for Teens

The Las Vegas-Clark County Library District runs 25 branches across the county. Most people use maybe one of them, and mostly for books, holds and the occasional printer run. The teen programming sits quietly behind all that, and it is far bigger than the average parent realizes.

Teen animeFEST itself has a history here. The District runs a logo design contest ahead of each year's convention, open to all youth in grades six through 12. For the 2026 event, teens could submit a design at any branch between May 15 and July 15, 2026, with entries due at the Youth Services Desk by 8 p.m. on Wednesday, July 15. The winning design gets featured on the District's promotional materials at all 25 branches and on its website and social media channels. That is a real design credit for a teenager, printed countywide, before they ever have a portfolio.

Beyond animeFEST, the District runs Teen Tech Labs, a program called Teens Work, the Nevada Career Explorer, free K-12 homework help and in-branch homework help centers, STEAM and maker learning, and 3D printing. Sahara West lists a 3D printer set aside for teens. The District also offers museum and park passes, study rooms you can reserve, free branch Wi-Fi, and Safe Place, a program for young people who need help.

The digital side is just as easy to miss. The District gives cardholders access to thousands of comics, manga and graphic novels online, plus eBooks, audiobooks, magazines, movies and music. A teenager who burned through a series at animeFEST can keep reading that night without spending a dollar. The District also keeps a Battle of the Books list tied to Clark County School District titles, so the reading kids are assigned at school lines up with what the branch stocks.

Sahara West is built for events. The branch has a multipurpose room with a capacity of 160, a board room, nine study rooms in the reference area, art galleries, a used bookstore and a computer center. Parking lots sit on the north and west sides of the building, and there are 12 accessible spaces near the main entrance with ramps at the pick-up and drop-off areas. That is why a convention can fit there without feeling like it was jammed into a reading room.

What Happens Next

Teen animeFEST 2026 is over. If your teenager missed it, the thing to do now is get on the list for next year rather than wait to hear about it through a friend. The Library District announces the event through its website, its blog and its social channels, and the logo contest usually opens months ahead of the convention itself.

In the meantime, the branch calendar keeps running. The District posts events by audience, and there is a dedicated teens filter at events.thelibrarydistrict.org. You can also filter by branch, so you can pull up just Sahara West, just Summerlin Library, just Centennial Hills, just Whitney or whichever branch is closest to your street. Programs for tweens and teens land on that calendar year round, not only in September.

Sahara West is open Monday through Thursday from 10 a.m. to 8 p.m., and Friday, Saturday and Sunday from 10 a.m. to 6 p.m. The branch phone number is (702) 507-3630, and the District's main line is (702) 734-READ. If you want to ask what is coming up for your kid's age group, the Youth Services Desk is the place to start.

It is also worth walking into the branch in person once. The Sahara West branch manager is Lorinda Soto, and the Youth Services Desk staff are the people who know what is coming before it is posted. Ten minutes of asking questions at a desk will tell you more about what your kid can do this fall than an hour of scrolling.

One more date worth noting for families: the branch is closed all day on Nevada Day, Friday, October 30, 2026, and on Veterans Day, Wednesday, November 11, 2026. Check the District's days of closing page before you drive over on a holiday.

Ryan's Take

I look at neighborhoods for a living, and library branches are one of the most underrated amenities in this valley. Buyers ask me about schools, parks, grocery stores and commute times. Almost nobody asks about the library. Then they move in, discover the branch three minutes away is running free homework help, a 3D printer and a full anime convention, and it changes how they feel about the area.

That is real value attached to a location. A free, walkable or short-drive place where a teenager can go on a Saturday is the kind of thing that keeps families in a neighborhood instead of chasing a new build twenty minutes further out. Sahara West sits in an established part of the west valley with mature trees and homes that have been lived in for a while, and the branch is part of why that pocket holds up.

My honest advice when you are house hunting in Clark County: pull up the Library District's branch map alongside the school zones. Find out which branch you would be assigned to and what it runs. It costs you nothing to check, and it tells you something about the daily life of a neighborhood that a listing photo never will.

What You Can Do

Start with a library card. It is free for Clark County residents, and the District's sign-up page is the fastest route. Your teenager can have their own card, which is what unlocks the study room reservations, the digital manga and comics collections, and the event registrations that fill up.

Next, bookmark the events calendar at events.thelibrarydistrict.org and set it to the teens audience filter. Add your home branch as a second filter. That one small step is the difference between hearing about Teen animeFEST the Monday after and having it on your calendar in July.

If you have an artist in the house, watch for the next Teen animeFEST logo contest. Grades six through 12 are eligible, entry forms come from the Youth Services Desk at any branch, and the winning design runs across 25 library locations. That is a legitimate line on a young artist's resume, and the entry cost is a sheet of paper.

Reserve a study room if your teenager needs a quiet place to work. Sahara West has nine of them in the reference area, and the District takes reservations online. During finals week that room is worth more than a coffee shop table, and it does not come with a four-dollar minimum.

Tell other parents. Honestly, that is the biggest fix here. The District does promote these events, but word of mouth in a school pickup line beats a social post every time. If you know a family with a kid who is into anime, gaming, drawing or music, send them the branch calendar link. It takes ten seconds and it is the reason half these programs fill up.

And if your kid is not an anime kid, the same calendar carries book clubs, STEAM sessions, maker programs, career exploration through the Nevada Career Explorer, and free homework help. The Library District is one of the few places in this county built to serve teenagers without asking them to spend money first.
