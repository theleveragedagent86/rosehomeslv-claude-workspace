Nearly 300 Customers Lose Power After a Car Hits a Pole Near Desert Inn and Valley View

Nearly 300 NV Energy customers in central Las Vegas lost power early Sunday morning, September 13, after a vehicle struck a utility pole near Desert Inn Road and Valley View Boulevard. The crash happened around 5 a.m., and the Las Vegas Metropolitan Police Department confirmed it. One pole took out electricity for a few hundred homes and businesses in one of the most central parts of the valley.

That is the whole story in two sentences, and it is small news by the standards of a busy Sunday. But it is the kind of small news that matters if you live there. If you woke up to a dark house, a silent air conditioner, and a dead garage door opener, you were part of it. And if you live anywhere else in Clark County, this is a useful reminder of how the grid actually reaches your home.

We spend a lot of time in Las Vegas talking about big power stories. Rate cases. Transmission lines that stretch across the desert. Solar projects on federal land. Those stories are real and they matter. But the thing that most often turns your lights off is not a policy fight. It is a single pole on a single corner, a few hundred feet from your driveway.

What Happened Near Desert Inn and Valley View

The outage started around 5 a.m. on Sunday, September 13, 2026. A vehicle struck a utility pole near the intersection of Desert Inn Road and Valley View Boulevard. Las Vegas Metropolitan Police confirmed the crash. The driver was taken to a hospital with minor injuries. NV Energy reported that nearly 300 customers lost power as a result.

We are not going to dig into the crash itself here. The investigation belongs to Metro, and the people involved deserve privacy. What we can talk about is the part that touched a few hundred households: the outage, how a single pole causes one, and what it means for the neighborhood around that corner.

It helps to understand what "nearly 300 customers" actually means. In utility language, a customer is a metered account, not a person. One single-family home is one customer. One small business is one customer. An apartment building might be one customer for common areas and dozens more for individual units, depending on how it is metered. So nearly 300 customers is a real slice of a neighborhood. Call it a few hundred households and storefronts, give or take, spread across a handful of blocks.

The Desert Inn and Valley View area sits right in the middle of the valley. It is west of Interstate 15, south of Sahara Avenue, and close to the Las Vegas Convention Center corridor and the resort district. The housing mix there includes older single-family neighborhoods, condo and townhome communities, and apartment complexes, along with small commercial buildings and service businesses. It is a part of town where a lot of hospitality workers, tradespeople, and long-time valley residents live, and where a lot of homes were built well before the newest master-planned suburbs went up on the edges of the county.

That matters for a power outage. Older parts of Clark County are more likely to carry electricity on overhead lines hung from wooden or steel poles along the street. Newer subdivisions built in the last few decades usually run their neighborhood distribution lines underground. Underground lines are not immune to failure, but they do not get knocked over by a vehicle. An overhead pole standing a few feet from a travel lane is exposed in a way that a buried line is not.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

An outage that lasts a couple of hours on a Sunday morning is an inconvenience for most people. It is more than that for some. If someone in your home uses a CPAP machine, an oxygen concentrator, a power wheelchair, or a medication refrigerator, the power going out is a medical problem, not a scheduling problem. If you work nights and sleep days, a dead air conditioner in September is a real issue. If you run a small business on that stretch of Desert Inn, a few hours without registers, lights, or refrigeration is lost revenue you do not get back.

September in Las Vegas is still hot. Even a morning outage can turn uncomfortable fast once the sun is up and the house starts absorbing heat. A well-insulated home holds cool air for a while, but an older home with single-pane windows and original ductwork gives it up quickly. That is the difference a lot of central-valley homeowners feel that a newer suburban homeowner does not.

There is a traffic side to this too. When a pole comes down or is damaged near a major intersection, crews need room to work. That can mean lane closures or a temporary shutdown while the pole is stabilized or replaced. Desert Inn Road and Valley View Boulevard are both real arterials, not side streets, and they carry commuters, delivery drivers, and airport traffic every day. A Sunday at 5 a.m. is about the best possible timing for that kind of work. The same event at 5 p.m. on a Thursday would have been a much bigger headache.

Signals are worth thinking about as well. When power drops near an intersection, traffic lights can go dark. Nevada law treats a dark signal like a four-way stop. A lot of drivers do not know that, or forget it in the moment. If you ever roll up on a black traffic light in this valley, stop, take your turn, and go slowly. It is the single most useful thing to remember about a local outage.

Renters feel outages differently than owners, and central Las Vegas has a lot of renters. If you own the home, you can add a surge protector at the panel, look into a portable generator, or plan for a battery. If you rent, most of that is not your call. Your realistic options are a good power bank, a cooler, and knowing your landlord's after-hours number. That is not a complaint about landlords. It is just the practical reality of who controls what.

There is also a quiet cost that nobody itemizes. A short outage can cost you a fridge full of groceries if it runs long enough, or a router and a modem if a surge catches them on the way back up. Neither one is a catastrophe. Both are the kind of unplanned hundred-dollar hit that a household on a tight month genuinely feels. Small outages are not free, they are just cheap enough that most people absorb them without saying anything.

Finally, there is the plain neighborly point. Nearly 300 households in central Las Vegas had their Sunday interrupted by something none of them caused and none of them could control. That is how infrastructure works. You do not think about the pole on the corner until the day it decides your morning for you.

Background: How One Pole Turns Off a Few Hundred Homes

Electricity gets to your house in stages. High-voltage transmission lines carry power long distances on big steel towers. Those lines feed substations, which step the voltage down. From a substation, distribution lines fan out through neighborhoods, either overhead on poles or underground in conduit. A transformer near your street steps the voltage down one more time before the service line runs to your meter.

A single distribution pole can carry the circuit that feeds several blocks. It may also hold a transformer, a fuse or cutout, and sometimes communications lines for phone or internet providers. When that pole is damaged, protective equipment upstream senses the fault and opens the circuit. That is a feature, not a failure. The system shuts the power off on purpose so that a downed or damaged line is not energized while people are near it.

This is why a few hundred customers can go dark from one impact. It is not that the crash destroyed 300 homes' worth of equipment. It is that all 300 of those meters sit downstream of the same piece of the circuit, and the circuit opened to keep everyone safe.

Restoration follows a predictable order. Crews secure the scene first and make sure nothing is energized that should not be. Then they assess the damage. If the pole can be repaired, they repair it. If it has to be replaced, they set a new one, transfer the hardware, and re-string the line. Only after the work is verified do they close the circuit and bring customers back. Sometimes a utility can reroute part of a circuit and restore a portion of the customers early while crews finish the rest. Vehicle strikes are one of the routine causes of local distribution outages, alongside wind, heat stress on equipment, animals bridging a transformer, and dig-ins during construction. None of those make headlines on their own. Together they are why the outage map almost always has a few small dots on it somewhere in the valley, even on a calm day with clear skies.

What Happens Next

For the block itself, the immediate next step is already done. Power came back, the pole was dealt with, and the intersection went back to normal. Metro handles the crash investigation on its own timeline, and that is separate from the utility work.

The longer story is about hardening. Across Southern Nevada, utility work continues on transmission capacity, equipment upgrades, and grid reliability. We have written about the regional side of that before, including the 155-mile transmission project and the demand charge refund story. Those projects address big-picture capacity and cost. Vehicle-versus-pole outages are a different and much more local problem, and the fixes for them are ordinary things: protective barriers where poles sit close to travel lanes, pole relocation during road widening, and undergrounding when a corridor gets rebuilt.

Undergrounding sounds like the obvious answer, and in new construction it usually is. Retrofitting an existing street is a different math problem. It is expensive, it requires trenching through developed right-of-way, and the cost eventually lands somewhere in rates or in a road project budget. Most valleys handle it the way Clark County does: new areas go underground by default, and older overhead lines get converted opportunistically when a street is being rebuilt anyway.

What to watch for in central Las Vegas specifically is road work. When an arterial like Desert Inn or Valley View gets a resurfacing, widening, or intersection project, that is the window when utility relocation and undergrounding decisions get made. If you live in that area and you care about overhead lines on your street, that is the meeting to show up to.

One more thing to keep an eye on is how quickly your own street gets attention when something like this happens. Utilities triage by customer count and by risk. A downed line near a school or a hospital moves to the front. A pole in a quiet cul-de-sac with a dozen meters behind it waits. Nearly 300 customers on one circuit is enough to register as a real priority.

Ryan's Take

I sell homes all over Clark County, and I will tell you something buyers almost never ask about: what the power looks like on the street. They ask about the roof, the HVAC, the schools, and the HOA. They do not ask whether the lines are overhead or buried, or how old the local distribution equipment is. It is not a dealbreaker either way. But it is a real difference between an older central-valley neighborhood and a newer one out by the edges, and it shows up on exactly the kind of morning this story describes.

Here is the honest framing. A few hundred customers losing power for part of a Sunday morning is not a mark against a neighborhood. Central Las Vegas has things the outskirts cannot buy: you are minutes from the resort corridor, minutes from the airport, minutes from the freeway, and you are buying into established streets with mature trees and price points that still make sense. That location value is real. The tradeoff is that older infrastructure is more exposed, and you feel it occasionally. Every part of this valley has a tradeoff. Knowing yours is just good homeownership.

What You Can Do

Get set up before the next one. NV Energy publishes an outage map and outage reporting tools at nvenergy.com. Bookmark it on your phone now, while the power is on and you can actually load the page. Make sure your account has a current mobile number attached so restoration alerts reach you.

Build a small outage kit and keep it somewhere you can find in the dark. A flashlight for each person beats candles, which are a fire risk in a house full of people moving around. A charged power bank, a few bottles of water, and a battery or hand-crank radio cover the basics. If anyone in the home depends on powered medical equipment, talk to your provider about backup options and ask NV Energy about medical priority programs for your account.

During an outage, keep the refrigerator and freezer closed. Unplug sensitive electronics so a surge does not hit them when power returns. Leave one lamp switched on so you know the moment service is restored. If you have an electric garage door, find the manual release cord now and learn how it works, because figuring that out in the dark with a car trapped inside is no fun.

And the safety rule that matters most: never approach a downed line, a leaning pole, or anything hanging low over a street or sidewalk. Assume it is live. Keep kids and pets well back, and call 911 and NV Energy. Crews would much rather respond to a report that turns out to be nothing than treat an injury that did not have to happen.
