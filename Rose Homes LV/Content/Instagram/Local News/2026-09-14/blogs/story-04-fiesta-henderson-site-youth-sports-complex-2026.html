Henderson Picks a Developer to Turn the Old Fiesta Site Into a Youth Sports Complex

The city of Henderson has picked a developer for the 35-acre former Fiesta Henderson resort site, and the centerpiece of the plan is a 150,000 square-foot indoor sports fieldhouse. California-based Agora Realty and Management would buy the land for $30 million and build a facility with 10 basketball courts, 20 volleyball courts, and 10,000 square feet of batting cages.

That is not the whole project. The plan also calls for outdoor sports fields, a hotel, 75,000 square feet of retail and dining space, and 30,000 square feet of office space on the same property off Lake Mead Parkway, just west of downtown Henderson.

If you have driven past that empty casino any time in the last six years, you already know why this is news. The building has sat dark since 2020. Now there is an actual plan attached to it, with a named developer, a price, and a use.

What Happened

On September 8, 2026, news broke that Henderson and Agora Realty and Management had announced plans for the former Fiesta Henderson property. The city followed with its own announcement about the partnership. The two sides have agreed on the shape of a deal, and now it has to move through the public process.

Here are the numbers that matter. Agora would pay $30 million for the 35-acre site. The city bought that same property from Station Casinos for $32 million in late 2022. So on paper, the city would sell for $2 million less than it paid four years earlier.

That gap is going to come up at the council meeting, and it should. But a land sale is only one piece of what a city gets out of a project like this. The other pieces are property tax, sales tax, room tax, jobs, and a corner of the city that stops being a vacant building.

The anchor of the project is the fieldhouse. At 150,000 square feet, it is a large indoor building by valley standards. Inside, the plan lists 10 basketball courts and 20 volleyball courts. Those numbers are not an accident. Tournament facilities are built around court count, because court count determines how many games you can run at the same time, which determines how many teams you can host in a weekend.

The 10,000 square feet of batting cages adds baseball and softball to the mix. Batting cages run year round and they run on weeknights, not just tournament weekends. That is a different kind of traffic than a basketball tournament, and it is steadier.

Agora would fund, own, and operate the fieldhouse itself. That detail matters more than most people realize. The city is not on the hook to build it and it is not on the hook to run it. Operating a tournament facility is a specialized business. Booking, officiating, concessions, and scheduling all have to work, or the building sits empty most of the year.

Around the fieldhouse, the plan adds outdoor sports fields, a hotel, 75,000 square feet of retail and dining, and 30,000 square feet of office. Retail, dining, and a hotel next to a tournament venue are not decoration. They are how a sports complex actually makes money for the surrounding area. Families who drive in for a Saturday tournament eat somewhere, and families who fly in sleep somewhere.

The purchase and sale agreement still has to go to a public hearing at a future Henderson City Council meeting. Until that hearing happens and the council votes, this is a plan and not a done deal.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Start with the parents. If you have a kid in club volleyball, club basketball, or travel baseball in Clark County, you already spend a lot of weekends driving. You know which gyms have decent parking and which ones do not. You know what it costs to eat lunch out of a vending machine for two days straight.

A 30-court indoor facility in Henderson changes that math for a lot of families on the east and south side of the valley. Court time is the single hardest thing to find in youth sports. Clubs rent whatever they can get, often late at night, often at schools and rec centers that were not built for tournaments. More courts in one building means more practice slots and more local tournaments that do not require a plane ticket.

Then there is the neighborhood effect. Lake Mead Parkway just west of downtown Henderson is a real neighborhood, not a tourist corridor. The people who live near that intersection have looked at a closed casino for six years. An empty building does not help a block. It does not generate foot traffic for nearby businesses, it does not draw new restaurants, and it does not make anybody feel better about their street.

Retail and dining are the part that residents will actually use week to week. Most Henderson families are not going to visit a volleyball tournament. But 75,000 square feet of retail and dining is a real amount of space, and what fills it will shape how that corner feels. Everyday businesses that neighbors use serve the block better than a strip of places that only make sense on tournament weekends.

Traffic is the honest downside, and nobody should pretend otherwise. Tournament weekends bring cars. A lot of them, in bursts, at predictable hours. If you live within a few minutes of Lake Mead Parkway, your Saturday morning will feel different on tournament days. That is worth watching in the traffic study and worth asking about at the public hearing.

There is a jobs piece too. A hotel, restaurants, retail space, and an operating sports facility all need staff. Those are not all full-time careers, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. But they are local jobs on a site that currently produces none, and a lot of them are the kind of first job that high school and college students in Henderson actually take.

For homeowners, the story here is about what sits at the end of your street. A closed casino is a question mark. An operating sports complex with a hotel and restaurants is an answer. Buyers who are looking at homes near downtown Henderson tend to ask about that site, and the answer just got a lot easier to give.

Renters near the site have a stake here too. Anything that turns a dead 35 acres into an active block tends to raise demand for housing nearby, and demand shows up in rents before it shows up anywhere else. That is not a reason to be against the project. It is a reason to pay attention to it if you are signing a lease in that part of Henderson over the next few years.

Background and History

Fiesta Henderson closed in 2020 and never came back. It sat empty while the valley around it kept growing. That is the part that stung for people who live nearby. Henderson has added homes, schools, and businesses on almost every side of that property, and the property itself stayed dark.

In late 2022, the city of Henderson bought the site from Station Casinos for $32 million. A city buying a 35-acre former casino is not a routine move. Cities usually rezone land and let the market work. Buying it outright means the city wanted control over what came next instead of taking whatever a private buyer proposed.

Four years later, that control is what produced this plan. The city got to choose a partner and a use rather than react to one. Whether the $2 million paper loss on the land was worth that control is a fair question, and it is exactly the kind of question a council hearing exists to handle.

The bigger backdrop is what Henderson has been doing with old industrial and commercial land generally. The valley is running out of easy dirt. Undeveloped land inside the built-up parts of Clark County is scarce and expensive. That pushes developers and cities toward sites that already have roads, power, and water in place, even when those sites come with a building that has to come down first.

It is also worth remembering what the Fiesta was to people who lived around it. Neighborhood casinos in Clark County have never been mainly about gambling. They were where locals ate cheap breakfast, watched a game, held a birthday party, or picked up a shift. When one closes, a block loses a gathering place and a pile of jobs at the same time. Six years is long enough that a generation of Henderson kids has never seen that building open.

That history is part of why a sports complex reads differently here than it would somewhere else. It puts a gathering place back on the same dirt, aimed at a different crowd. Instead of adults on a Friday night, it is families on a Saturday morning. The building changes and the reason people drive there changes, but the corner goes back to being a place people go.

You can see the same pattern elsewhere in Henderson. A large master-planned community recently opened on a cleaned-up former mine site off Lake Mead Parkway, which is a different kind of reuse of difficult land in the same part of the city. Reusing what is already there is becoming the normal way growth happens in Henderson, not the exception.

What Happens Next

The next real step is the public hearing. The purchase and sale agreement between the city and Agora goes to a future Henderson City Council meeting, where residents can speak and the council votes. No date for that hearing was announced with the initial news. [NOT VERIFIED]

If you care about this project either way, that hearing is the moment that counts. Council members hear from the people who show up. A sports complex with a hotel and 30 courts is the kind of project where the details get set early, and the details are where traffic patterns, access points, hours, and design standards live.

After a council approval, a project like this still moves through the normal steps. Design, permitting, and demolition of the existing structure all come before anything gets built. Specific construction and opening timelines for this project were not included in the announcement. [NOT VERIFIED]

One more thing to keep an eye on is the financing structure for the pieces around the fieldhouse. Agora has committed to funding, owning, and operating the fieldhouse itself. The announcement did not spell out the same arrangement for the hotel, the retail, and the office space, and those often get built in later phases by other parties. Phasing is normal, but it is also the reason some big announcements deliver an anchor building and then take years to fill in the rest. [NOT VERIFIED]

What to watch for in the meantime: any changes to the mix of uses, any changes to the fieldhouse court counts, and the terms attached to the hotel and retail portions. Big projects get revised between announcement and groundbreaking. That is normal and not a sign of trouble by itself. It is just a reason to follow the council agenda rather than the press release.

Ryan's Take

I have watched a lot of empty-building stories in this valley, and most of them stay stories. A named developer, a price, and a defined use puts this one in a different category. It is not approved yet, but it is further along than the usual "someone is looking at the site" rumor.

What I find most interesting is the choice of a youth sports anchor instead of housing or another casino. Tournament sports bring people into Henderson from outside the valley on weekends, and they bring local families in on weeknights. That is a two-sided traffic pattern, and it is a lot more predictable than retail alone. The hotel next door tells you the plan is built around out-of-town teams, not just local leagues.

For homeowners near downtown Henderson and along Lake Mead Parkway, I would treat this as a positive with a caveat. A vacant casino sitting on 35 acres is a drag on the feel of a neighborhood, and replacing it with an active use is almost always better. The caveat is traffic, and that is a legitimate thing to raise at the hearing rather than a reason to oppose the whole project. Ask about access points, ask about the traffic study, and ask about what happens on a Saturday in March.

What You Can Do

Watch the Henderson City Council agenda. The purchase and sale agreement has to come back for a public hearing, and agendas are posted ahead of each meeting on the city's website. If this project matters to you as a neighbor, a parent, or a business owner nearby, that is the page to check.

Show up or submit a comment when it lands. Public hearings on land deals are usually short, and they are usually attended by a handful of people. That means individual comments carry real weight. Come with a specific question rather than a general feeling. Access and traffic, hours of operation, and what kinds of retail are allowed are all fair and useful things to ask.

If you have a kid in club sports, talk to your club director about what a 30-court facility would mean for your season. Clubs are the ones who book court time, and they will have a clear read on whether this changes their schedule. That perspective is also worth sharing at the hearing, because council members hear from neighbors far more often than they hear from the families a project is designed to serve.

If you own or run a business along Lake Mead Parkway, start thinking about it now rather than after the doors open. Tournament crowds arrive early, eat between games, and leave in a block. Businesses that plan for that pattern do well next to facilities like this. Businesses that get surprised by it mostly just complain about the parking.

And if you own a home nearby, keep an eye on this. Big redevelopment next door is a real factor in how a neighborhood shows to buyers, both while it is under construction and after it opens.
