Smoke and Fire Barbecue Is Closing After 6 Years on East Russell Road

Smoke and Fire, the neighborhood barbecue spot at 3315 E. Russell Road in Las Vegas, is closing. The owners announced on Instagram that the last day of service is Saturday, September 26, 2026, which closes the book on six years in the same building.

If you live in southeast Las Vegas, you know the stretch. Russell Road near Pecos Road is not a tourist corridor. It is a working part of the valley full of houses, apartments, warehouses, and people driving home from a shift. Smoke and Fire sat right in the middle of that, and it built a following the slow way, one plate at a time.

The announcement was short and honest. The owners wrote that "with everything going on right now, continuing just isn't something we're able to do." That is all they said about the reason, and no further explanation has been given publicly. There is no word yet on whether another operator will take over the space.

So the practical news is simple. You have until September 26 to go back one more time. After that, the smokers go quiet.

What Happened

On September 10, 2026, the Las Vegas Review-Journal reported that Smoke and Fire would serve its last customers on September 26. The restaurant had shared the news on its own Instagram account first, which is how a lot of small Las Vegas restaurants tell their regulars anything these days.

The address is 3315 E. Russell Road, Las Vegas. That puts it in the southeast part of the valley, near Pecos Road. It is an easy drive from Green Valley, from the neighborhoods around Sunset Park, and from the apartment communities that fill in the blocks between Eastern Avenue and Boulder Highway.

The restaurant opened in the summer of 2020. Think about what that means for a second. It opened in the middle of the year that shut down dining rooms across Clark County, when almost nobody was signing a restaurant lease. It opened anyway, and it lasted six years.

The menu was straightforward barbecue with a few signature items people came back for. Smoked wings. Blueberry chicken sliders, which is not something you find on every barbecue menu in town. A smoked brisket sandwich. Full racks and half racks of ribs. That was the core of it.

The owners did not cite a specific cause. The line they posted, "with everything going on right now, continuing just isn't something we're able to do," is the entire public explanation. It could mean costs. It could mean the lease. It could mean something personal. We are not going to guess, and neither should anyone else.

Operating hours for the final days of service were not listed in the announcement. Brisket and ribs are the first things to go in that situation.

It is also worth noting how the news traveled. The restaurant posted it. Regulars saw it, shared it, and started tagging friends. A local news outlet picked it up the same week. That is the whole life cycle of a neighborhood business announcement in 2026, and it is why a lot of people in southeast Las Vegas heard about this before anyone who lives across the valley did.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

This is not a Strip closing. No celebrity chef, no buyout, no press conference. This is the kind of closing that actually changes a neighborhood's routine.

A restaurant that lasts six years in one spot becomes part of how people organize their week. Somebody picked up wings there every Sunday. Somebody's kid's birthday dinner happened in that dining room. Somebody stopped for a brisket sandwich on the way home from a swing shift because the drive-through options on Russell got old. Six years is long enough for all of that to become habit, and habits are what make a neighborhood feel like home instead of just an address.

There is also a practical side for residents of southeast Las Vegas. When a sit-down local restaurant leaves a commercial strip, the nearest alternative usually sits a few miles farther out. That is a small thing on paper. In real life it means one more left turn, one more mile, one more reason to just order delivery instead. Multiply that across a few closings and a neighborhood starts to feel less served than it did.

For homeowners, the connection is indirect but real. Buyers ask about food. They ask it constantly. When someone tours a house near Russell and Pecos, one of the first questions after schools and commute is some version of "what is around here to eat?" A well-liked local barbecue joint is an easy answer. Losing one does not move a home value by itself, but the overall health of nearby commercial strips absolutely factors into how a neighborhood shows.

There is one more layer here that locals feel and visitors never do. Las Vegas gets written about as a restaurant town constantly, and almost all of that coverage points at the Strip. Meanwhile the places that feed the people who actually run this city sit on roads like Russell, Rainbow, Nellis, and Craig. Those restaurants rarely get a review. They get regulars instead. When one closes, the only people who notice are the ones who live nearby, which is exactly why it is worth writing about.

And for renters and young families in that part of the valley, the story lands a different way. Southeast Las Vegas has a lot of people who work in hospitality, warehousing, construction, and health care. Affordable, filling, locally owned food is not a lifestyle choice there. It is Tuesday dinner. Every spot that closes takes an option off that list.

Background and History

Smoke and Fire opened in the summer of 2020. Almost every restaurant that opened in Clark County that year started with the deck stacked against it. Dining rooms were limited or closed, tourism had collapsed, and the usual playbook for building a customer base did not apply. Restaurants that survived that period did it on takeout, on word of mouth, and on whoever lived within a few miles.

That is exactly the kind of customer base Smoke and Fire ended up with. Barbecue travels well, which helped. It holds up in a to-go box better than most food does. A smoker running in southeast Las Vegas found an audience that could smell it on the way past.

Six years later, the environment is different but not easy. Restaurant operators across the valley have talked publicly for the last several years about rising costs on almost every line of the business, from food to labor to rent. Independent operators absorb those increases without the buying power a chain has. Barbecue in particular runs on cuts of meat that have not gotten cheaper, and on hours of cook time that have to be paid for whether the dining room fills or not.

None of that is a stated reason for this closing. The owners did not name one. But it is the backdrop every independent restaurant in Clark County is operating against right now, and it is worth understanding when you see a six-year-old neighborhood spot call it.

The location tells part of the story too. Russell Road is a long east to west artery that runs from the far southwest across the valley to Boulder Highway, passing south of the airport along the way. The eastern stretch near Pecos is mixed use in the plainest sense: single family homes, older apartment complexes, light industrial buildings, and small commercial pads. A barbecue restaurant fits that mix naturally. It does not need a marquee or a valet. It needs a parking lot and people who are hungry on the way home.

Southeast Las Vegas has been quietly changing for years. It is one of the more affordable inner parts of the valley, it sits close to the airport and the resort corridor where a lot of residents work, and it has older housing stock that buyers can still get into without stretching. Commercial strips in that part of town turn over more often than the newer master planned areas out west, and that turnover cuts both ways. Spaces open up for new operators, but long tenures are harder to build.

It is also worth saying that six years is a real run. The restaurant industry is brutal on new openings, and a place that opens in a pandemic summer and still has regulars in 2026 did something right. That is not a failure story. That is a story about a small business that worked for a long time and then reached the end of what its owners could carry.

What Happens Next

The immediate timeline is short. Service continues through Saturday, September 26, 2026. That is the last day. After that, the restaurant at 3315 E. Russell Road stops operating under the Smoke and Fire name.

What comes next for the building is unknown. No replacement tenant has been announced, and the owners have not said publicly whether they plan to reopen somewhere else, sell the concept, or step away entirely. [NOT VERIFIED] Anything you read speculating about a new location or a relaunch should be treated as rumor until the owners say it themselves.

In the valley, a vacated restaurant space usually goes one of three directions. Another independent operator takes it because the kitchen buildout is already there and that saves enormous money. A small regional chain takes it for the same reason. Or it sits for a while. Which one happens depends on the lease terms and the landlord, and none of that is public here.

There is one more thing to watch. Gift cards, punch cards, and prepaid catering orders become a problem the moment a restaurant closes. If you are holding any of those from Smoke and Fire, use them before September 26. Do not wait, and do not assume there will be a grace period afterward. That advice applies to any closing restaurant, anywhere in Clark County.

Expect the final two weeks to be busy. Closing announcements in Las Vegas reliably bring out people who have not visited in a year and suddenly remember they love the place. That is the bittersweet part of these stories. The crowd shows up at the end. If you want a quiet last meal there, go on a weekday and go early.

Ryan's Take

I pay attention to these closings because they tell me something about a neighborhood that a spreadsheet does not. Commercial corridors like Russell near Pecos are the connective tissue of southeast Las Vegas. They are where people actually spend their time between home and work. When a long-running local business closes there, I want to know whether it is one operator's situation or a sign the whole strip is struggling.

In this case, the owners pointed to their own circumstances, not to the corridor. So I read this as one small business reaching the end of its run, not as a warning about the area. Southeast Las Vegas remains one of the more practical parts of the valley for buyers who want space, an easy drive to the airport and the Strip corridor, and a price point below what you pay in Summerlin or the southwest.

I also watch what replaces things. If a new operator opens in that building within a few months, that tells me demand on the corridor is fine and the space is worth having. If it sits empty into next year, that is a different signal, and it is one I would factor into how I talk about the area with a buyer. Either way, the building itself is a decent asset. A turnkey kitchen with six years of foot traffic behind it is not nothing.

Here is the part I would tell any homeowner in that area. Local businesses stay open because neighbors show up on ordinary Tuesdays, not because they show up in the last two weeks. If there is a place near your house you would miss, go now, while going still matters. That is the whole lesson in this one.

What You Can Do

First, go. Smoke and Fire is open through September 26 at 3315 E. Russell Road. Order the things the restaurant is known for: the smoked wings, the brisket sandwich, the ribs, and the blueberry chicken sliders if they are still on the board. Check the restaurant's Instagram before you drive out, because hours in the final stretch were not announced and popular items may sell out.

Second, tip well and say something to the staff. A closing means a crew of people are out of work on September 27. Many of them live in the same neighborhoods they served. A good tip and a genuine thank you cost you nothing extra and land more than you think.

Third, look around your own corner of Clark County. Every neighborhood in the valley has an independent restaurant that has been there long enough that you assume it always will be. Aliante, Centennial Hills, Green Valley, Mountains Edge, Spring Valley, and the older parts of North Las Vegas all have them. Pick one this month and go on a normal night. That is what keeps them open.

Fourth, bring somebody with you. Closings like this one turn into small reunions, and the drive out to Russell and Pecos is a lot better with a neighbor in the passenger seat than alone. Take pictures if the place meant something to you. Six years from now those photos will be the only record that a barbecue restaurant used to sit on that corner, because the sign will be gone and the building will look like whatever comes next.

Fifth, if you want to keep track of local openings and closings, follow the food coverage from the Las Vegas Review-Journal and other local outlets. These announcements almost always break on a restaurant's own social account first and get picked up a day or two later.
