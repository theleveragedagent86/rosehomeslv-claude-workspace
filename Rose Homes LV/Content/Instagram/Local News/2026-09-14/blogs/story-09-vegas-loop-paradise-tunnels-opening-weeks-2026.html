The Vegas Loop's Paradise Road Tunnels Are Weeks From Opening

The Vegas Loop is about to grow under Paradise Road. Steve Hill, president and CEO of the Las Vegas Convention and Visitors Authority, told the Las Vegas Review-Journal that a just over 2-mile dual-tunnel section under Paradise Road is set to open in stages over the coming weeks.

That section runs between Westgate Las Vegas and a planned station at 4744 Paradise Road. It is the first stretch of the underground system that points toward the airport side of the valley in a way locals might actually notice.

Hill was direct about the timing. "We really think the first tunnel on Paradise is within weeks of opening," he said. "Hopefully by the end of this month. Almost certainly in October." That is what an official said, not a guarantee. Big infrastructure dates move, and this one could too.

What Happened

Steve Hill leads the Las Vegas Convention and Visitors Authority, the agency that has been the public partner on the Boring Company's tunnel system in Las Vegas. He gave the update to the Review-Journal for a story published September 14, 2026.

The headline number is the length. The Paradise Road piece is just over 2 miles of dual tunnels. Dual means two bores, one for each direction of travel, rather than a single tube where cars take turns. That matters for how many vehicles the segment can move once both sides are live.

The two end points are named. On the north end is Westgate Las Vegas. On the south end is a planned station at 4744 Paradise Road. Between those two points, the tunnels run under the Paradise Road corridor, one of the busiest north to south surface streets on the east side of the resort district.

Hill described the opening as happening in stages. That phrasing is worth reading carefully. It suggests one tunnel goes live before the other, and it suggests service may start with a shorter run before the full segment is in use. Hill said he thinks the first tunnel on Paradise is within weeks of opening, and he pointed at the end of September as a hope and October as the more likely window.

Staged openings are normal for tunnel work. Boring a tunnel and finishing a tunnel are two different jobs. Once the digging is done, crews still have to handle lighting, ventilation, power, safety systems, striping and the connections at each end. Any one of those can move a date by weeks.

That is also why the wording from Hill matters more than the calendar. He said the first tunnel is within weeks of opening and put a hope on the end of the month. He did not announce a date. Officials who work on infrastructure tend to speak that way on purpose, because the last five percent of a project is where the surprises live.

One more detail from the reporting is already in place today. Airport service runs to and from most active system stations for $14 per person. That is the current fare structure for that trip, and it is the closest thing the Loop has right now to a service a resident would use on a regular day.

Everything else about the Paradise segment, including the exact day the first tunnel carries passengers, has not been announced. Treat any specific date you see floating around as unconfirmed until the LVCVA or the Boring Company says it out loud. [NOT VERIFIED]

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Most of the Vegas Loop conversation so far has been about visitors. Convention attendees getting from one hall to another. Tourists hopping between resorts. Fun to watch, easy to ignore if you live in Spring Valley or Henderson and never set foot in a convention center.

The Paradise Road segment is the first piece that changes that math a little. Paradise Road is a street locals actually drive. It runs the length of the resort corridor on the east side, past the convention center, past UNLV, and down toward Harry Reid International. If you live in the valley, you have sat in traffic on it.

The bigger deal is the airport angle. Airport service already runs to and from most active system stations for $14 per person. A resident flying out of Harry Reid has three normal options today: drive and pay to park, pay for a rideshare, or ask somebody to drop you off. Every one of those has a real cost, in dollars or in favors.

A $14 flat fare per person is not nothing, but for a solo traveler on a four-day trip it can compete with airport parking. For a family of four it gets expensive fast. So this is not a universal win. It is a new option, and options are how a transportation network gets useful.

There is also a simple congestion argument. Every vehicle moving under Paradise Road is a vehicle not sitting at a light on top of it. Two miles of dual tunnel will not fix Las Vegas traffic. But the resort corridor is where the valley's worst surface congestion lives, and anything that pulls trips off Paradise during a big convention week helps the people trying to get to work through that same area.

Think about who actually works in that corridor. Hospitality workers, convention staff, security, food service, maintenance crews, rideshare drivers, students at UNLV picking up shifts nearby. Tens of thousands of Clark County residents commute into the resort district every single day, and a lot of them do it at hours when parking is tight and traffic is unpredictable. Any improvement to how people move in and out of that zone lands on residents before it lands on tourists.

There is a second group worth naming. People who live east of the Strip and fly for work. Sales reps, tradespeople who travel for jobs, folks with family out of state. For someone taking ten or twelve flights a year, a reliable and cheap airport connection is not a convenience. It is a real line item in a household budget.

For renters and homeowners on the east side of the valley, the practical question is whether this eventually becomes something you can use without a car. Right now the honest answer is not yet. The system is still built around the resort corridor. But this segment moves it closer to the airport, and the airport is the one destination almost every resident needs to reach a few times a year.

Background and History

The Boring Company built the original tunnel system under the Las Vegas Convention Center. That first piece was a short loop designed to move convention attendees between exhibit halls faster than walking. It worked well enough that the conversation shifted almost immediately to expansion.

What followed was a steady series of approvals to extend the tunnels outward under the resort corridor, connecting hotels and other destinations to the convention center core. The Vegas Loop name describes that larger network rather than the original convention center segment alone.

The system is unusual compared to traditional transit. It is not a train. Passengers ride in vehicles through the bores, station to station, on a point to point basis rather than following a fixed schedule with intermediate stops. That design is part of why it has been debated so loudly. Supporters point to the speed and the low construction cost compared to rail. Critics point to capacity limits.

Las Vegas has argued about rail and other fixed transit for the resort corridor for decades without building much of it. The Monorail exists and serves a slice of the east side. Beyond that, the valley has leaned on buses, taxis, rideshare and personal cars. The Loop moved faster than the rail conversations largely because private money and private construction carried it.

It also helps to understand why Paradise Road specifically. The Strip itself is dense with utilities, foundations and existing underground work, and the properties along it are enormous. Paradise runs parallel to the Strip one corridor east, which makes it a natural spine for reaching the convention center, the properties on that side, and eventually the airport. If you were drawing a line on a map, that is roughly where you would draw it.

Westgate Las Vegas sits right next to the convention center campus, which is why it works as one end of this segment. The 4744 Paradise Road address sits farther south along the same corridor. Two miles between them sounds small until you remember how long that same drive takes on the surface during a convention week with every light against you.

That history is why the Paradise Road segment gets attention out of proportion to its length. Two miles is short. But it is two miles in a direction that finally points toward something residents care about, and it opens in a year when the valley is also getting driverless ride hailing and a steady run of road projects. The transportation picture in Clark County is changing faster than it has in a long time.

What Happens Next

The nearest milestone is the first tunnel on Paradise going live. Hill said he hopes that lands by the end of September and called October almost certain. Watch for an announcement from the LVCVA and the Boring Company rather than a rumor, and expect the second bore to follow on its own timeline since the opening is happening in stages.

The station at 4744 Paradise Road is described as planned. A tunnel can be finished before the station serving it is fully open to passengers, so the address showing up on a map is not the same as the door being unlocked. If you want to know when you can actually ride to that stop, the station opening is the date to track, not the tunnel completion.

After that, the question is what connects next. The stated ambition has always been a network that links the resort corridor, the convention center and the airport. Each new segment makes the next one more valuable, because a transit system is only as useful as the number of places it can take you. Two miles that connect two points is a demonstration. Ten miles that connect fifteen points is a service.

Capacity is the other thing to watch once the segment is live. A dual tunnel moves more than a single bore, but the real number is how many vehicles run through it per hour during a peak convention day. That figure has not been published for this segment, so anyone quoting one is guessing. [NOT VERIFIED] The first big trade show after the opening will tell the story better than any projection.

Local residents should also watch the fare. Airport service currently runs $14 per person to and from most active stations. Whether that price holds, rises, or picks up a resident or multi rider discount as the system grows will decide whether locals treat the Loop as real transportation or as a novelty they try once.

Ryan's Take

I pay attention to transportation news because it shows up in home values eventually, just slower than people expect. A buyer almost never says "I am buying here because of a tunnel." They say the commute feels manageable, or getting to the airport is easy, or they can get to the Strip for a shift without fighting traffic. That is the same thing said in human language.

The Paradise Road corridor is an interesting spot for that reason. It sits between the resort district and the neighborhoods east of it, close to UNLV, close to the airport. It is already a place where people trade a little extra noise and traffic for being close to everything. If the Loop eventually makes an airport trip from that corridor simple and cheap, it strengthens an argument that is already there.

The other thing I watch is how a project like this changes the story a seller can tell. Right now, a listing near Paradise leans on distance to the Strip, distance to the airport and distance to UNLV. Those are all still true. If the Loop matures into something residents use, the pitch gets one more line, and lines like that matter in a market where buyers have plenty of choices and are taking their time.

I would not tell a client to buy a home today based on a two mile tunnel. That is speculation, and speculation is how people overpay. What I would say is this: if you are already looking at the east side of the valley, this is one more thing pointing in the right direction, and it belongs in the pile with the schools, the commute and the price. Infrastructure that actually gets built is worth more than infrastructure that gets announced, and this one is close enough to opening that it counts.

What You Can Do

If you want the real opening date, go to the source. The Las Vegas Convention and Visitors Authority is the public agency on this project, and announcements about service starting will come through official channels before they show up in your feed. Local news outlets covering transportation will carry it the same day.

If you have a flight coming up and you are curious, look at the current airport service before the Paradise segment opens. It runs to and from most active system stations for $14 per person right now. Trying it once on a trip you were taking anyway is the cheapest way to find out whether it fits how you travel.

Talk to your neighbors about it too, especially if you live near the corridor. Station access, surface traffic and parking all shift when a new stop opens, and the people who notice first are the ones living a few blocks away. Neighborhood groups and HOA meetings are where those details get raised, and local input is easier to give early than late.

And if you drive Paradise Road regularly, expect the usual construction pattern around station work and surface access points.
