Las Vegas' Naked City Gets Its First Ground-Up Housing in More Than 65 Years

A Las Vegas neighborhood that has not seen a single new home built from the ground up in more than 65 years is finally getting new housing. The neighborhood is Naked City, the small grid of streets tucked just north of the Strip behind the Strat, and it sits inside the larger Gateway District. New residential construction is underway there for the first time in over six decades.

That news arrived alongside another number worth paying attention to. Calls for service across the Gateway District have dropped from 6,352 in 2022 to a projected 4,000 this year. New businesses have opened. Older apartment properties have been renovated. For a neighborhood that has spent generations getting passed over by builders, this is a real shift.

If you own property anywhere near the Strat, or you have been watching the close-in Las Vegas neighborhoods for a place to buy, this story matters to you. Here is what is happening, why 65 years is such a big number, and what it usually means when construction crews show up in a place that has not had them in a very long time.

What Happened

On September 8, 2026, the Las Vegas Review-Journal published a report by Patrick Blennerhassett on the changes taking place in Naked City. The headline finding was simple. The neighborhood is getting a from-the-ground-up housing project, and it is the first new residential construction there in more than 65 years.

The specific project names have not been verified for this article, so we are not going to name them here. [NOT VERIFIED] What is confirmed is the category of activity. New businesses have moved in. Existing apartment properties have been renovated rather than left to decay. And a housing project is being built from scratch on ground that has not held new construction since the Eisenhower years.

The report also carried public safety numbers from the Las Vegas Metropolitan Police Department. Metro Sgt. Miles Peshlakai told the Review-Journal that crime in the area was down month over month. He also said the neighborhood had gone more than a month without a single violent crime as of late August 2026. That is a specific, measurable stretch, and it is the kind of detail that people who live there notice long before anyone writes about it.

The broader district numbers tell the same story over a longer window. Calls for service across the Gateway District, which is the larger area that contains Naked City, have fallen from 6,352 in 2022 to a projected 4,000 for this year. That is a drop of roughly a third in four years. Calls for service are a useful measure because they capture more than arrests. They count the moments when someone felt the need to pick up the phone.

Part of that shift traces back to a 2022 decision by LVMPD. That year the department expanded its Safe Village Initiative into the Gateway District. The initiative had been operating elsewhere in the valley, and 2022 is when the district was folded in. The construction activity and the falling call volume are happening on the same timeline, which is worth keeping in mind as you read the rest of this.

One more note on sourcing. The Review-Journal article is partially behind a paywall. The crime figures, the calls-for-service numbers and the 65-year construction gap all come from the published portion of the piece. Everything in this article is limited to what could be read and confirmed.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Start with the people who already live in Naked City. For them, this is not an abstract real estate story. It is the difference between a landlord who reinvests and a landlord who lets a property run down. Renovated apartment buildings mean working air conditioning in July, which in this city is not a small thing. New businesses mean somewhere to buy groceries or coffee without getting in a car.

For homeowners in the surrounding blocks, new construction changes the math on their own property. Appraisers look for comparable sales nearby. When a neighborhood has gone 65 years without new housing, there is very little recent, high-quality inventory to compare against. Everything gets measured against older stock. New construction gives appraisers something fresher to work with, and that tends to pull the whole block upward over time.

Location is the quiet advantage here. Naked City sits north of the Strip, behind the Strat, and it is genuinely close-in. A lot of the housing being built in Clark County right now is out on the edges, in the far southwest, the far northwest, or out past Henderson. Those are good communities. They also come with a commute. A neighborhood this close to the resort corridor and to downtown offers something the perimeter cannot: a short drive to where a huge share of Las Vegas jobs actually are.

That matters for the service workers, hospitality staff, nurses and trades people who keep the corridor running. Housing near work is one of the few real fixes for a household budget. Every mile you do not have to drive twice a day is money and time back in your pocket. Any new housing that goes up inside the loop is a small piece of that puzzle.

Renters across Clark County have a stake in this too, even renters who will never set foot in Naked City. Southern Nevada does not have enough housing near its job centers. Every unit that gets built close in takes a little pressure off the rest of the market. It is a small amount on its own. It adds up when other close-in neighborhoods follow the same path.

And for anyone who has been priced out of the newer communities on the valley's edge, close-in neighborhoods with older housing stock have historically been where first-time buyers found a way in. Those homes are usually smaller and older, and they usually cost less. When a neighborhood like this starts to see real investment, the window for buying in at the lower end does not stay open forever.

There is also a fairness point worth making plainly. Neighborhoods do not go 65 years without investment by accident. Capital chose to go elsewhere, over and over, for a long time. The families who stayed and kept the neighborhood going through those decades are the reason there is still a neighborhood there to invest in. The new construction is news, but the people were there the whole time.

Background and History

Sixty-five years is the number to sit with. If the last ground-up housing in Naked City went up around 1960 or earlier, that predates the modern Las Vegas Strip as most people know it. It predates the freeway system that carries the valley today. It predates the master-planned communities that now define how Clark County grows.

In that same stretch, Las Vegas went from a modest desert city to one of the fastest-growing metro areas in the country. Summerlin was planned and built. Henderson grew into Nevada's second-largest city. Whole communities like Mountains Edge, Inspirada, Skye Canyon and Cadence appeared on land that had been empty desert. Builders poured concrete in every direction. They just did not pour it here.

That is the pattern worth understanding. Las Vegas grew outward, not inward. Growth chased cheap raw land at the edges because raw land is simpler to build on. Close-in neighborhoods with older housing, smaller parcels and split ownership are harder. Assembling enough lots to build something meaningful takes years and patience. So developers went where the work was easier, and the older neighborhoods near the core kept aging in place.

The 2022 expansion of the Safe Village Initiative into the Gateway District is one marker on the way back. It is not the whole explanation, and nobody should claim that a single program turned a neighborhood around. But it lines up with the four-year decline in calls for service, from 6,352 in 2022 to a projected 4,000 now. Something changed in that window, and that is the documented change we can point to.

It also helps to remember what the Gateway District is. It is the stretch of Las Vegas that connects the north end of the Strip to the edge of downtown. For decades it functioned mostly as the space between two destinations, which is a hard role for any neighborhood to carry. Land in a place people drive through tends to get less care than land in a place people drive to. That is starting to change now that both ends of the corridor have seen their own investment.

The business openings and apartment renovations came along in the same period. That sequence is familiar to anyone who has watched a neighborhood turn. Small businesses usually move first because they can commit at a smaller scale. Property owners renovate next. Ground-up construction, which is the biggest bet of all, comes last. When a builder commits to new construction, it means somebody ran the numbers and decided the neighborhood's next 30 years look better than its last 30.

What Happens Next

The near-term thing to watch is the construction timeline itself. No completion date has been verified for the ground-up project, so we are not going to guess at one. [NOT VERIFIED] What you can watch for yourself is simpler and more reliable: permits pulled, fences going up, foundations poured. Those are public and visible, and they tell you more than any announcement does.

The second thing to watch is whether the first project draws a second one. This is how these things usually go. One builder takes the risk and proves the numbers work. Other builders watch closely. If the first project leases or sells well, the calls to land owners start. If it struggles, the neighborhood can sit quiet for another long stretch. The first project is the test case for everything that follows.

The third thing is whether the public safety trend holds. A month without a violent crime in late August is a genuinely good stretch, and the four-year decline in calls for service is a real trend. Both still need to keep going. Trends get confirmed by the next year of data, not by one good month. Watch the 2027 numbers when they come out.

Watch the small businesses as well. Retail and restaurants are an honest early indicator because they live or die on foot traffic and local spending. If the businesses that opened recently are still open in two years, and if a few more join them, that tells you the neighborhood economy is real and not just a short burst of attention. If storefronts go dark again, that is a signal too.

The last thing to watch is what happens to rents. When investment arrives in a neighborhood that has not had it, housing costs usually move. That can be good news for owners and hard news for long-time renters at the same time. Both of those things are true at once, and both deserve attention. If you rent in the area, read your lease renewal carefully over the next couple of years and know what notice your landlord owes you.

Ryan's Take

I pay a lot of attention to where the shovels go, because shovels are a commitment. Anyone can write a press release about a neighborhood's potential. Very few people will spend millions of dollars to pour a foundation in a place that has not seen new housing since before the freeway. That decision comes after somebody studied the area hard and decided the risk was worth it.

Here is the part I want Las Vegas owners to hear. Close-in land is finite. The valley is boxed in by federal land and mountains, and the easy perimeter acreage is not infinite either. Over the next 20 years, the neighborhoods that sit inside the loop and near the resort corridor get more valuable simply because they cannot be replicated. Naked City is inside that ring. A lot of the valley's newest housing is not.

I also want to be careful with how this kind of story gets told. This is a neighborhood with a long history and people who have lived there for decades. They are not a problem that got solved. They are residents who waited a very long time for the same investment other parts of this valley got again and again. The honest version of this story is that the capital finally showed up. That is worth being glad about, and it is worth watching closely to make sure the people already there get to benefit from it too.

What You Can Do

If you own property in or near the Gateway District, get a fresh valuation. If your last appraisal or market check was done a few years ago, it was done before this activity started. New construction nearby can change what your property is worth, and you cannot make a good decision on a stale number.

If you are shopping for a home and you want to be close to work, put the close-in neighborhoods on your list instead of only looking at the new-build communities on the edges. Drive the streets at different times of day. Check the commute from the actual address to the actual job. Look at what is being renovated and what is being built. You will learn more in one afternoon of driving than in a week of scrolling listings.

If you want to follow the official record rather than the rumors, the City of Las Vegas and Clark County both post planning agendas, permit records and meeting materials online. Development items go through public hearings, and those hearings accept public comment. If a project near you is up for review, you are allowed to show up and speak. That is how neighborhood decisions actually get made in this valley, and the rooms are usually emptier than they should be.

And if you rent in the area, keep records. Save your lease, save your renewal notices, and know your rights under Nevada law about notice periods and rent changes. Being informed early is far easier than reacting late.
