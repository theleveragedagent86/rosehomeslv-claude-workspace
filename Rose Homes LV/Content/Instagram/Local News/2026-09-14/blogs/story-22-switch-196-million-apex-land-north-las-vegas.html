Switch Pays $196 Million for Another 176 Acres at Apex in North Las Vegas

Las Vegas data center company Switch paid $196 million for 176.5 acres inside the Apex Industrial Park in North Las Vegas. The deal closed on August 31, 2026, and the land came from two separate ownership groups.

That works out to roughly $1.1 million an acre for raw desert land at the far north edge of the valley. Ten years ago, that kind of number would have stopped traffic. Today it is becoming normal for land near power.

If you live in North Las Vegas, Aliante, Eldorado, Sunrise Manor, or anywhere along the Interstate 15 north corridor, this is your backyard. Apex is the big industrial zone at the top of the valley, and it keeps getting bigger. Every acre that fills in up there changes the job map, the traffic map, and the conversation about power and water in Southern Nevada.

What Happened

The Las Vegas Review-Journal reported on September 11, 2026 that Switch bought 176.5 acres at Apex Industrial Park in North Las Vegas for $196 million. The story was updated on September 14. The sale closed August 31, 2026.

The land did not come from one seller. Two separate ownership groups sold pieces of the total. That detail matters more than it sounds. It tells you Switch is assembling a footprint rather than buying one ready made parcel. Companies assemble land when they plan to build something large and want control of the ground around it.

This is not the first time Switch has done this. The company has been buying large tracts across Southern Nevada and borrowing billions of dollars over the past few years as it builds out data center capacity. The Apex purchase is another step in a pattern that has been running for a while now.

Switch is a Las Vegas company. That is worth saying out loud, because a lot of the data center growth in other states comes from outside operators. Switch already runs campuses in Southern Nevada and has grown here for years. When Switch buys land at Apex, it is a local company expanding on home ground, not a stranger parachuting in.

What Switch plans to build on these specific 176.5 acres, and on what timeline, has not been detailed in the reporting. That is normal at this stage. Land closes first. Site plans, permits, and construction schedules come later, and they go through North Las Vegas and Clark County review before anything rises out of the dirt. Any specific building plan for this parcel is [NOT VERIFIED] until the company or the city puts it on paper.

For now, the verified facts are simple. A $196 million check. 176.5 acres. Two sellers. One buyer. August 31. Apex Industrial Park, North Las Vegas.

It helps to picture the scale. 176.5 acres is bigger than most master planned neighborhood phases in this valley. If you have driven past a new subdivision going up with a few hundred homes in it, you have looked at a piece of land in that range. Switch bought that much ground in one move, and it is only part of what the company has assembled across Southern Nevada.

The price tells its own story. At roughly $1.1 million an acre, this is not bargain desert. Land near real transmission capacity has become one of the most competitive things to buy in the western United States, and Southern Nevada is now part of that market. Industrial ground that used to trade quietly is now being chased by buyers with very deep pockets.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Start with jobs, because that is what most families ask about first. Apex is a job zone. Industrial parks bring construction crews during the build, then permanent staff once the buildings open. Data centers also pull in electricians, HVAC technicians, security staff, network engineers, and the trades that keep big mechanical systems running. I am not going to hand you a headcount for this project, because no verified job number has been published for these 176.5 acres. Anyone quoting you one right now is guessing.

What I can say is that a $196 million land buy is not a small commitment. Companies do not spend that on dirt they plan to sit on. The money signals build intent, and build intent eventually means payroll somewhere in North Las Vegas.

Second, housing. North Las Vegas has spent the last decade turning from an overlooked corner of the valley into one of its fastest changing cities. Employment centers pull housing toward them. If Apex keeps filling with large employers, the neighborhoods that sit closest to it become more valuable to buyers who want a short drive to work. That includes Aliante, the Craig Ranch area, Eldorado, and the newer subdivisions off the 215 and I-15 north.

This does not mean prices jump next month. Housing follows jobs slowly. It means the long term story for the north valley gets stronger every time a serious employer commits real money up there.

Third, power and water. This is the part everyone in Clark County is arguing about, and it is a fair argument. Data centers use a lot of electricity and they need cooling. Nevada is a desert. Those two facts sit next to each other in every conversation about this industry, and residents are right to ask how the math works. I am not going to invent power or water figures for this project, because none have been verified for this parcel. But the debate is real, and it is already showing up in policy proposals across the valley.

Fourth, the tax side. Nevada offers data centers a reduced 2 percent sales and use tax rate, plus abatements on personal property tax. Nevada Current reported that the state abated $240 million in sales tax for data centers in 2023 and 2024. Whether those abatements are the right trade for the jobs and investment they attract is one of the biggest live questions in Nevada right now.

Background and History

Apex Industrial Park sits at the north end of the Las Vegas Valley, along Interstate 15 past the edge of the built out city. For years it was mostly empty. It had size and it had freeway access, but it lacked the infrastructure to attract big users. That has changed. Apex has become the valley's landing zone for large industrial and logistics projects, and more recently for data centers.

The reason is simple. Big facilities need three things: large flat parcels, transmission capacity, and a highway. Apex has all three, and very little residential development directly next door. When you are siting a building that runs around the clock, being away from neighborhoods is a feature.

Switch's growth has tracked the wider boom in computing demand. The company has been buying large tracts across Southern Nevada and borrowing billions over the past few years as it builds out capacity. That is a long term bet, and it is being made with borrowed money, which tells you the company expects demand to keep climbing.

At the same time, the politics of land use around data centers have gotten more active across Clark County. In Boulder City, voters will decide Question 1 in November, which asks whether data center facilities should become an approved land use inside the Eldorado Valley Transfer Area. The city charter blocks development on that 100,000 acre tract without a public vote, and more than 86,000 acres of it are protected by a conservation easement. If Question 1 passes, the City Council could lease up to 14,000 additional acres, with each project still going through normal land use review. Supporters point out the city could charge roughly 10 times more rent per acre for data center leases than it gets for current solar leases.

On the school funding side, the Clark County Education Association released a plan on September 9, 2026 that would end the abatement of the Local School Support Tax for data centers and revisit the 75 percent personal property tax abatement. The union estimates the change could send $2 billion into the state's pupil centered funding formula over four years. Its broader eight point plan also asks for local control over siting, dry cooling or closed loop systems so cooling does not deplete water, power sources that do not hit ratepayers, mandatory compliance audits, and data center revenue directed to workforce retraining. Union leaders said they are not calling for a moratorium. The Nevada State Education Association and the Sierra Club Toiyabe Chapter both criticized the proposal.

Put those pieces side by side and you get the picture. The industry is expanding fast. The rules that govern how much it pays and where it can go are still being written.

It is also worth noticing the direction of travel. Apex spent years as the valley's open land, the place people drove past on the way out of town. Now it is where the largest checks in Southern Nevada real estate are being written. That shift did not happen in one deal. It happened one parcel at a time, and this purchase is the latest one.

The Boulder City and CCEA items above come from separate reporting, but they belong in the same frame as the Apex sale. One company buying land, one city asking its voters about land use, and one union asking the state about tax policy are three parts of a single question: how does Southern Nevada want this industry to grow here.

What Happens Next

The land is bought. The next milestones are permits and site plans. Any construction at Apex has to move through the normal approval process, which means public meeting agendas in North Las Vegas and at Clark County. Those agendas are where residents get their first look at real building footprints, heights, traffic studies, and utility plans.

Watch the power side too. Large users have to work out how they get served, and those arrangements go through regulatory review. That is where the ratepayer question gets settled, and it is the piece that most directly touches your monthly bill. Nothing has been published about how this specific parcel will be powered, so treat any claim about that as unverified for now.

Boulder City's Question 1 lands on the November ballot. However it goes, the result will tell every city in the valley something about how residents feel about this industry. A yes vote opens up leasing conversations in Eldorado Valley. A no vote keeps that land off the table and pushes demand back toward places like Apex.

The tax question runs on a longer clock. CCEA's plan is a proposal, not a law. Changing the sales tax rate or the personal property abatement for data centers would take action in Carson City, and that means the next legislative session. Expect this debate to keep building between now and then.

Finally, watch for more land deals. Switch has been assembling ground across Southern Nevada for years. This purchase fits the pattern, and the pattern has not shown signs of stopping.

One more thing to keep an eye on is the road network. Big industrial users bring truck traffic and shift traffic, and the state and county plan road improvements around where that traffic is expected. Ryan's Take

Here is how I read this as someone who sells homes in this valley every week. North Las Vegas has been quietly building an economic base that does not depend on the Strip. Apex is the clearest example. Distribution, manufacturing, and now data centers are all landing in the same corridor, and that changes the long term value story for the neighborhoods nearby.

For years, buyers looking at North Las Vegas asked me the same question: what is the commute like to the Strip or to the airport. That is still a fair question. But more of my clients now work somewhere other than the resort corridor, and a growing share of the valley's steady, non tourism jobs sit north of the 215. When a company writes a $196 million check for land at Apex, it is making a bet on that corridor. Homeowners up there are on the same side of that bet.

I will be honest about the other half of it too. The power and water conversation is not noise, and it is not settled. If you own a home in Clark County, your utility bill and your water future are part of this story. I would rather my clients go into that conversation with real numbers than with slogans from either direction. Right now the verified numbers are thin, and I am not going to fill the gaps with guesses.

The practical advice I give buyers is the same as always. Buy the neighborhood, not the headline. A land sale at Apex does not change what a house on your street is worth this week. What it does is add another reason to believe the north valley will keep adding jobs, and jobs are what hold home values up over a decade. If you were already comfortable in North Las Vegas, this is a point in your favor. If you were not, one land deal should not talk you into it.

What You Can Do

If you live near Apex or anywhere in North Las Vegas, start with the meeting calendars. The City of North Las Vegas and Clark County both post agendas online before their planning and council meetings. Site plans, zoning items, and traffic studies show up there first. Reading an agenda item takes five minutes and gives you far better information than a social media post about the same project.

If the power and water questions are what worry you, follow the regulatory process rather than the headlines. Utility service arrangements for large customers are reviewed in public proceedings, and the documents are public. That is where the actual numbers live. The same goes for water. Conservation and allocation decisions in Southern Nevada are made in public meetings with published materials.

If you are in Boulder City, read Question 1 before November. It is a land use question about whether data centers become an approved use inside the Eldorado Valley Transfer Area, and whether the City Council may lease up to 14,000 additional acres. Each individual project would still go through the city's normal review. Knowing exactly what the question does, and what it does not do, is worth the ten minutes.

And if you are thinking about buying or selling in North Las Vegas, factor the job base into your timeline. Employment growth is one of the slower moving forces in real estate, but it is one of the most reliable. Knowing which corridors are attracting serious employers helps you pick a neighborhood you will still be happy with in ten years.
