Boulder City Voters Will Decide in November Whether to Open 14,000 More Acres to Data Centers

Boulder City voters will answer a land question this November. Question 1 asks whether data center facilities should become an approved land use inside the Eldorado Valley Transfer Area, on the part of that land that sits outside the Multi-species Habitat Conservation Easement.

If voters say yes, the Boulder City Council would have the ability to lease up to 14,000 additional acres to data center developers. If voters say no, that land stays off limits to data centers, and the city keeps leasing it the way it does today.

This is a land use and city revenue question. It is not a race between people. Below is a plain look at what the land is, what the vote would change, what it would not change, and what both answers would mean for Boulder City's budget and for the homes around it.

What Question 1 Actually Asks

Boulder City owns a very large piece of desert called the Eldorado Valley Transfer Area. It is about 100,000 acres. That is the tract at the center of this vote.

The Boulder City charter does something unusual with that land. It bars development on the tract unless the voters approve it first. In most Nevada cities, a council can approve a land use on city land on its own. In Boulder City, the charter puts a gate in front of that decision, and only voters can open it. That is why this question is on a ballot at all instead of on a council agenda.

Not all 100,000 acres are in play. More than 86,000 acres of the tract are protected by a Multi-species Habitat Conservation Easement. That easement exists to protect desert species and their habitat, and Question 1 does not touch it. The question applies only to land outside that easement.

That leaves the piece people are actually voting on. If Question 1 passes, the City Council could lease up to 14,000 additional acres in that area to data center developers. Fourteen thousand acres is the ceiling the question describes, not a project that is already drawn up and ready to build.

Here is a detail worth reading twice. A yes vote would not approve any specific data center. It would add data centers to the list of uses the council is allowed to consider on that land. Every individual project would still go through the city's normal land use and leasing review after that. The vote opens a door. It does not put a building on the other side of it.

It also helps to know what 14,000 acres looks like. That is roughly 22 square miles of desert, which sounds enormous until you set it next to the 100,000 acre tract it comes out of. It is a meaningful slice, and it is also a small fraction of the whole. Both of those statements are true at the same time, and people on each side of this question tend to reach for only one of them.

A no vote is just as clear. Data center facilities would not become an approved use in that area. The council would not gain the ability to sign those leases. The land would stay in its current status, and anyone who wanted to change that later would have to come back to the voters again.

Why It Matters to Southern Nevada Residents

The clearest reason this matters is money. Boulder City already leases parts of this land, and solar projects are the biggest current tenant type out there. Supporters of Question 1 point to a simple comparison: the city could charge roughly 10 times more rent per acre for data center leases than it charges for its current solar leases.

Think about what a multiplier like that means for a small city. Land lease revenue is money the city collects without raising a tax on a homeowner. It flows into the general fund and helps pay for the ordinary things residents notice, like streets, parks, public safety staffing, and the water system. A city with more lease income has more room to work with. A city without it leans harder on other sources.

That is the honest case for yes, and it is a real one. It is also the reason the no side of this question is not simply about being against growth. Land you lease to a data center is land you are not leasing to something else for the length of that lease. Acreage is a one time decision in practice. Once a use is approved and a lease is signed, the flexibility is gone for a long stretch of years.

There is a quality of life layer too, and it is fair to name it without exaggerating it. Data centers are large, low profile buildings that use a lot of electricity and need cooling. Southern Nevada has been arguing about power capacity and water for years, and every new large user adds to that conversation. At the same time, these buildings generate relatively little traffic compared to a warehouse or a retail center, and they sit on land that is currently empty desert several miles from neighborhoods.

It also helps to be precise about what a lease is and what it is not. Boulder City would not be selling this land. A lease keeps the city as the owner and turns the acreage into an income producing asset for a set term. That is a different decision from a sale, and it is why the rent per acre number matters so much in this conversation. The city is essentially deciding what kind of tenant it wants on ground it will still own decades from now.

For a homeowner, the practical translation is straightforward. City revenue and city services move together over the long run. When a small city has a stable non tax revenue line, it has more cushion when costs rise, and residents feel that in the quality of the streets, the parks, and the response times. When that cushion is thin, the pressure eventually shows up somewhere else. Neither outcome is automatic, and neither is a promise, but that is the mechanism behind the yes argument.

For people who do not live in Boulder City, this vote still carries weight. Boulder City is the last part of the valley that has deliberately held its own line on growth, and the rest of Clark County watches what it does. If Boulder City opens land to data centers, it tells the regional market that the appetite for this kind of development now reaches every corner of the valley. If it does not, that tells the market something too.

Background: How Boulder City Ended Up With This Question

Boulder City has always handled land differently from its neighbors. The charter rule requiring a public vote before development on the Eldorado Valley tract is the plainest example. It was written to keep a very large piece of city owned desert from being committed by one council in one meeting. It puts slow, deliberate friction in front of a big decision on purpose.

The conservation easement on more than 86,000 acres is the second layer. That easement locked away the large majority of the tract for habitat protection, which means the land actually available for any kind of lease was always the smaller remainder. When people hear "100,000 acres," it is easy to picture the entire tract being up for grabs. It is not. Question 1 concerns the part outside the easement, and it names a 14,000 acre ceiling.

The third layer is the solar era. Over the last stretch of years, Boulder City leased large blocks of Eldorado Valley land to solar projects, and those leases became a real revenue line for a small city. That experience is exactly what makes the current comparison possible. The city knows what an acre of its desert rents for under a solar lease, so it has a baseline to measure a data center lease against. That baseline is where the roughly 10 times figure comes from.

The fourth layer is regional. Data center demand in Southern Nevada has been climbing hard. Just up the valley, Las Vegas data center company Switch paid $196 million for 176.5 acres in North Las Vegas at Apex Industrial Park, a sale by two separate ownership groups that closed on August 31. Switch has been buying large tracts across Southern Nevada and borrowing billions over the past few years as it builds out capacity. That is the market pressure sitting behind Boulder City's question.

It is worth pausing on the charter rule itself, because it is the most unusual part of this story. Most land use fights in Clark County happen at a zoning hearing where a handful of appointed and elected officials make a call. Boulder City wrote its rules so that a decision this size cannot be made that way. Whether you like the outcome or not, the process here hands ordinary residents the pen, which is rare.

Put those four layers together and the ballot question makes sense. A city with a lot of desert, a charter that hands the decision to voters, an existing lease program with known numbers, and a regional industry that wants land. The question is the mechanism Boulder City uses to answer a question like this. It is working exactly the way the charter intended.

What Happens Next

Between now and November, the practical work is informational. The question text, the maps of the affected acreage, and the city's own materials are what a voter should be reading. The most useful thing any resident can do is look at where the 14,000 acres would actually sit relative to the easement boundary and relative to town.

If the question passes, nothing is built the next morning. Passage adds data center facilities to the approved uses on that land and gives the council the ability to enter leases up to the 14,000 acre ceiling. Each proposal after that runs through the city's normal land use and leasing process, which means public meetings, city staff review, and council votes on the specific terms. Residents who care about a particular site would follow those individual hearings, not the ballot.

If the question fails, the land keeps its current status and the council does not gain that leasing authority. The city continues with the lease program it already has. Any future attempt to add data centers as an approved use in that area would have to go back through the charter's voter approval gate, which means another ballot question at a future election.

Either way, watch two things after the votes are counted. The first is the pace. A 14,000 acre ceiling is a maximum, not a schedule, and how quickly the council moves toward it, if at all, will tell residents more than the ballot result did. The second is the terms. Rent per acre, lease length, and what a developer is required to provide are decided in the individual lease agreements, and those details are where the roughly 10 times comparison either holds up in practice or does not.

One practical note. Confirm the exact election date, your registration status, and the mail ballot and early voting windows with the Boulder City Clerk and the Clark County Election Department, since those specifics are set on the county's election calendar. [NOT VERIFIED]

Ryan's Take

I am not going to tell anyone how to vote on this one, and I do not think a real estate agent should. What I will say is that this is one of the cleaner civic questions I have seen on a Southern Nevada ballot, because both answers are legible. Yes means the council gets the ability to lease up to 14,000 acres for a use that pays roughly 10 times what solar pays per acre, with each project still reviewed on its own. No means that land stays as it is and the question comes back another day if someone wants it to.

From a housing standpoint, Boulder City is its own market and it always has been. Inventory is thin, the town caps its own growth, and buyers who want to live there are usually buying the town itself and not just the house. A revenue stream that helps fund city services without a new tax is a real factor in what it costs to own a home in a small city over time. So is the character of the land around town, which is a big part of why people pay to live there in the first place. Those two things are genuinely in tension, and that tension is the whole vote. Read the question, look at the map, and decide which one you weigh heavier.

What You Can Do

Start with the ballot question itself. Read the full text of Question 1 rather than a summary of it, including the words about the Eldorado Valley Transfer Area and the Multi-species Habitat Conservation Easement. The wording tells you which land is affected and which land is not, and that single distinction clears up most of the confusion around this question.

Next, go to a Boulder City Council meeting or watch one. Land use and leasing items are discussed in public, and the staff presentations are usually the best plain English explanation available of how the city's lease program works and what it currently brings in. If you want to understand the revenue argument, that is where the real numbers live.

It also helps to talk to your neighbors about it, calmly. In a town this size, most people will hear about Question 1 secondhand, and a lot of that secondhand version will be wrong in the same two ways: that the whole 100,000 acres is on the table, and that a yes vote approves a specific project. Neither is true. Correcting those two points in a conversation over a fence does more good than almost anything else you can do here.

Finally, check your voter registration and your ballot options with the Clark County Election Department well before the deadline, and encourage your neighbors to do the same. A question like this one is decided by the people who show up, and Boulder City is small enough that local turnout genuinely moves the result. Whichever way you lean, the worst outcome is having an opinion and not casting it.
