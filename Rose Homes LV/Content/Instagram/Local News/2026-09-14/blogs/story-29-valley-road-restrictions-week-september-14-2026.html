This Week's Valley Road Restrictions: Buffalo and Charleston, Lake Mead Boulevard, and the Charleston Storm Drain

Three road projects are squeezing lanes in Clark County this week, and all three sit on streets that locals use every single day. Intersection work at Buffalo Drive and Charleston Boulevard is holding traffic down to one lane in each direction on every approach, Monday through Friday from 7 a.m. to 6 p.m. Lake Mead Boulevard between Losee Road and Simmons Street stays torn up through fall 2026. The Charleston storm drain job from Essex to Lindell is in final cleanup with a target finish of September 19, 2026.

All of this comes from the Regional Transportation Commission of Southern Nevada's Seeing Orange traffic page, which the agency updates every Thursday. That weekly refresh matters. What is true on Monday morning may not be true by Friday, so treat this article as a starting point and check the official page before you actually leave the house.

What Is Happening, Corridor by Corridor

Let us take these one at a time, because each corridor pulls in a different group of drivers. If you only care about one of them, skip to your street.

Buffalo Drive and Charleston Boulevard. This is intersection improvement work, and it is the restriction most likely to cost you time this week. Crews are holding one lane open in each direction on all approaches to the intersection. That means northbound Buffalo, southbound Buffalo, eastbound Charleston, and westbound Charleston are all reduced. The restriction runs Monday through Friday, from 7 a.m. to 6 p.m. That window covers the morning commute, the whole workday, the school pickup run, and the start of the evening rush.

Charleston Boulevard storm drain, Essex to Lindell. This project is in final construction cleanup. The target completion date is September 19, 2026. Final cleanup usually means the heavy digging is done and crews are finishing surfaces, striping, and site work, but the RTC's alert page is the place to confirm what is actually restricted on any given day. The Essex to Lindell stretch sits east of Buffalo along the same Charleston corridor, which is why this week feels rough if Charleston is your main east and west route.

Lake Mead Boulevard, Losee Road to Simmons Street. This one is the long haul. Road improvements on that segment continue through fall 2026. Crews are keeping a minimum of one lane open in each direction. Martin Luther King Boulevard southbound is reduced to two lanes as part of the same work. This corridor runs through North Las Vegas and connects a lot of neighborhoods to a lot of jobs, so the impact is broad even though the work is not new.

One more thing worth saying plainly. The Seeing Orange list is a snapshot of the week it was published. It does not claim to be every cone in the valley. Utility crews, private developers, and city maintenance teams all put up short-term restrictions that may never make a regional alert page. So if you round a corner and find a lane taped off that nobody warned you about, that is normal. The alert page covers the big, scheduled, multi-week items, which are exactly the ones worth planning around.

Who These Restrictions Actually Touch

Charleston Boulevard is one of the valley's true spine streets. It runs east and west across a huge slice of the city, and it does not stop being useful just because a newer beltway exists. People use Charleston because it connects neighborhoods to neighborhoods, not because it is fast. When two separate projects sit on the same corridor in the same week, one at Buffalo and one between Essex and Lindell, the corridor stops being the easy default it usually is.

Buffalo Drive plays the same role running north and south on the west side. It is the street people take when they do not want to deal with a freeway for a three-mile trip. Households in the neighborhoods that feed off Buffalo between Charleston and the residential streets nearby are going to notice this one most, because Buffalo is often the only realistic way out of the pocket they live in.

Lake Mead Boulevard is a different animal. Between Losee Road and Simmons Street it moves people across North Las Vegas, and the neighborhoods along it lean residential and working. Add the southbound reduction on Martin Luther King Boulevard and you have two north and south and east and west options constrained at once in the same part of the county. For a household with one car and a shift start time, that is not an abstraction. That is a real decision about leaving fifteen minutes earlier every day through the fall.

Renters and homeowners feel this differently, too. If you rent and your lease is up, a rough commute is something you can solve by moving. If you own, you wait it out, and you hope the finished product is better than what you had. In this case the finished product is the point. Intersection improvements and storm drain capacity are long-life investments in streets that were built for a smaller Las Vegas.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Buffalo and Charleston is not a tourist intersection. Nobody flies into Harry Reid and drives out to Buffalo and Charleston. That corner belongs to people who live in northwest and west Las Vegas, and to the workers, parents, and delivery drivers who cross it a dozen times a week. When an intersection like that drops to one lane in every direction for eleven hours a day, the delay does not land on visitors. It lands on you.

Think about how a single-lane approach behaves. Every left turn blocks the only lane behind it. Every bus stop, every hesitant driver, every car waiting to pull into a shopping center becomes a chokepoint. The backup does not stay at the intersection either. It stretches back down Charleston and down Buffalo, and it touches the side streets and neighborhood entrances that feed into them. If you live in a subdivision off either road, your own driveway can feel the effect.

The 7 a.m. to 6 p.m. window is the part people underestimate. A lot of construction restrictions run overnight so they miss the rush. This one does the opposite. It is live for the morning drive, live for midday errands, live for school release, and live for the front half of the evening rush. The only clean windows are early morning before 7 and evening after 6, plus weekends, when the restriction is not listed as being in effect.

On the Lake Mead Boulevard side, the story is different but the pain is real. That corridor carries people across North Las Vegas, and with Martin Luther King Boulevard southbound also down to two lanes, two of the area's useful connections are pinched at the same time. A minimum of one lane each direction means traffic keeps moving, but it moves slowly, and it moves slowly for months, not days. Fall 2026 is the stated horizon.

Background and History

The Seeing Orange page exists because Southern Nevada has a lot of agencies digging in a lot of places at once. The RTC of Southern Nevada maintains it as a single regional list of traffic and transit alerts so drivers do not have to check a different website for every city, every county department, and every utility. It updates every Thursday, which is a useful thing to know. If you look on a Wednesday, you are looking at last week's list.

That weekly cadence also tells you something about how this kind of work gets managed. Road projects are scheduled, then rescheduled. Weather moves them. Material delivery moves them. A utility conflict found in the trench moves them. The agency does not publish a list that is good for a month because a list that is good for a month would be wrong within a week.

Storm drain work has its own history in this valley, and it is worth understanding why Charleston got dug up in the first place. Las Vegas sits in a desert basin with hard ground and very little natural absorption. When monsoon rain comes, it comes fast, and it runs across pavement instead of soaking in. Storm drain infrastructure is what keeps that water from turning surface streets into washes. The Essex to Lindell segment of Charleston is one more piece of that system. The short-term cost is a few months of cones. The long-term benefit is a street that handles a summer storm better.

Intersection improvement projects like the one at Buffalo and Charleston tend to be about capacity and safety at the corner itself. The RTC's alert lists the restriction and the hours. It does not, on the alert page, spell out the full design scope or a completion date for the Buffalo and Charleston work. What Happens Next

The nearest hard date in this set is September 19, 2026, the target completion for the Charleston storm drain project between Essex and Lindell. Target is the operative word. A target completion date is a plan, not a promise, and a week of bad weather or one unexpected find in the trench can move it. If it holds, that stretch of Charleston should get easier right after.

Lake Mead Boulevard from Losee to Simmons is the opposite kind of timeline. That work is scheduled to continue through fall 2026, with at least one lane open each direction the whole time and Martin Luther King Boulevard southbound held at two lanes. Plan around it as a standing condition rather than a one-week inconvenience. If that corridor is part of your daily drive, the smarter move is to rebuild your routine around it instead of hoping it clears next week.

For Buffalo and Charleston, the restriction as listed is a weekday pattern, 7 a.m. to 6 p.m., Monday through Friday. Expect it to appear again on the next Thursday refresh until the work is finished. The single most useful habit you can build is checking the Seeing Orange page on Thursday evening or Friday morning, once a week, for the week ahead. It takes about ninety seconds and it is more reliable than any secondhand summary, including this one.

Ryan's Take

I drive this valley for a living, so I have opinions about orange cones. Here is the honest one. Short-term road work is annoying and long-term road work is a sign your area is getting attention. Storm drain capacity on Charleston and improvements on Lake Mead Boulevard are the kind of unglamorous infrastructure that quietly protects property. A street that floods every monsoon is a street buyers ask about. A street that drains is a street nobody thinks about, which is exactly what you want.

The part that actually shows up in real estate is commute time, and it shows up more than people expect. When I take buyers out in the northwest, the question underneath every showing is how long it takes to get to work, to school, and to the grocery store. A temporary lane restriction does not change what a home is worth. But if you are touring homes near Buffalo and Charleston on a weekday afternoon right now, you are seeing that corner at its absolute worst, and that is not a fair picture of the neighborhood. Drive it again on a Saturday morning before you decide anything.

What You Can Do

First, go to the source. The RTC of Southern Nevada publishes the current restrictions at seeingorangenv.com, updated every Thursday. Bookmark it. Check it before a week when you have appointments that cannot slide. Schedules shift with weather and with field conditions, and the official page is the only version that is current.

Second, adjust your timing where you can. The Buffalo and Charleston restriction is listed for weekdays from 7 a.m. to 6 p.m. If your schedule has any flex at all, moving a trip to before 7 a.m. or after 6 p.m. is the cheapest fix available. The same logic applies to errands. Saturday morning on that corner is a very different experience than Tuesday at 4 p.m.

Third, know your parallel streets before you need them. The RTC alert does not publish official detour routes for these restrictions, so anything you pick is your own call, not an agency recommendation. Still, the west side of the valley has a grid, and a grid gives you options. Look at the arterials that run parallel to Charleston and parallel to Buffalo on a map tonight, pick one you already know, and have it in your head before you are sitting in a line of brake lights deciding in the moment.

Fourth, give the crews room. One open lane in each direction means workers are close to moving traffic. Slow down, put the phone away, and do not try to beat the merge. It is a work zone, and the people in the orange vests are somebody's neighbors.

Fifth, share what you know. If you live on a street that feeds into Buffalo and Charleston or into the Lake Mead Boulevard work zone, tell your neighbors. Post it in the community group. The single best traffic tool in any neighborhood is a neighbor who left five minutes ago and can tell you what it actually looks like right now.

And one last reminder, because it is the most important line in this whole article. Schedules shift with weather. Southern Nevada gets monsoon moisture, high heat, and wind, and any of those can stop a crew or push a target date. Every detail here comes from the RTC's weekly alert list as it stood for the week of September 14, 2026. Before you build your morning around it, open the official page and confirm.
