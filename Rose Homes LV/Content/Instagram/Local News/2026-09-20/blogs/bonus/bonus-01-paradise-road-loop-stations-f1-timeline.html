The Vegas Loop's Paradise Road Timeline: Every Station and Every Date Between Now and F1 Weekend

Three new Vegas Loop stations are scheduled to land on the Paradise Road corridor before the Formula 1 Las Vegas Grand Prix runs November 19 through 21, and the tunnels feeding them open in stages rather than all at once. If you live, work, or commute anywhere near Paradise Road, the useful thing is not the ribbon cutting. It is the order the pieces switch on.

We covered the original announcement in Vegas Loop Paradise Tunnels Weeks Away. This piece is the timeline itself, laid out station by station and date by date, so you can see exactly what is supposed to be running by the time race weekend arrives and what is still a construction zone.

The Paradise Road Buildout, Stage by Stage

Start with the tunnel, because nothing else opens until the tunnel does. The segment in question is a little over two miles of dual Boring Company tunnels running under the Paradise Road corridor. Dual means two bores, one for each direction of travel, which is how the Loop avoids the single-lane bottleneck problem that slowed the original convention center segment during peak events.

The tunnel runs between Westgate Las Vegas on the north end and a planned station at 4744 Paradise Road on the south end. That is the spine. Everything else described below hangs off it. LVCVA President and CEO Steve Hill said in mid-September that this segment would open in stages within weeks, with the hope of starting by the end of September and near certainty of being underway in October.

Stage one, then, is the Westgate side. Westgate already sits at the edge of the existing convention center network, so that end of the tunnel plugs into something that is already carrying passengers. When the northern portion lights up, riders get a longer continuous run without surfacing.

Stage two is the south end at 4744 Paradise Road. That address is a new station, not an existing resort connection, and it is the anchor for the southern half of the segment. Once both ends are live, the full two-mile run under Paradise is functioning as a single corridor rather than two disconnected pieces.

The other two new stations Hill named are Virgin Hotels Las Vegas and the former Gordon Biersch site behind Sphere. Those are the two that most locals will recognize immediately. Virgin Hotels sits on Paradise Road itself, and the Gordon Biersch parcel sits in the block behind Sphere, which puts a Loop entrance within walking distance of one of the busiest event venues in the county.

It is worth being precise about what "in stages" means here, because it is the whole point of the timeline. A staged opening is not a soft launch of a finished product. It is a series of real openings, each one adding usable distance to the system, with construction continuing on the sections that have not opened yet. So the corridor does not go from closed to complete on a single morning. It gets longer, in steps, over several weeks.

That also means the answer to "is the Paradise segment open" will be yes and no at the same time for part of October. A rider could reasonably be told the tunnel is open while the specific station they wanted is not yet accepting passengers. If you are planning around it, ask about the station, not the tunnel. The station is the part that determines whether a trip is actually possible.

The hard deadline sitting over all of it is November 19. Hill said he expects both Paradise tunnels to be open ahead of the Grand Prix, which runs November 19 through 21. That is the date the schedule is built around, and it is the date to judge the project against. Everything between late September and mid-November is the window.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Paradise Road is not a tourist-only street. It runs past the convention center, past UNLV, past hospitals, past office parks, and past a long line of apartment buildings and older single-family pockets that house people who work in the resort corridor. When something changes the way Paradise Road moves, it changes a commute that thousands of Clark County residents make every day.

That is what makes this stretch different from most of the Loop's map. A lot of the system so far has connected resort to resort, which is great for a convention attendee and mostly irrelevant to somebody living off Swenson or Cambridge. Paradise Road is one of the few places the system touches property that locals actually use. Three new stations on that corridor is a meaningful shift.

The short-term effect is the opposite of relief. Tunnel work, station construction, and the surface restoration that follows all take lane space. Anyone who has driven Paradise during an active construction phase knows what that feels like at 8 a.m. and again at 5 p.m. The stage-by-stage opening is partly about getting those surface impacts cleared in sequence instead of all at once.

The longer-term effect is about access. A Loop station is not a train stop, and it is not going to replace a car for a family in Spring Valley. But for somebody who works a shift at a Paradise Road property, or who attends events at Sphere several times a year, a walkable underground connection is a real reduction in parking cost and parking stress. That is the kind of thing that eventually shows up in how renters and buyers rank a location.

There is also a plain traffic math point. Every trip that goes underground is a trip that is not sitting at a light on Paradise. The system will not move enough people to erase congestion on a race weekend, and nobody serious is claiming it will. But on a normal Tuesday in February, a corridor with three tunnel entrances behaves a little differently than a corridor with none.

Renters feel this first. The apartment stock along and just off Paradise Road is deep, and a lot of it houses people who work within a few miles of where they sleep. For that group, a station entrance they can walk to is not a novelty, it is a potential change to a daily routine and a monthly parking bill. Rental demand tends to respond to convenience faster than sale prices do, because a lease turns over every year and a mortgage does not.

Background and History

The Vegas Loop did not start as a valley-wide network. It started as a short underground connector serving the Las Vegas Convention Center campus, built and operated by the Boring Company for the LVCVA, and it was designed to solve one specific problem, which was moving convention attendees across a very large campus quickly.

That first segment worked well enough as a proof of concept that the conversation shifted almost immediately from "does this work" to "how far can it go." Approvals followed for a much larger franchise area covering the resort corridor and stretching beyond it, with individual property owners signing on to fund and build their own station connections. That structure is the key to understanding why the map grows in an uneven, property-by-property pattern rather than in neat straight lines.

Each station gets built when the property behind it decides to build it. That is why you see clusters: a group of adjacent properties commits, a tunnel segment gets dug between them, and a chunk of the map fills in. The Paradise Road corridor is exactly that kind of cluster, with Westgate, Virgin Hotels, the 4744 Paradise Road site, and the parcel behind Sphere forming a workable chain.

The other piece of the background is the event calendar. Las Vegas has spent the last several years stacking major events into November, and Formula 1 is the biggest of them in terms of street impact. The race uses public roads, which means road closures, which means the alternatives to those roads matter more than usual. That is why an F1 deadline shows up on a tunnel project at all. The Grand Prix is functioning as the forcing date.

It is also useful to remember how new all of this still is. The first public segment has been carrying passengers for only a handful of years. Most transit systems are judged over decades, and the Loop is nowhere near that point. Paradise Road is one of the first real tests of whether the network can extend past a single campus and start behaving like a connected corridor rather than a shuttle.

What Happens Next

Here is the sequence to watch, in order. First, the initial stage of the Paradise segment opens, targeted for the end of September and expected no later than October. That first opening is the one to pay attention to, because it tells you whether the rest of the schedule is realistic or whether it is already slipping.

Second, the remaining stages of the two-mile segment come online through October. Hill described this as a staged process, so expect the corridor to become usable in pieces rather than switching on complete. Practically, that means a rider may be able to make part of the trip underground and part of it on the surface for a stretch of weeks.

Third, the three new stations at 4744 Paradise Road, Virgin Hotels Las Vegas, and the former Gordon Biersch site behind Sphere come into service. Station openings and tunnel openings are not automatically the same event. A tunnel can be complete while a station above it is still finishing interior work and inspections.

Fourth is the November 19 checkpoint. Hill's stated expectation is that both Paradise tunnels are open before race weekend. If that holds, the corridor goes into its highest-demand three days of the year with the new capacity available. If it slips, the fallback is the same surface network everyone already uses, under race-weekend closures.

One honest caveat. Opening dates on tunnel projects move, and "weeks away" in mid-September is a forecast, not a guarantee. Treat the end-of-September target as optimistic, the October window as the realistic one, and the pre-F1 deadline as the commitment that actually carries weight.

After race weekend, watch a quieter signal. The interesting number is not how many people ride during F1, because a three-day event will fill anything. The interesting number is how the corridor performs in January and February, on ordinary weekdays, once the crowds are gone. That is the reading that tells you whether Paradise Road has gained a piece of everyday infrastructure or a piece of event infrastructure. Those are two very different things for a homeowner nearby.

Ryan's Take

I pay attention to transit projects because they are one of the few things that change a neighborhood's value slowly and permanently rather than quickly and temporarily. A new restaurant opens and closes. A tunnel entrance does not move. When a corridor gains three access points to a system that connects to the convention center and the Strip, the properties near those entrances get a little more useful every year the network grows.

That said, I would not tell a buyer to pay a premium today for a Paradise Road address on the strength of a Loop station. The system is still young, the map is still filling in property by property, and the honest answer about long-term ridership is that nobody knows yet. What I would say is that the downside risk is low and the option value is real. You are buying near infrastructure that is being built, not infrastructure that is being studied.

The near-term reality is less romantic. Between now and November, the Paradise corridor is a construction corridor, and if you are showing or selling a property along that stretch, plan your routes and set expectations with clients before they hit a lane closure on the way to a showing. Small thing, but it is the difference between a smooth appointment and a client who arrives annoyed.

What You Can Do

If you drive Paradise Road regularly, build a habit now of checking conditions before you leave. Staged openings mean staged construction, and the restrictions will move around as crews finish one section and start the next. A two-minute check saves a twenty-minute detour, especially during the weeks when a station entrance is being finished at street level.

If you are planning around race weekend, remember that the Grand Prix runs November 19 through 21 and that the street closures around it are a separate issue from the tunnel work. Our rundown of the closure map is in F1 Road Closures on the Las Vegas Strip, and it is worth reading alongside this one if you live or work inside the affected zone.

If you own a home or a rental near the Paradise corridor, this is a good moment to think about how you describe the location. Proximity to a transit connection is a feature worth mentioning, and it is the kind of detail that gets left off a listing because the seller stopped noticing it. Three new entrances within a two-mile stretch is a story a buyer from out of state will not figure out on their own.

And if you are on the fence about the corridor as a place to buy, go drive it at two different hours. Drive Paradise at 8 a.m. on a weekday and again at 9 p.m. on a Saturday. The corridor behaves like two different streets, and no amount of reading substitutes for sitting in it yourself. That is the test I would run before making a decision on any address inside this stretch.
