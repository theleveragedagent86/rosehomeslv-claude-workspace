Henderson Homeowners Own the Water Line Under Their Yard, and the City Just Made That Very Public

The buried water and sewer lines that run across your property in Henderson belong to you, not to the city, and standard homeowners insurance usually will not pay to dig them up when they break. The city of Henderson made that point in public on Thursday, September 17, 2026, when it announced it has contracted with HomeServe's Service Line Warranties of America to offer residents optional repair plans for those lines.

Enrollment is completely voluntary. Nobody in Henderson is required to buy anything, and the city is not billing anyone for it. But the announcement did something more useful than sell a product. It put a spotlight on a piece of home ownership that a lot of people in this valley have no idea they are responsible for until a plumber is standing in their front yard with a shovel.

That is the part worth your attention. Whether or not a repair plan makes sense for your house is a personal decision you should make after reading the actual terms and checking your own policy. Understanding where your responsibility starts and stops is not optional at all.

What Henderson Announced

The city of Henderson said it has partnered with HomeServe, working through the company's Service Line Warranties of America brand, to make optional water and sewer service line repair plans available to Henderson homeowners. The plans cover repair work on the portions of those utility lines that sit on private property. SLWA also offers coverage options for interior plumbing and for interior drainage, so the water and sewer line plans are not the only products a homeowner might be shown.

In its announcement, the city underlined the reason the partnership exists at all. Sections of the water and sewer lines that run across private property are the homeowner's responsibility to maintain and to repair. Those sections are typically not covered by a standard homeowners insurance policy. That combination is what leaves people surprised when a line fails.

Henderson is not the first city in the country to do this. Arrangements like this one are common across the United States, where a municipality lets a service line repair company market to residents under a formal agreement. The city's involvement generally means the company can reference the city in its mailings, which is precisely why residents should read carefully rather than assume a letter with the city's name on it is a bill or a requirement.

Two things are worth stating plainly. First, this is an optional third-party product, not a city utility charge and not a new fee. Second, nothing here is a recommendation. I am not endorsing the plan, I do not sell it, and I have no stake in whether anyone signs up. What I want Henderson homeowners to walk away with is the underlying fact about who owns the pipe, because that fact is true whether you enroll or not.

Why It Matters to Henderson and Clark County Residents

Picture the water line for your house. It starts at the city main, which usually runs under the street. It passes through a meter, which sits near the curb or in a box at the front of the property. From the meter, the line continues across your yard, under whatever is on top of it, and into your home.

That last stretch, from the meter to the house, is almost always the homeowner's. The sewer side works the same way in reverse. The lateral line that carries waste from your home out to the city main is generally yours all the way to the connection point. If it cracks, collapses, or clogs beyond a simple snaking, you are the one hiring the contractor.

Now add the Las Vegas valley to the picture. Our soil is dense and rocky in a lot of places, and caliche layers can make digging slow and expensive. Older Henderson neighborhoods, particularly the ones built decades ago near Water Street and the original townsite, may have older pipe materials in the ground. Newer master planned areas out toward Inspirada, Cadence, and the Anthem foothills have younger lines, but they also tend to have more landscaping, hardscape, and driveway concrete sitting directly on top of the run. When the dig happens, the pipe is often the cheap part. Restoring what was above it is not.

Here is the other piece. Most homeowners insurance policies are built to respond to sudden events inside or against the structure, not to gradual wear on buried infrastructure outside it. A policy may cover water damage inside the home that results from a failure, and may not cover the cost of excavating and replacing the failed line itself. Those are two different questions, and a lot of people assume the first answer covers the second. It usually does not. The only way to know what your policy does is to call your agent and ask about your specific service line coverage in writing.

Renters are not on the hook for this, which is worth saying in a valley where a large share of homes are leased. If you rent in Henderson and a service line fails, that is the owner's repair, not yours. But if you own a rental property here, it is yours, and it is one of the maintenance items that rarely shows up in a pro forma. Landlords with several doors across Henderson should think about this the same way they think about a water heater or an HVAC unit, as a known future expense rather than a surprise.

Background and History

The split between public and private responsibility for utility lines is not new and it is not unique to Henderson. Cities build and maintain the mains in the street because those serve everyone. The connection from the main to an individual property serves one household, so historically that piece has fallen to the property owner. Most people never think about it because a well installed service line can sit quietly in the ground for decades.

What changed over the last twenty years or so is that a whole industry grew up around that gap. Companies began offering low cost monthly plans that pay for covered service line repairs, and they began signing agreements with cities to reach residents more credibly. Henderson's arrangement with HomeServe's SLWA brand follows that national pattern.

Henderson itself has grown enormously across that same window. The city is now one of the largest in Nevada, with housing stock spanning everything from mid century homes near the original downtown to brand new construction in the southern and eastern edges of the city. That spread matters. A 1960s home and a 2024 home carry very different risk on this particular issue, and a single blanket answer does not fit both.

There is also a broader water context in Southern Nevada. Water is the most closely watched resource in this valley, and leaks are not just a homeowner expense. A slow break in a private service line can waste a meaningful amount of water before anyone notices, and it can show up first as an unexplained jump in a monthly bill. Paying attention to your line is good for your wallet and good for a region that counts every gallon.

What a Service Line Repair Actually Involves

This is the part almost nobody sees until it happens to them, so it is worth walking through.

It usually starts small. A damp patch of soil in the yard that never dries out. A water bill that climbs with no change in habits. A gurgle in a drain, or a toilet that backs up when the washing machine runs. On the sewer side, a smell near the cleanout is a common first sign.

Next comes diagnosis. For a sewer lateral, a plumber typically runs a camera down the line to find the break, the root intrusion, or the collapsed section, and marks the spot on the surface. For a water line, a leak detection technician may use acoustic equipment to listen for the escape point. Diagnosis alone is a billable visit before any repair begins.

Then the dig. A crew has to reach the pipe, which means opening whatever sits above it. That can be turf, decorative rock, pavers, a concrete walkway, a driveway approach, or a mature tree's root zone. Some repairs can be done with trenchless methods that limit the excavation to access pits at each end, though not every line and not every soil condition allows it. Your plumber will tell you what is possible at your specific address.

After the repair comes inspection and restoration. Depending on the work, a permit and an inspection may be required, and the city can confirm what applies in Henderson. Restoration is the step homeowners forget to budget for. Putting a driveway back, replacing hardscape, and regrading and replanting a yard can easily rival the plumbing bill. Costs vary widely by depth, length, access, and what has to be rebuilt afterward, so any specific dollar figure you read online is a guess until a licensed contractor looks at your property.

What Happens Next

Henderson homeowners should expect marketing. When a city signs an agreement like this, residents generally begin receiving mailers describing the optional plans. Those letters often carry the city's name, and that is exactly the moment to slow down rather than speed up.

A few simple steps protect you. Confirm anything you receive through the city of Henderson's own website or by calling the city directly, rather than trusting a phone number printed on a letter. Read the plan documents, not the envelope. Look for what is covered, what is excluded, whether there is a waiting period before coverage begins, what the annual benefit limit is, whether restoration of landscaping and hardscape is included, and how cancellation works. Those details decide whether a plan is worth anything, and they are not in the headline.

Then compare against what you already have. Call your homeowners insurance agent and ask directly whether your policy includes any service line endorsement. Some carriers sell one as an add on for a small annual amount, and if you already carry it, a separate plan may be redundant. Also check whether a home warranty you already pay for touches this. Paying twice for the same coverage is a common and avoidable mistake.

Finally, consider the alternative of self insuring. Setting aside money in a home maintenance fund covers this risk and every other one, and it never expires or denies a claim. That is a legitimate choice, and for some households it is the better one.

Ryan's Take

I have watched this catch people off guard more than once, and it is almost never during a leisurely week. It shows up mid escrow, or three months after a first time buyer closes and is already stretched. The reaction is always the same. They assumed a buried pipe leading to a city main was the city's problem, and it was not.

So my take is not about the plan. My take is that this is a gap in what buyers get told, and the city putting it in a press release is genuinely useful even if you never enroll. If you are buying in Henderson, ask your inspector about the sewer lateral. A sewer scope is a separate service from a standard home inspection in most cases, and on an older home it is some of the best money you can spend during your due diligence window. A camera down the line before you close tells you something a visual inspection cannot.

If you already own, just know where your meter is and where your cleanout is. Watch your water bill for a jump that has no explanation. Those two habits catch most problems early, when they are still small. And when a letter shows up with the city's name on it, read the terms and compare them to your own policy before you sign anything. That is the honest answer, and it is the same answer whether the plan turns out to be right for you or not.

The last thing I will add is about timing. Sellers get caught by this too. A sewer issue discovered during a buyer's inspection can stall a deal at the worst possible moment, and the repair estimate usually arrives while both sides are already negotiating something else. If you are thinking about listing a Henderson home in the next year, especially an older one, finding out the condition of your lateral early gives you options. You can fix it on your own schedule, price it into the listing, or disclose it and move on. All three beat discovering it on day nine of escrow.

What You Can Do

Start with a five minute inventory of your own property. Find the water meter box near the curb and note where it sits. Walk the likely path from the meter to the house and note what is on top of it, because that tells you what a future repair would have to go through. Locate the sewer cleanout, which is usually a capped pipe near the foundation or in the side yard. Knowing these three things before you ever need them saves time and money later.

Next, make one phone call to your insurance agent. Ask specifically whether your policy covers the repair or replacement of buried water and sewer service lines on your property, and ask whether a service line endorsement is available and what it costs. Get the answer in writing. This single call is the most valuable thing in this article, because it tells you whether you have a gap at all.

If you do want to look at the Henderson plans, go to the city of Henderson's official website or call the city to verify the program before responding to any mailing. Read the full terms, including exclusions, waiting periods, and benefit caps. Compare the annual cost against what a repair would realistically run on your street and against what your insurance already does. Then decide. Enrollment is voluntary, and there is no wrong answer as long as it is an informed one.

One more practical step. Ask a licensed Nevada plumber for a rough estimate of what a line replacement would cost at your address, given your lot and what sits over the run. A real number for your property beats a national average every time.
