Nevada Reading Scores Slipped and Fewer Schools Held Five Stars This Year

Nevada's reading proficiency for grades 3 through 8 slipped to 45.2 percent this year, down 0.4 points, and it is the first drop in three years. At the same time, the share of five-star schools across the state fell from about 17 percent to about 14 percent, while one-star schools rose from about 16 percent to nearly 19 percent.

Math went the other way. Statewide math proficiency for those same grades rose 0.8 points to 36.2 percent. So the headline is not "everything got worse." It is more specific than that, and the details are what matter if you are a parent picking a school or a buyer trying to read a rating before you write an offer.

There is one more new piece this year. Nevada rolled out its first district-level report card, called the Nevada District Performance Framework. Until now the state rated individual schools. Now it also rates the districts that run them, on a three-level scale. That gives families a second number to look at, and it is public.

What Happened

The Nevada Department of Education released its annual pile of school performance data, and The Nevada Independent pulled out the pieces that actually move. The first is reading. Statewide proficiency in English language arts for grades 3 through 8 landed at 45.2 percent. That is a decline of 0.4 points from the prior year, and it breaks a three-year run of small gains.

A 0.4 point move is not a collapse. It is a nudge. But direction matters when you have been climbing back from pandemic-era losses, and this is the first year the reading line pointed down instead of up. Fewer than half of Nevada students in those grades are reading at grade level, which is the bigger and more stubborn fact underneath the year-to-year wiggle.

Math moved the opposite way. Proficiency rose 0.8 points to 36.2 percent statewide. That is still the lower of the two numbers by a wide margin, and it has been the lower number for years. But a gain is a gain, and it is roughly twice the size of the reading dip.

The star ratings shifted too. Nevada rates schools on a one to five star scale built from test scores, growth, graduation rates, English learner progress, and other indicators depending on the grade band. This year the share of schools earning five stars dropped from about 17 percent to about 14 percent statewide. The share sitting at one star climbed from about 16 percent to nearly 19 percent. Both ends moved in the direction nobody wants.

The newest piece is the Nevada District Performance Framework. This is the state's first report card for districts rather than campuses, and it uses three levels instead of five stars. In the first run, Eureka County, Carson City, and the State Public Charter School Authority landed at the top. Elko County, Nye County, and Lander County landed at the bottom. Clark County School District is measured under the same framework alongside every other district in the state.

All of this data lives on the Nevada Report Card, the state's public school accountability site. That is the primary source. Anything you read in a news story, including this one, is a summary of numbers you can pull up yourself in about two minutes.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Clark County is the overwhelming majority of Nevada's public school enrollment. When a statewide number moves, Clark County is usually a big part of why. So a statewide reading slip is not some rural story happening somewhere else. It is the backdrop to the ratings on the schools in your neighborhood, whether you live in Summerlin, Green Valley, Aliante, Mountain's Edge, or a street off Eastern that does not have a fancy community name.

Here is the part that surprises people. Star ratings are relative to a fixed set of targets, so the pool of five-star schools can shrink statewide without any single campus getting worse in a way a parent would notice walking through the front door. A school can lose a star because a growth measure moved, or because a subgroup indicator shifted, or because proficiency slid a point or two. That is very different from a school falling apart. It is also very different from a school improving. Both things happen, and the star alone does not tell you which one you are looking at.

That is why the year-over-year direction is worth more than the single number. A three-star school that was two stars last year is a different story than a three-star school that was four. The Nevada Report Card lets you see the history. Most rating aggregator sites do not, or they bury it.

For families, this data also feeds decisions that are genuinely hard to reverse. Where you buy or rent decides your zoned school for years. Transferring later is possible in Clark County, but it depends on space, program, and timing. It is much easier to check the ratings and the trend before you sign than to sort it out after.

And for homeowners who are not moving anywhere, the ratings still show up. Buyers pull them. Appraisers do not use them, but buyers absolutely do, and buyer behavior is what sets what your home sells for on a given weekend. Knowing what the public data says about your zoned schools is just knowing something about your own asset.

It is also worth saying plainly that Clark County is not one school district experience. It is hundreds of campuses spread across a valley with wildly different building ages, program offerings, and enrollment pressures. A statewide average tells you almost nothing about the specific elementary school six blocks from a house you are considering. The average is context. The campus report is the answer.

Renters should care about this as much as owners, maybe more. A lease decision locks in a zoned school for the school year just like a purchase does, and renters move more often, which means they run this decision more often. The same public data applies, and the same zoning check applies.

Background and History

Nevada has used the star rating system for years as part of its state accountability framework. Every rated school gets a score built from multiple indicators, and the mix changes by level. Elementary and middle schools lean on proficiency and academic growth. High schools add graduation rate and college and career readiness measures. Schools serving English learners carry a progress indicator for that group as well.

Ratings were paused during the pandemic years when testing was disrupted, then restarted. The years since have been a slow climb back, with reading recovering a little faster than math in most years. That is the run this year's 0.4 point reading dip interrupts. It is one year of data, not a trend line, and the honest read is that it is a caution flag rather than an alarm.

Math has been the harder subject for Nevada, and for most states, since the shutdowns. The 36.2 percent statewide figure reflects that. The 0.8 point gain this year is the kind of movement that only becomes meaningful if it repeats. One year up does not fix a number that low, and the state knows it.

The district framework is the genuinely new thing. Rating districts rather than only campuses is a shift in how Nevada talks about accountability. Until now, a parent could see that a specific elementary school held four stars but had no official state measure of how the district running it was performing overall. Now there is one, on three levels, and every district in the state including Clark County sits inside it.

The districts that landed at the top in the first run are worth understanding rather than just listing. Eureka County is one of the smallest districts in the state, Carson City is a compact urban district, and the State Public Charter School Authority is not a geographic district at all. It oversees charter schools spread across Nevada. Comparing any of those directly to a district the size of Clark County is closer to apples and oranges than a leaderboard suggests, which is exactly the kind of thing a first-year framework has to work out.

One more piece of background matters for Las Vegas families. Enrollment across Clark County has been falling for several years, and that has driven real budget changes on campuses this fall. Staffing, program offerings, and class composition all move when enrollment moves, and all of those eventually touch the indicators that build a star rating. We covered the enrollment and budget side of that story separately, and it is the other half of the picture behind any rating change you see this year.

What Happens Next

The immediate next step is local. Individual school ratings get posted and discussed at the campus level, and schools that moved in either direction usually address it with families at the start of the year. If your child's school changed stars, the principal is the person who can tell you why, and that explanation is often more useful than the rating.

The district framework results will get worked through at the board and state level over the coming months. Because this is the framework's first run, expect some argument about the indicators themselves and how they are weighted. First-year accountability measures almost always get adjusted after everyone sees what they actually produce. Watch for that conversation rather than treating year one as settled.

The next statewide data release comes a year from now, and that is when the reading number becomes a story instead of a data point. If reading slips again, Nevada has a two-year trend. If it recovers, this year reads as noise. Math faces the same test in reverse. One up year is encouraging. Two would be a pattern.

In Clark County specifically, the thing to watch is whether individual campuses hold their ratings while the statewide pool of five-star schools shrinks. We covered the Clark County share of schools that held their star ratings in a separate piece, and that is the local counterweight to the statewide numbers here.

There is also a hopeful version of this story that does not make headlines. Individual Clark County campuses have jumped multiple stars in short stretches when a staff and a community got after it, and one local elementary school moved from one star to five in two years. Statewide averages drift slowly. Single schools can move fast.

Ryan's Take

I get asked about school ratings constantly, and my answer is always the same. I will not tell you which neighborhood to buy in based on a star rating, and you should be skeptical of anyone who does. What I will tell you is where the real data lives and how to read it, because that is a question with an actual answer.

Go to the Nevada Report Card. Look up the specific schools your address zones to, look at the star rating, and then look at the three prior years. A school on the way up and a school on the way down can sit at the same rating in the same year, and the number by itself hides that completely. Then look at proficiency in reading and math separately, since the statewide gap between those two is large enough that an average blurs real information.

The other thing I would say is this. A star rating measures a set of test-based indicators. It does not measure whether your kid will be happy there, whether the commute works, whether the arts program is any good, or whether the teacher your child gets in third grade is going to change everything. Those things matter enormously and none of them show up in the state file. Use the ratings as one input, walk the campus, and talk to parents who actually go there.

What You Can Do

Start with the primary source. The Nevada Report Card at nevadareportcard.nv.gov is the state's official public accountability site, and it holds star ratings, proficiency figures, enrollment, and multi-year history for every rated school in Nevada. It is free, it requires no account, and it is the same data every news story is summarizing. Search by school name or by district and pull the report for the campus you care about.

Next, confirm your actual zoning. In Clark County, the school you assume you are zoned to and the school you are actually zoned to are not always the same, especially near boundary streets and in newer developments where boundaries have shifted. The Clark County School District website has a school locator tool where you enter an address and get the assigned elementary, middle, and high school. Do that before you draw any conclusion from a rating.

Pull more than one year. This is the single most useful habit, and almost nobody does it. A school sitting at three stars today after two years at four is telling you something different than a school sitting at three stars after two years at two. Both look identical on a real estate listing site. Only the multi-year view on the state site separates them, and it takes an extra thirty seconds.

Read the subscores, not just the star. The state report breaks out reading and math proficiency, academic growth, and other indicators depending on grade level. A campus can be strong in growth and weaker in raw proficiency, which often happens at schools serving students who arrive behind and move quickly. That is genuinely useful information for a parent, and it is completely invisible in the single star.

Then go look. Call the front office and ask about a tour. Ask the principal what changed if the rating moved. Ask about magnet and program options, since Clark County has a large set of them and program choice can matter more than a zoned campus rating for a specific kid. If you want to hear the district-level conversation directly, CCSD board meetings are public and trustees hold community meetings throughout the year.
