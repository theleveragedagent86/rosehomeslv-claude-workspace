CCSD Sets a 2031 Goal to Move More Students With IEPs Into General Education Classrooms

Clark County School District trustees voted 7-0 on September 10, 2026 to approve a report on pre-kindergarten expansion and inclusive practices, and inside that report sits a target that will reshape how special education is delivered on Clark County campuses. The board set a goal of raising the share of students with an Individualized Education Program who spend at least 80 percent of the school day in general education classrooms from 46.3 percent to 60 percent by 2031.

That one number is the story. Today, a little under half of Clark County students with an IEP spend most of their day in a general education classroom. The board wants three out of five students to be there five years from now. That is a change to schedules, staffing, training, and classroom setup at hundreds of schools, and most families have not heard a word about it yet.

We covered the pre-K side of this same board item in CCSD Tops 14,000 Free Pre-K Seats and Just Landed 1,700 More. This piece is about the part of the report nobody covered, the 2031 inclusion target and what it actually changes on campus.

What Happened at the September 10 Board Meeting

The Clark County School District Board of Trustees met on September 10, 2026. Trustees voted 7-0 to approve a report covering two connected subjects, pre-kindergarten expansion and inclusive practices. The report noted that 1,700 pre-K seats were added for the 2026-27 school year through Nevada Ready! funding, which is the state program that pays for free pre-K seats across Nevada.

The inclusive practices half of the report is where the 2031 goal lives. The board set a target of moving the share of students with IEPs who are in general education classrooms at least 80 percent of the school day from the current 46.3 percent up to 60 percent by 2031. That is a jump of about 13.7 percentage points over five years. The goal came out of the board's own action on this report, so it is a district commitment rather than a state mandate or a court order.

Several other items rode the same 7-0 consent agenda that night, and each one connects back to the same set of students. Trustees approved a three-year partnership between CCSD and Special Olympics Nevada. They approved an agreement tied to the UNLV Autism Leadership Cohort, which is a university program that trains educators to work with students with autism. They approved a batch of new dual credit courses, the kind that let high school students earn college credit while still in high school. And they approved purchasing awards totaling $9,568,172.84.

None of those votes were close. All of it moved 7-0, which tells you the board treated this as settled business rather than a debate. That is worth understanding, because consent agenda items get almost no public discussion. A policy goal that touches every campus in the fifth largest school district in the country passed in the portion of the meeting most people tune out.

How the 80 Percent Measure Actually Works

The 46.3 percent and 60 percent figures are not a measure of how many students receive special education services. They are a measure of where those students spend their time. A student counts in this category when they are inside a general education classroom for at least 80 percent of the school day. Pull the math out and that is roughly the equivalent of one class period or less spent somewhere else on a typical schedule.

That matters because the measure can move in more than one way. A student who goes from a separate classroom to a general education classroom with a co-teacher counts. So does a student whose pull-out services get delivered inside the classroom instead of down the hall. So does a student whose schedule gets rebuilt so their support period lands during an elective rather than during core instruction. Some of the change is about placement and some of it is about scheduling.

It also means the number alone does not tell you whether a student is well served. A student sitting in a general education classroom without the supports written into their IEP is counted the same as a student thriving in a co-taught room. That is the real risk with any percentage goal, and it is the question parents should ask at their own school. Moving the number is easy on paper. Moving it while services stay intact is the hard part, and it is the only version worth doing.

Reading the number the other direction is useful too. At 46.3 percent, slightly more than half of Clark County students with an IEP currently spend more than a fifth of the day outside a general education classroom. For some of those students, that is exactly the right plan, written by a team that knows them. The district is not saying otherwise. It is saying the balance across 300 plus campuses is further toward separate settings than it should be, and it intends to shift that balance over five years.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Start with what an IEP is, because the term gets thrown around and a lot of families outside the system have never had it explained. An Individualized Education Program is a written plan required under federal law for a student who qualifies for special education services. It lists the student's current skill levels, the annual goals the team sets, the services the school will provide, and the supports the student gets in class. Parents are part of the team that writes it. The plan gets reviewed at least once a year.

The second term you need is least restrictive environment, often shortened to LRE. Federal law says students with disabilities should be educated alongside students without disabilities to the greatest extent appropriate for that student. A separate classroom or a separate school is allowed when a student's needs require it, but it is supposed to be the exception rather than the starting point. The 80 percent measure the board is tracking is the standard federal way of counting how many students are meeting that expectation.

So what changes for families? If you have a student with an IEP, the district is signaling that the default setting over the next five years leans toward the general education classroom with supports brought to the student, rather than the student being pulled out to a separate room. Your IEP team still decides what is right for your child. The law does not let a district set a number and then force every student into it. But the district's stated direction does shape what options get offered first at the table.

If you have a student without an IEP, the change shows up as more adults in the room. Meeting a goal like this usually means more co-teaching, where a general education teacher and a special education teacher share a classroom, plus more paraprofessional support and more training time for staff. Done well, that is a classroom with more help available for everyone in it. Done without the staffing and training behind it, it is a general education teacher with a longer list of needs and the same number of hours.

For families shopping for a home in Clark County, this matters in a practical way. Parents of students with disabilities often pick a neighborhood around a single school that has the program their child needs. If services shift toward being available on more campuses, that search gets less restrictive over time. That is a slow change, not an overnight one, but it is the direction the board just voted for.

Background and History

The legal backbone here is the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act, the federal law that guarantees students with disabilities a free appropriate public education. It has required the least restrictive environment standard for decades. Every state reports inclusion rates to the federal government, and the 80 percent threshold is one of the standard categories in that reporting. That is why the board picked that specific measure. It is already tracked, so progress can be checked year over year without inventing a new metric.

Clark County is the largest school district in Nevada and one of the largest in the country, with more than 300 schools. A figure like 46.3 percent covers an enormous range of campuses. Some Clark County schools already run strong inclusive models. Others lean more on separate settings, often for reasons that have to do with building layout, staffing history, or which specialized programs happen to be housed there. Moving a countywide average takes work at the buildings that are furthest from the target, not at the ones already doing it.

The staffing picture is the part nobody can wish away. Special education teachers and licensed support staff have been among the hardest roles for Nevada districts to fill for years. Inclusion done properly is staff intensive in a different way than a separate classroom, because supports travel to the student across multiple rooms and periods. That is why the same night's votes on the UNLV Autism Leadership Cohort and the Special Olympics Nevada partnership belong in the same conversation as the target. Both are pipeline and capacity moves.

Budget is the other piece of background. Clark County schools have been working through a tight funding stretch, and every new commitment lands on top of that. A five-year goal set in 2026 has to survive five budget cycles, several rounds of enrollment changes, and whatever the Legislature does in between. Goals that get funded move. Goals that get announced and then left alone tend to sit where they started.

What Happens Next

The immediate next step is implementation planning at the school level. A district goal like this typically turns into building level targets, professional development calendars, and changes to how master schedules are built so that co-taught sections exist in the first place. Most of that work happens quietly over the summer and shows up to families as a schedule that looks different in August.

Watch the annual reporting. The 46.3 percent figure is a baseline, which means there should be an updated number each year between now and 2031. If the share climbs a couple of points a year, the goal is realistic. If it sits flat for two years, the district is going to have to say why, and that is the moment parents should be paying attention at a board meeting.

The Special Olympics Nevada partnership runs three years, so it will come back to the board for renewal inside this window. The UNLV Autism Leadership Cohort agreement should produce trained staff on a similar timeline. Those are both checkpoints where trustees will have to look at whether the support side is keeping pace with the target.

Families can also watch their own child's annual IEP meeting for the practical version of this. If the conversation at your table starts including more general education placement options with supports attached, that is the policy arriving in the room. You are allowed to ask what supports come with any proposed change, in writing, before you agree to it.

One more thing to watch is whether the district reports the goal in public view or buries it in an annual data packet. The cleanest version would be a short update to trustees each year showing the current percentage, the schools that moved the most, and the staffing added to make it happen. Parents should not have to file a records request to find out whether a five-year commitment is on track. Ask for that update by name at a board meeting and it is harder to skip.

Ryan's Take

I work with families every week who pick a house around a school. For parents of a student with an IEP, that search is tighter than most people realize, because the question is not just whether a school is good. It is whether the specific services their child needs are actually offered on that campus. I have watched buyers rule out entire zip codes over it.

So a district goal that spreads services across more campuses is meaningful to how people shop for a home here, even though it will never show up in a market report. If it works, a family has more neighborhoods to choose from in 2031 than they do today. That is real. I would also say plainly that a target is not a result. Five years is long enough for a goal to quietly fade, and the honest way to judge this one is to look at the number every year rather than at the announcement. I will keep an eye on it.

What You Can Do

If your child has an IEP, ask your case manager how your school is tracking on this goal and what changes are planned for next year. You are entitled to understand the placement options on the table and the supports attached to each one. Bring questions in writing to your annual meeting, and ask for the answers in writing too. That record helps if anything gets disputed later.

If you want to weigh in at the district level, Clark County School District board meetings include public comment, and the agendas are posted in advance on the district website. Consent agenda items like this one are exactly where public comment does the most good, because they otherwise pass with no discussion at all. Trustees also hold community meetings around the valley where you can ask questions in a smaller room.

Two trustees are hosting one of those community meetings on September 28 in North Las Vegas, and it is free to attend. If you are buying or selling a home and school services are part of your decision, call the school directly and ask what programs are housed on that campus this year. Zone maps and rating sites will not tell you that.
