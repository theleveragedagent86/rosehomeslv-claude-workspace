Two CCSD Trustees Take Questions in North Las Vegas on September 28

Two Clark County School District trustees, Tameka Henry and Brenda Zamora, are holding an open community meeting on Monday, September 28, from 5:30 p.m. to 6:45 p.m. at Jo Mackey iLead Academy, 2726 Englestad St. in North Las Vegas. It is free, it is open to families, students, staff and anybody who lives in the area, and the district asks that you RSVP through an online form first.

If you have been wondering what happened to your school's budget this fall, this is the kind of meeting where you can ask a trustee directly and get a plain answer. CCSD has cut roughly $100 million across two rounds this year, including $51.6 million this fall, and North Las Vegas campuses felt that the same way every other part of the valley did. A 75 minute meeting inside a neighborhood school is a much easier place to raise a question than a long board agenda downtown.

What Is Happening and Where

The Clark County School District announced the meeting on September 18. Trustees Tameka Henry and Brenda Zamora are co-hosting what the district calls a Community Engagement Meeting. The date is Monday, September 28. The time is 5:30 p.m. to 6:45 p.m. The location is Jo Mackey iLead Academy at 2726 Englestad St., North Las Vegas, which sits just off of Carey Avenue in the older part of the city near Lake Mead Boulevard.

The district describes the meeting as open to community members, families, students and staff. That wording matters. It is not a parent only event and you do not have to have a child enrolled at Jo Mackey to walk in. If you rent an apartment three blocks away and you care about the elementary school your future kids would attend, you are welcome. If you are a retiree whose property taxes help fund the district, you are welcome. If you are a CCSD employee who wants to say something about staffing, you are welcome.

CCSD is asking attendees to RSVP through an online form linked from the district newsroom post announcing the meeting. RSVP is requested rather than required, and the practical reason is simple. Hosts need to know whether to set out 30 chairs or 130, and whether the meeting fits in a library, a multipurpose room or the cafeteria. If you plan to come, fill out the form. If you decide to come at the last minute and never filled it out, go anyway.

There is no cost to attend. There is no ticket. Parking at a CCSD elementary campus in the evening is generally straightforward, and the meeting is short enough that it fits between dinner and bedtime for a family with young kids. Seventy five minutes is a real constraint, though, so the people who get the most out of a meeting like this are the ones who walk in knowing what they want to ask.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

School district decisions reach into almost every corner of daily life in Clark County, and most of those decisions get made in rooms that ordinary residents never set foot in. A regular CCSD board meeting is a long, formal affair with a printed agenda, staff presentations, and a public comment process that runs on a clock. It works, and it is the place where binding votes happen. But it is not built for a back and forth conversation.

A community engagement meeting is built for exactly that. The trustee is the person your neighborhood elects to represent it on the board, and the whole point of an evening like this is to hear from the people in the district they serve without the machinery of a formal agenda in the way. You can ask a follow up question. You can describe what you are actually seeing at your kid's campus. You can ask what a number in a budget document means in practice.

For North Las Vegas specifically, the timing lands well. This fall's cuts touched school level budgets across the valley, and families have been comparing notes about class sizes, support staff, and which programs survived. Those are fair questions to bring to a trustee. So are questions about school safety, bus routes, start times, facility repairs, and how a campus gets picked for a rebuild or a modernization project.

There is also a housing angle that I do not think gets talked about enough. Buyers in Clark County ask me about schools constantly. They pull star ratings, they look at feeder patterns, they ask which elementary sends kids to which middle school. What they almost never do is look at whether the community around a school shows up. Campuses with engaged families tend to hold onto good principals and good teachers longer, and that shows up in the data eventually. Attendance at a meeting like this is a small piece of that.

Background and History

The Clark County School District is one of the largest school districts in the country, with more than 300 schools spread across an enormous geographic area. Trustees represent districts, not individual campuses, and a single trustee district can cover dozens of schools and a very wide mix of neighborhoods. That scale is exactly why the district runs community engagement meetings. Without them, a trustee's contact with constituents narrows down to email and the public comment microphone.

The budget backdrop this fall is real and it is why parents have questions. According to News 3 Las Vegas, CCSD is absorbing $51.6 million in cuts this fall on top of roughly $50 million in the spring, which puts the district near $100 million in reductions across two rounds. The same reporting described roughly $85 million that would have gone to CCSD classrooms last fiscal year instead staying in Nevada's Education Stabilization Account, the state's education savings fund, which the Governor's Office of Finance expects to grow past $800 million.

That is a statewide mechanic, not a Clark County one. Of Nevada's 17 school districts, only two are projected not to lose some portion of the money the state had approved. So a parent in Elko and a parent in North Las Vegas are looking at versions of the same problem. What differs is scale. When you are the district serving the majority of the state's students, a percentage point moves tens of millions of dollars and touches every campus.

It is also worth remembering how CCSD school budgets are built, because it explains why two campuses in the same city can feel a cut very differently. A large share of a school's money follows its students, so enrollment is the single biggest lever. A campus that loses students loses funding tied to those students, and a campus that grows picks some up. Layer a district wide reduction on top of that and you get uneven results across the valley. That is the reason a parent at one school reports losing a position while a parent a few miles away says nothing changed, and both of them are telling the truth.

North Las Vegas has been through a lot of change in the last two decades. Enrollment patterns shifted as new housing went up on the north and west edges of the valley while the older established neighborhoods near Lake Mead Boulevard and Carey Avenue stayed built out. Those older areas have solid housing stock, mature trees, and schools that have been serving the same streets for generations. They also compete for district attention against fast growing areas that are adding students every year. That tension is an ordinary part of running a district this size, and it is a fair thing to ask a trustee about.

Jo Mackey itself has an interesting recent history as a host site. It operates as an iLead Academy, a magnet style program model, and it sits in one of the older established residential pockets of North Las Vegas. Hosting a community meeting at a neighborhood campus rather than at a district building is a deliberate choice. It puts the conversation inside the building people are asking about.

How Public Input Works at CCSD

It helps to know the difference between the two kinds of rooms, because people sometimes show up to one expecting the other.

A community engagement meeting like the September 28 event is informal. There is no vote, no motion, and no official record of who said what. Trustees usually give a short update and then open the floor. The format is closer to a neighborhood meeting than a hearing. Nothing gets decided in the room, but what trustees hear can absolutely shape how they ask questions of district staff later.

A regular Board of School Trustees meeting is the formal channel. Those meetings are noticed in advance under Nevada's open meeting law, with an agenda posted ahead of time listing every item the board can legally act on. Public comment is part of those meetings, and the board president sets the ground rules for it, including how long each speaker gets and whether comment is taken at the start, the end, or both. Sign up procedures and time limits are published with the agenda, so check the current agenda before you go rather than relying on what somebody told you last year.

Two practical notes. First, comment at a formal board meeting is a statement, not a conversation. Trustees generally do not debate speakers from the dais, so if you want an actual answer to a question, a community meeting is the better venue. Second, you can always contact a trustee's office directly by email or phone between meetings. That is the least dramatic option and often the most effective one.

One more thing worth understanding is the difference between what a trustee controls and what they do not. Trustees set district policy, approve the budget, and hire and evaluate the superintendent. They do not run individual campuses day to day, and they do not set state funding levels. If your question is about a classroom assignment or a specific staff member, the principal is usually the right first stop. If your question is about a policy, a budget priority, or how the district sets its direction, that is squarely trustee territory. Knowing which bucket your question falls into is the fastest way to get a real answer instead of a referral.

What Happens Next

The immediate next step is the meeting itself on Monday, September 28, from 5:30 p.m. to 6:45 p.m. at Jo Mackey iLead Academy. Fill out the RSVP form on the CCSD newsroom announcement if you intend to go. Between now and then, the useful work is figuring out what you actually want to ask.

After September 28, watch the regular board calendar. CCSD trustees meet on a published schedule, and budget items, enrollment reports and facility decisions show up on those agendas throughout the fall. If something you hear at the community meeting matters to you, the board agenda is where you track whether it turns into a decision. Agendas and supporting documents are posted publicly in advance.

Also watch the state picture. The Education Stabilization Account balance and how much of the approved funding actually reaches districts are decisions made at the state level, not by CCSD trustees. A trustee can tell you how a cut lands on a campus. What happens to the statewide fund is a separate track, and it is worth following on its own if school funding is the thing you care most about.

Ryan's Take

I sell homes in Clark County for a living, and school questions come up in almost every buyer conversation I have. People want to know the rating, the feeder pattern, and whether the middle school is any good. Those are reasonable questions. But the answers to them move slowly, and they move partly because of decisions made in rooms most buyers never think about.

Here is what I tell clients about North Las Vegas. It is one of the parts of the valley where the gap between reputation and reality is widest. There are established neighborhoods with real value, solid inventory at price points that are getting hard to find elsewhere in the valley, and a city that has been investing in itself. Schools are a genuine part of that conversation and they should be. A community where families turn out for a Monday evening meeting at an elementary school is a community that is paying attention to itself, and over a long enough horizon that shows up in property values.

So my honest advice is not complicated. Go if you can. Bring one specific question rather than a general complaint. And if you are thinking about buying in North Las Vegas and want a read on how a particular school zone actually looks right now, ask me and I will give you the unvarnished version.

What You Can Do

Start with the RSVP. The CCSD newsroom post announcing the September 28 meeting links to the online form, and it takes under a minute. Put the date on your calendar with the address, 2726 Englestad St., North Las Vegas, and plan to arrive a few minutes early so you are seated when it starts at 5:30 p.m.

Then write down one question. Seventy five minutes split among a room full of people is not a lot of time, and the questions that get useful answers are the specific ones. Reasonable examples for this fall: how were the $51.6 million in reductions distributed across campuses, what happened to a particular support position at your school, how does a campus get on the modernization list, what the plan is for a specific bus route, or how a program at Jo Mackey is funded going forward. Bring numbers if you have them.

If you cannot make it on September 28, you still have options. Email your trustee's office directly. Watch the board agendas as they are posted and submit comment at a regular meeting. Show up to your school's organizational team or parent group, which is where a lot of campus level decisions actually get shaped. And tell a neighbor about the meeting, because turnout at these events is usually the difference between a conversation that gets remembered and one that does not.
