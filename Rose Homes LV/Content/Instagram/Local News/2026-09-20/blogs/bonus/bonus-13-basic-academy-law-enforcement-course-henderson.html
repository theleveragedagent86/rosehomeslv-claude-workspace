A New Law Enforcement Course Is Drawing Students at Basic Academy in Henderson

Basic Academy of International Studies in Henderson launched a new Law Enforcement course this school year, and students are signing up for it. The class covers state laws, police radio codes, and the real career paths that open up after graduation. It is taught by a retired City of Henderson police captain who walked the same hallways as a Basic student himself.

That last detail is the part people keep repeating when they talk about this class. The teacher is not a guest speaker who visits once a semester. He is a former Henderson police captain who came back to the school he graduated from to teach teenagers what the job actually looks like. Clark County School District shared the story on September 17, 2026.

If you are a Henderson family trying to figure out which high school your kid should attend, this is exactly the kind of thing worth paying attention to. Career and technical education programs are one of the quiet factors that shape where families want to live, and most people do not think about them until they are already house hunting.

What Happened

Basic Academy of International Studies added a Law Enforcement course to its catalog for the 2026-27 school year. According to Clark County School District, the class walks students through Nevada state laws, the radio codes police officers use on the job, and the range of careers available inside the field. It is a survey course in the best sense, meaning it shows students the whole landscape instead of just one narrow slice of it.

The class is taught by a retired City of Henderson police captain. He graduated from Basic himself, which means he is teaching in the same building where he sat as a student. That is not a small thing for the kids in the room. A teacher who can point at a hallway and say he used to walk it every day carries a kind of credibility that a textbook does not.

The course is not all lecture. Students get K9 demonstrations, where working police dogs and their handlers come to campus and show what the animals are trained to do. There are also guest speakers pulled from the field, meaning working professionals who come in and talk about what their days actually look like. Those sessions are the part students tend to remember years later.

Clark County School District described the class as grabbing student interest, and the enrollment reflects that. Elective courses live and die by word of mouth inside a school. A class that students talk about in the lunchroom fills up. A class nobody mentions quietly disappears from the catalog after a year or two. This one is in the first category.

The subject matter itself is broader than most people expect. Learning police radio codes is the piece that sounds fun to a fifteen year old, but the state law portion is the part that sticks. Understanding how Nevada statutes are written, what they do and do not cover, and how the legal process moves from a call to a courtroom is genuinely useful knowledge, whether a student ends up in the field or not. A class that teaches teenagers how the law actually works in their own state is doing something worthwhile even for the students who go a different direction.

Basic Academy of International Studies sits in Henderson and serves families across a wide swath of the city. The school already carries a reputation built on its international studies focus, and adding a law enforcement pathway broadens what it can offer to students whose interests run in a different direction. Not every kid wants to study international relations. Some want to know what it takes to wear a badge.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Here is the thing most people do not realize until they are deep into a home search. In Clark County, the address you buy determines the high school your kid is zoned for. And high schools in this valley are not interchangeable. They have different programs, different magnet tracks, different career and technical education pathways, and different cultures. A family that wants a law enforcement pathway, a nursing pathway, or an aviation pathway is making a housing decision as much as a school decision.

Career and technical education, usually shortened to CTE, is one of the most underrated parts of the CCSD system. These are courses that connect classroom work to actual jobs. Some of them lead to industry certifications students can carry into the workforce the week after graduation. Others feed directly into community college programs or apprenticeships. For a family that is not certain their kid is headed straight to a four year university, a strong CTE program is worth real money.

Henderson families in particular tend to shop schools harder than buyers in some other parts of the valley. Henderson has a deep bench of well regarded campuses, and parents compare them. When one of those campuses adds a program that nothing else nearby offers, it moves the needle. It gives a family one more reason to look at homes on that side of town instead of another.

There is also the community angle. A course like this puts high school students in the same room as working professionals from the police field. Those conversations happen face to face, at a normal volume, in a setting where a teenager can ask a question without pressure. That kind of contact between young people and public safety professionals is good for a city, and it costs almost nothing to create.

For homeowners who do not have kids in school, this still matters in an indirect way. School programs shape the reputation of a school, and school reputation shapes what buyers are willing to pay for a home in that attendance zone. You do not have to be a parent to benefit from living in a zone people want.

There is a workforce piece too. Southern Nevada needs people in public safety roles, and those jobs are filled by people who already live here. A student who discovers the field in eleventh grade at a Henderson high school is far more likely to build a career in Henderson than a student who never heard about it until after graduation. Local programs create local hires, and local hires buy local homes.

Renters feel this as well. Plenty of Henderson families rent rather than own, and zoning applies the same way. If you are signing a lease and you have a high schooler, the address on that lease decides which campus and which programs your student can access for the next year. That is a detail worth checking before you sign, not after you move the furniture in.

Background and History

Clark County School District has been building out career and technical education for years. The district runs dedicated CTE academies as well as CTE programs housed inside comprehensive high schools. The dedicated academies draw students from across the valley and often require an application. The programs inside neighborhood high schools are usually open to students zoned for that campus, which is why the zoned school matters so much.

Basic Academy of International Studies is the newer name for a campus Henderson residents have known for generations. The international studies focus gave the school a distinct identity, and the campus has carried a solid reputation in the city for a long time. Adding a law enforcement course expands the menu without diluting what the school already does well.

The staffing angle here is worth a moment. Districts across the country have struggled to find qualified instructors for career and technical courses, because the people who know the work best are usually still doing the work. Hiring a retired police captain is how this gets solved. A professional finishes one career, has the knowledge and the patience to teach, and steps into a classroom. Everybody wins when that pipeline works.

It also helps that this particular instructor is a Basic graduate. Alumni who come back to teach bring institutional memory and a genuine stake in the school's outcome. They are not passing through. Students notice the difference, and so do parents.

The broader trend behind all of this is a shift in how schools talk about life after graduation. For a long stretch, the message to students was essentially one path, which was a four year degree. That has changed. Districts now talk openly about certifications, apprenticeships, trade programs, and public service careers alongside college. A law enforcement elective fits squarely in that newer conversation, and it reflects where CCSD has been heading for several years.

What Happens Next

The immediate thing to watch is enrollment. If the Law Enforcement course keeps filling, Basic has a case for expanding it into a multi year sequence instead of a single elective. That is how CTE pathways usually grow at CCSD campuses. One popular class becomes an introductory course, then a second level course gets added, and eventually the school has a full pathway with certifications attached to it.

Course registration for the next school year typically opens in the spring, which means families who want this class for their student should be watching the Basic Academy course catalog in the coming months. Counselors are the right people to ask. They know which classes have seats, which ones have prerequisites, and which ones fill within days of registration opening.

For families considering a move into the Basic attendance zone, the timing question is the practical one. Zoning is tied to your address, and CCSD verifies residency. If the program is a reason you are moving, you want the move done before registration, not after. That is a conversation worth having early, not in July.

The other thing to watch is whether other Henderson and Las Vegas campuses follow. When one school runs a popular elective and the word gets around, other principals notice. If a second or third campus adds a similar course in the next year or two, that tells you the district sees real demand. If Basic stays the only one, then the program stays a genuine differentiator for that attendance zone, which matters for families weighing where to buy.

Ryan's Take

I talk to a lot of Henderson buyers, and school zoning comes up in almost every conversation with a family. What surprises people is how specific the questions get once they start digging. It is rarely just "is it a good school." It is "does that campus have the program my kid actually cares about." A teenager who wants to work in public safety is not going to be inspired by a school's debate team. They want the class with the K9 demo.

That is why stories like this one matter to me professionally. A new CTE course does not show up on a star rating chart or a test score report. It does not get factored into the automated school scores that show up next to listings online. But for the right family, it is the single most important thing about that school. If you are only looking at the numbers attached to a listing, you are missing the part that will actually determine whether your kid likes going to school every day.

My advice to Henderson families has been the same for years. Before you fall in love with a house, call the high school. Ask what programs they run, ask which ones have open seats, and ask what the pathway looks like over four years. It takes twenty minutes, and it can change which neighborhoods you tour.

I will also say this plainly. A hired instructor who spent a career in the field and then came back to his own high school is the kind of detail that tells you something about a campus. It says the school is willing to go find the right person instead of filling a slot. That is not something you can measure, but it is real, and families can feel it when they walk a campus and talk to staff.

What You Can Do

If you have a student at Basic Academy of International Studies and the Law Enforcement course sounds like a fit, start with the school counseling office. Counselors can tell you whether the class has space, what grade levels it is open to, and whether anything needs to be taken first. They can also tell you what the plan is for expanding it, if there is one.

If you do not have a student at Basic and you want to know what your own zoned high school offers, CCSD publishes school information and course catalogs online, and every campus has a counseling office that will answer a phone call from a parent. Ask about career and technical education by name. Ask which pathways lead to a certification. Those are the questions that get you real answers.

And if you are house hunting in Henderson right now, do the school research before you narrow your list of neighborhoods, not after. Zoning boundaries do not always follow the lines people assume they follow. Two homes a few blocks apart can feed into different campuses. Verify the zone for the specific address, every time, before you write an offer.

Parents who want to support programs like this one have options beyond enrollment. Schools running career courses are always looking for professionals willing to come speak to a class for an hour. If you work in a field a teenager might want to learn about, call the counseling office and offer. A single guest speaker can change how a student thinks about the next ten years of their life, and it costs you one lunch break.
