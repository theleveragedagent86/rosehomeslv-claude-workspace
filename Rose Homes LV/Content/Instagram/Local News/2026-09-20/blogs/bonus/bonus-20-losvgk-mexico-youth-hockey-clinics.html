Golden Knights Youth Coaches Ran Clinics for About 300 Kids, and It Is the Same Staff That Teaches Vegas Kids to Skate

The Vegas Golden Knights ran their second LosVGK en Mexico trip from September 6 to September 11, 2026, putting on a week of on ice clinics and fan activations with about 300 participants. Two youth clubs took part, Club Osos Coyoacan and Club Buffalos Metepec. The reason it matters to a parent in Clark County is simple. The coaches who ran those drills are the same coaching staff that runs learn to skate and youth hockey programming here at home.

Brian Salcido, the team's Director of Coaching and Instructor Programming, led the group. Team alum Deryk Engelland went along, and so did members of the Knights Guard. That is the same office, the same curriculum, and in some cases the same people you would meet if you walked your six year old into a beginner skate class in the valley this fall.

So while the headline is about a trip, the story underneath is about how this organization builds a youth program. It sends its coaching staff out to teach. It puts alumni on the ice with beginners instead of just at autograph tables. And it treats a week of clinics as real work, not a photo opportunity. Parents in Las Vegas, Henderson, and North Las Vegas who are trying to figure out whether hockey is a realistic sport for their kid should pay attention to that, because it tells you a lot about what happens when you sign up locally.

What Happened

The Golden Knights held the second edition of LosVGK en Mexico over six days, September 6 through September 11, 2026. The program mixed on ice clinics with fan activations off the ice. About 300 people took part across the week, according to the team.

Two youth hockey clubs were involved. Club Osos Coyoacan and Club Buffalos Metepec both sent players to skate in the sessions. These are established youth clubs with their own rosters, coaches, and schedules, so the clinics were not a first introduction to the sport for every kid on the ice. Some were beginners. Others already play organized hockey and were getting instruction from a different set of coaches than the ones they see every week.

Brian Salcido ran the coaching side. His title with the Golden Knights is Director of Coaching and Instructor Programming, which means his day job is building and overseeing the teaching curriculum the team uses across its youth and beginner programs. He is not a guest speaker. He is the person responsible for how the organization teaches skating and skills.

Deryk Engelland joined the trip as a team alum. Engelland played in the NHL and spent years with the Golden Knights, and he has stayed close to the organization since he stopped playing. Members of the Knights Guard, the team's costumed ambassador group, also took part in the week. Between the coaching staff, the alum, and the Knights Guard, the trip had both the instructional piece and the fan facing piece covered.

The team framed the week as growing the game. That is the plain description of what a clinic does. You put kids on the ice, you teach them to fall down and get back up, you teach them edges and stops and how to hold a stick, and some of them come back next week. A week of clinics with 300 participants is not going to fill a rink by itself, but it plants a flag and builds a relationship with clubs that already exist.

This was the second year of the program, which is worth noting. A one time visit is a headline. A second year is the start of something the organization intends to keep doing, and repeat programming is how youth sports actually grow. The kids who skated in the first edition had a year to keep playing before this one came around.

It is also worth being clear about what a clinic is and is not. Six days of ice time does not turn a beginner into a player. What it does is give a kid a correct first experience, which is the single biggest predictor of whether they stick with any sport. Bad first lessons teach kids that they are bad at something. Good first lessons teach them that the thing is learnable. Coaches who do this professionally know the difference, and that is exactly why the organization sent its coaching director instead of a group of volunteers.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Here is the part that actually lands for a Clark County family. The Golden Knights employ a full time director whose entire job is coaching and instructor programming. That is a real position with a real budget, and it exists because the organization decided that teaching kids to play is a core function rather than a side project. When the team sends that person and his staff on the road for a week, it is using the same teaching system it uses at home.

For parents, the practical question is usually not about outreach trips. It is about cost, time, and whether the program is any good. Hockey has a reputation for being expensive, and in a lot of markets that reputation is fair. Gear adds up, ice time is costly, and travel teams eat weekends. What changes the math in Las Vegas is that the NHL team here runs beginner programming directly, which tends to keep entry level costs lower and quality higher than a market where every option is a private club.

The valley also has more ice than people outside hockey assume. City National Arena in Summerlin is the Golden Knights practice facility and the hub for a lot of the team's youth and beginner programming. Lifeguard Arena in Henderson serves the Henderson Silver Knights and hosts community skating as well. That gives families on both sides of the valley a realistic drive instead of one single rink that everyone has to fight over.

There is a neighborhood angle here too, and it is the one I hear most often from clients with kids in sports. Where you live determines which activities are actually sustainable. A twenty minute drive to a 6 a.m. practice is doable. A fifty minute drive twice a week is how families quit. When buyers tell me their kid plays hockey, or skates, or swims, the location of the facility becomes a real search criterion, right next to schools and commute.

The other reason this matters is what it says about the sport's staying power here. Las Vegas got an NHL team in 2017, and the honest question at the time was whether hockey would take root in a desert city or stay a downtown entertainment product. Nine years later the answer is visible in youth registration, rink usage, and the fact that the organization now exports its coaching program. That is not a tourist story. That is a local participation story.

One more thing parents ask about constantly is safety. Beginner hockey is heavily padded, heavily supervised, and slow. The contact people picture from watching an NHL game is not what a learn to skate class looks like. At the entry levels the sport is mostly skating, and skating is a skill that transfers to figure skating, to speed skating, and to simply being comfortable on the ice for the rest of your life. Even the kids who try it and move on to something else keep that.

Background and History

The Golden Knights built community programming into the franchise almost immediately. When an expansion team lands in a market with no hockey history, it has two choices. It can sell tickets to transplants who already like hockey, or it can grow its own fans from scratch. Vegas did both, and the second part is slower and less glamorous and matters more over twenty years.

Youth hockey in Southern Nevada existed before the NHL arrived, but it was small. The rinks that were here served a committed group of families, many of them transplants from colder states. What changed after 2017 was volume and visibility. Kids who had never seen the sport started asking about it, and the team had programming ready for them.

Deryk Engelland's involvement is a good example of how the organization uses its alumni. He was on the original roster, he lived in Las Vegas before the team existed, and he became one of the faces of the franchise's connection to the city. Having a former player at a beginner clinic changes the temperature of the room. Kids pay attention differently, and parents take the program more seriously.

The Knights Guard fills a different role. They are the fan facing side of the operation, the group that makes an event feel like an event rather than a practice. In an outreach week, that matters. A clinic that is only drills is a lesson. A clinic with a fan activation attached is a memory, and memories are what bring a kid back for a second season.

The first LosVGK en Mexico trip established the format. This year's version repeated it with two named clubs and a participant count in the hundreds. The pattern is the same one the team uses locally: bring the coaching staff, bring an alum, bring the fan experience, and run it with people who already teach for a living.

What Happens Next

The immediate next step for local families is the fall and winter programming calendar. Beginner skating and youth hockey sessions in the valley generally run in blocks through the school year, and registration for most of them opens ahead of each block rather than once a year. Anyone interested should check the Golden Knights website and the individual arena schedules directly, because session dates, age groups, and pricing change from block to block.

If the pattern from the first two years holds, expect the team to keep running the Mexico program in future seasons. Two editions in a row is a program, not a pilot. Whether it expands, adds clubs, or changes format is not something the team has announced, so treat that as an open question rather than a plan.

Locally, the thing to watch is ice capacity. Growth in youth participation eventually runs into a hard limit, which is how many hours of ice exist in the valley. Every new beginner class competes with adult leagues, figure skating, public sessions, and team practices for the same sheets. If registration keeps climbing, the conversation in a few years will be about building more rinks, and that is a development story with real implications for the neighborhoods near any proposed site.

The other thing to watch is what happens to the kids who came through the first wave of beginner programs after 2017. Those kids are teenagers now. Some of them are playing at levels that did not exist here a decade ago, and a handful will end up playing junior or college hockey. That is the real measure of whether a youth program worked, and Las Vegas is only just reaching the point where the answer is visible.

Ryan's Take

I pay attention to youth sports infrastructure because it is one of the quieter things that makes a neighborhood work for families. Schools get all the attention in a home search, and they should. But the second question, usually a few minutes later, is what my kid is going to do after school and on weekends, and how far I have to drive to make it happen.

Hockey in Las Vegas went from a niche activity to a normal option for a Clark County kid in under a decade, and that did not happen by accident. It happened because the organization put money and staff behind teaching beginners, and because the facilities are spread across the valley instead of concentrated in one spot. When I am working with a buyer whose kid skates, that changes which parts of Summerlin, Henderson, or the northwest actually make sense for them. It is the same calculation I run for a family with a travel soccer player or a competitive swimmer. Proximity to the facility is a real part of the value of a house, even though no appraisal will ever line item it.

I also think this is one of the few sports stories that is genuinely about community building rather than results. Nobody won anything in Mexico. Three hundred kids got a week of good coaching, and the organization that runs our local beginner programs got better at doing the thing it does. That is the same work that happens at a rink in Summerlin or Henderson on a Tuesday night, with nobody covering it.

What You Can Do

If your kid has been asking about hockey, the cheapest first step is a public skate session. Rent the skates, spend an hour on the ice, and see whether they want to come back. You do not need to buy a single piece of equipment to find that out, and it costs less than a movie.

If that goes well, look at the beginner and learn to skate programs run through the Golden Knights and the local arenas. Check the team's official website and the arena schedules for current session dates, age groups, and costs, since those change throughout the year. Many beginner programs include loaner gear for the first block, which removes the biggest up front barrier. Ask about that specifically when you call.

For families already in the sport, the Mexico clinics are a reminder that the coaching staff behind the local programs is the same group the organization sends out to represent it. If you have questions about curriculum, skill progressions, or which program level fits your kid, the people who built the system are reachable through the team's youth hockey pages. That is a better source than a message board.

And if the drive to the rink is the thing making your week miserable, that is worth saying out loud during a home search. It is a legitimate criterion, and it narrows a search faster than most people expect.
