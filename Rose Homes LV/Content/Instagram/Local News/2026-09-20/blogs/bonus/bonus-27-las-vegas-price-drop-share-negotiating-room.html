One in Five Sellers Nationally Cut Their Price. In Las Vegas It Is Almost Two in Five.

Redfin reported that 39.1 percent of Las Vegas listings had a price drop in August 2026, up 1.1 percentage points from August 2025. Over roughly the same stretch, Redfin's national reading showed 20.8 percent of U.S. listings with a price drop in the four weeks ending September 6, 2026, up from 19.8 percent a year earlier. If you are shopping for a home in Clark County right now, that gap is the single most useful number you can carry into a negotiation.

Here is the plain-English version. Nationally, about one seller in five has already lowered an asking price. In Las Vegas it is closer to two in five. Sellers here are adjusting at almost double the national share, and many of them are doing it before a buyer ever walks through the front door.

We covered the national side of this story in National Price Cuts Catch Up to Las Vegas, which looked at the country slowly moving toward the pattern Southern Nevada has been living with for a while. This piece is not about the headline. This piece is about what a buyer actually does with the 39.1 percent figure when it is time to write an offer.

What the Redfin Numbers Actually Say

Two separate Redfin readings sit behind this story, and they cover two different periods. That matters, so let us keep them apart.

The national figure comes from Redfin's housing market update covering the four weeks ending September 6, 2026. In that window, 20.8 percent of U.S. listings had a price drop, up from 19.8 percent in the same four-week period a year earlier. That is a four-week rolling reading, the kind Redfin publishes on a short cycle to catch what is happening week to week.

The Las Vegas figure comes from Redfin's Las Vegas market data and covers the full month of August 2026. In that month, 39.1 percent of local listings had a price drop, up 1.1 percentage points year over year. That is a monthly reading, not a four-week one. The two numbers are not measured over the identical calendar stretch, so treat the comparison as a fair general picture rather than a precise same-day matchup.

Now the distinction that trips up almost everybody. A price drop on a listing is not the same thing as a drop in sale prices. When Redfin counts a price drop, it is counting a seller lowering the number on the listing sheet. That seller may still end up selling for more than they paid, more than the neighbor did last year, or right at the neighborhood median. A 39.1 percent price-drop share tells you how many sellers revised their asking price. It does not tell you that home values in Clark County fell by any amount. Those are two separate measurements, and mixing them up is how people end up with a badly wrong idea of the market.

What the figure does tell you is something about seller behavior. A price drop is a seller publicly acknowledging that the first number did not bring the response they wanted. It is an admission, in writing, on a public listing, that the opening ask was above what the market was willing to pay. When nearly four in ten local sellers have made that admission in a single month, you are looking at a market where asking prices and buyer willingness are not lining up on the first try.

Redfin published the national figure in a September 10, 2026 update titled "High Costs Sideline Some Would-Be Homebuyers, Handing Upper Hand to Those Who Stay in the Market." The Las Vegas figure comes from Redfin's ongoing Las Vegas, NV housing market page. Both are linked at the bottom of this article.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Buyers

The useful thing about a price-drop share is that it is a rough map of where the conversation is happening. Flip 39.1 percent around and you get the other side of the picture. By Rose Homes LV calculation, if 39.1 percent of local listings had a price drop in August, then roughly 60.9 percent did not. That is not a small group. Six out of ten Clark County sellers held their number through the month.

So the first practical takeaway is not "everything is negotiable." It is "a large minority of sellers have already blinked, and the rest have not." Your job as a buyer is figuring out which pile a specific house is in. That is a property-level question, not a market-level one, and no headline percentage answers it for you.

For a Clark County family shopping in Spring Valley, Mountains Edge, Centennial Hills, or Green Valley, this changes the shape of a house hunt. In a market where almost nobody cuts, you move fast and you pay close to ask. In a market where a big share of sellers cut, patience becomes an actual strategy. A home that has been listed for a while, with one or two reductions behind it, is a different negotiation than a home that hit the market last Thursday.

It also changes what you ask about. Days on market and price history are public information your agent can pull in seconds. If a house has been sitting for two months and dropped twice, the seller has already told you, in public, that they are willing to move. If a house came on eight days ago at a sharp price and got three showings the first weekend, the same offer strategy will get you nowhere.

One more thing that matters and gets underused. Price is not the only lever. A seller who does not want to cut the sticker number may be perfectly willing to pay closing costs, buy down your rate for the first couple of years, fix the roof item on the inspection, or leave the washer and dryer. In a market where a lot of sellers are already adjusting, concession conversations tend to be easier than they are when inventory is thin. Sometimes a seller will hand you three thousand dollars in closing costs faster than they will drop the list price by three thousand, because the list price is what shows up on the comps.

How This Pattern Built Up in Southern Nevada

Las Vegas has run a higher price-drop share than the country for a while, and the 1.1 percentage point year-over-year rise in August was a continuation, not a sudden turn. The national number moved a full point too, from 19.8 percent to 20.8 percent, which tells you the same pressure exists outside Nevada. It is just further along here.

Part of the reason is how Southern Nevada prices homes in the first place. This is a market with a lot of tract housing, a lot of near-identical floor plans, and a lot of sellers who can look at a neighbor's closed sale and pick a number. When several neighbors list in the same quarter, small differences in condition and upgrades get sorted out by the market rather than by the original asking price. The correction shows up as a price reduction.

Part of it is the new construction competition. Builders across Clark County have their own pricing tools, and they move fast. A resale seller two streets away from an active builder community is competing with a company that can adjust incentives weekly. When the builder moves, the resale listing has to respond somehow, and a price reduction is the most visible way to do it.

And part of it is simply the amount of standing inventory. More homes listed means more homes that do not get an offer in the first three weekends, and a listing without an offer after three weekends almost always gets a price conversation. None of this is unique to Las Vegas. It just happens at a higher rate here than in the national average, which is exactly what the two Redfin figures show.

What to Watch From Here

Redfin updates both readings on a regular cycle, so the honest answer about the next few months is that we will find out when the data lands. I am not going to tell you where the price-drop share goes next, because nobody knows, and anybody who says otherwise is guessing in a confident voice.

What is worth tracking is the gap between the two numbers rather than either number on its own. If the national share keeps climbing while the Las Vegas share moves slowly, the two converge and the local market looks less unusual. If the local share moves faster, the gap widens. Either way, the gap is the story, not the raw local percentage.

The second thing worth tracking is what happens after a price drop. A reduction is a data point, not an outcome. Some reduced listings go under contract within two weeks of the cut. Others reduce again. Watching how quickly reduced listings in your target neighborhood go pending tells you more about real buyer demand than the price-drop share ever will, and that information is available to any buyer working with an agent who pulls it.

Also keep an eye on the months-of-supply figure for Southern Nevada and on how many listings are sitting without an offer. Those two numbers plus the price-drop share form a reasonable dashboard. No single one of them answers the question by itself.

Ryan's Take

I want to be careful here, because this is the kind of number that gets turned into a promise, and I am not going to promise you anything. A 39.1 percent price-drop share does not mean you are going to get a deal. It does not mean every Clark County seller will take a low offer. It means the odds that a given seller has already moved on price are meaningfully higher here than they are nationally. That is useful information. It is not a coupon.

What I tell buyers is to stop shopping the market and start shopping the listing. Pull the price history on every house you like. Look at how long it has been available, what the original list price was, how many reductions there have been, and whether the seller has gone off market and come back with a new listing date. That story, house by house, tells you where to push and where you would just be wasting a weekend.

And be honest about the other six in ten. A well-priced, well-presented Las Vegas home in a neighborhood people want still moves, and a lowball on that house does nothing except tell the seller you are not serious. Use the data to be precise, not to be aggressive across the board. Precision is what actually wins offers.

What You Can Do

Start by asking for the price history before you tour anything. Any agent can pull original list date, original price, every reduction with its date, and the current days on market. Ask for it on every home on your list, and ask for the same data on the last several comparable sales in that neighborhood. Two hours of this work will do more for your negotiating position than any market headline.

Next, write down what you actually need besides price. Closing cost help, a rate buydown, a repair credit, a longer or shorter close, an appliance package. Put them in priority order before you get emotional about a house. When a seller will not move on the number, having a ranked list of alternatives is what keeps a negotiation alive instead of letting it stall.

Then get your financing lined up in a way that makes your offer easy to say yes to. A seller who has already cut their price once is thinking about certainty, not just dollars. A clean pre-approval, a realistic timeline, and a buyer who does not need to sell something first can be worth real money at the table.

Finally, watch your specific neighborhoods rather than the whole valley. Price-drop behavior is not uniform across Clark County. What is happening in one zip code may look nothing like the next one over, and the county-wide average hides all of it.
