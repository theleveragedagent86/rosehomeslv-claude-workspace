A 23-Year-Old's Coffee Trailer Gives Part of Every Friday to Valley Shelters

Jordan Haas is 23 years old, and her mobile coffee trailer, Pups and Cups, donates a portion of its profits every Friday to Las Vegas area animal shelters and rescues. Her motto says it all: "sip a cup, save a pup." Buy a latte, help a dog find a home.

The trailer moves around the valley, so Haas posts her schedule and photos of adoptable dogs online before each stop. She has recently set up at Bruce Trent Park, and the Friday donation runs go toward food, blankets, and beds that shelters and rescues actually need, often bought straight off their Amazon wish lists.

It is a small operation with a big weekly habit, and it lands at a moment when Clark County's animal shelters and rescue groups have been asking the public for exactly this kind of steady, practical support. A coffee trailer cannot fix shelter capacity by itself, but it can keep showing up, week after week, and that consistency is the part of this story worth paying attention to.

What Happened

Pups and Cups is a mobile coffee trailer owned and run by Jordan Haas, a 23-year-old with a simple idea. Every Friday, she sets aside part of the day's profits and gives it to a Las Vegas area shelter or rescue. It is not a once-a-year fundraiser or a holiday special. It is a standing weekly habit built into how the business runs, and it happens whether the week was a big sales day or a slow one.

The donations are not always cash. Haas often checks a shelter's Amazon wish list and buys exactly what they say they need: food, blankets, beds, and other everyday supplies that keep animals comfortable while they wait for a home. That approach means the shelter gets something it can use right away instead of waiting on a check to clear, and it takes the guesswork out of giving. A shelter that says it needs blankets gets blankets, not a general donation that has to be sorted into a budget line somewhere.

Outside the trailer, Haas posts photos of adoptable dogs so people who stop for coffee can see a face and maybe fall for one. It turns a coffee run into a small chance encounter with a dog that needs a family. Someone who was only planning to grab a drink on the way to work might leave with a name, a photo, and a reason to call a shelter later that day. The trailer has recently been parked at Bruce Trent Park, one of several valley stops on her rotation, and each stop becomes its own small pop-up adoption ad without any formal event needing to be scheduled.

On the menu, drinks run $6 for a 16 ounce cup and $7 for a 24 ounce cup, prices that sit in line with what a valley coffee stand or drive-through typically charges. Her best seller is a drink called the Funky Puppy, a name that fits the whole idea of the business and gives regulars something to order by name. Haas has said she is working toward opening a brick and mortar shop down the road, one where she could host adoption events on a bigger scale instead of just outside a trailer window. A permanent location would also let her build steadier relationships with individual shelters rather than rotating support week to week based on wherever the trailer happens to park.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Small businesses that give back on a regular schedule are rare, and Pups and Cups is doing it every single week, not just when it is convenient. For neighbors, that means a cup of coffee turns into something more, without costing extra or asking for anything beyond showing up. There is no separate donation form to fill out and no pressure to give more than the price of a drink.

Clark County's shelter and rescue system has been under real pressure for a while now. Intake numbers have stayed high across the valley, and rescues and shelters are constantly working through supply needs on top of the harder job of finding animals permanent homes. A steady stream of food, blankets, and beds from a business like this does not solve that on its own, but it helps a shelter stretch its budget toward the bigger costs, like veterinary care and staffing, instead of spending down its cash reserves on everyday supplies.

There is also a simple community benefit here. A mobile trailer that rotates through different parks means different neighborhoods get a turn to interact with it. Families who might never drive across town to a shelter can run into adoptable dogs at a park they already visit on a Saturday morning, which lowers the barrier between "I care about this" and "I actually did something about it."

For residents who care about animal welfare but do not have the time to volunteer at a shelter, this is a low effort way to contribute. Buying a coffee is not a donation drive with paperwork. It is a normal transaction that happens to help, and that is exactly the kind of everyday, low friction giving that tends to stick around because nobody has to change their routine to do it.

It also puts a face, literally, on the valley's ongoing shelter capacity conversation. News coverage of overcrowded shelters and strained rescue budgets can start to feel abstract after a while. A photo of a specific adoptable dog taped up next to a coffee window brings that conversation back down to something a person driving through their own neighborhood can actually act on that same afternoon.

This kind of story also tends to travel well on social media in a way that pure news coverage does not always manage. A short video of a dog greeting customers at a coffee window is the sort of thing people share with a friend who has been talking about adopting, and that casual word of mouth can do as much for a shelter's visibility as a formal campaign. Businesses that lean into that dynamic, intentionally or not, end up doing outreach work that shelters themselves often do not have the staff or budget to do on their own.

Background and History

Mobile coffee trailers and food trucks have grown steadily across the Las Vegas valley over the past several years, giving new business owners a lower cost way to start a food and beverage business without signing a long commercial lease. That lower barrier to entry is part of what let someone as young as Haas launch her own operation instead of waiting years to save up for a storefront. A trailer also lets an owner test different neighborhoods and parks before committing to one fixed address.

Pairing a coffee business with animal rescue work is not entirely new nationally, but building the donation into a fixed weekly rhythm, every Friday, without fail, is what sets Pups and Cups apart from a one-time charity pop-up. It turns generosity into a system rather than an event. A single fundraiser can raise a bigger number in one day, but a weekly commitment builds a relationship with the same shelters and rescues over months, which tends to matter more to an organization trying to plan its own budget.

Clark County's animal shelter capacity has been a recurring topic in local news over the past year, with shelters and rescue groups regularly asking the public for fosters, adopters, and supply donations. Intake pressure tends to spike after the holidays and again in the heat of summer, when owner surrenders and stray pickups both climb. Local businesses stepping in to help meet those supply needs, even in a small way, fits into a broader pattern of the valley's business community pitching in where it can, whether that is a restaurant hosting an adoption weekend or, in this case, a coffee trailer building the giving directly into its weekly sales.

Haas building adoption photos into her regular social media posts also mirrors a shift many shelters and rescues have made themselves, leaning on visibility and storytelling as much as intake numbers to move animals into homes faster. A small business with its own following can extend that reach into an audience that might not otherwise follow a shelter's own page.

Young business owners starting mobile operations in Las Vegas also tend to build a following stop by stop rather than through a single grand opening. A trailer earns regulars one park, one neighborhood, one Friday at a time, and that slower, more personal growth pattern is part of why customers feel like they know the owner by name instead of just knowing a brand. Haas being 23 and already thinking about a permanent location says she is treating the trailer as a first chapter rather than the whole story.

What Happens Next

Haas has said she is working toward a brick and mortar location. A permanent shop would let her host actual adoption events rather than relying on the smaller footprint of a trailer window, and it could mean a steadier, more visible partnership with local shelters and rescues. It would also give her a fixed address that regulars and shelters alike could count on, rather than a schedule that changes from week to week.

In the meantime, the trailer keeps moving. Because Pups and Cups is mobile, its schedule shifts from week to week, so anyone who wants to catch it should check her current posted schedule before heading out. A park stop one Friday does not guarantee the same spot the next, and a business built this way lives and dies by how well it communicates where it will be.

Watch for updates on which shelters and rescues receive the weekly donations. As the business grows, that list could expand to reach more of the valley's animal welfare groups, and a future storefront could turn what is now a rotating series of stops into a fixed, dependable partner for whichever shelters end up closest to it.

There is also a question worth watching for any small business built this way: how it scales without losing the thing that made it work in the first place. A trailer can rotate to a new park every week and keep the giving personal, tied to one owner making one decision each Friday. A storefront brings steadier hours and a fixed address, but it also brings higher fixed costs, and how Haas balances that growth against the weekly donation habit will say a lot about where the business goes from here.

Ryan's Take

I like seeing a young entrepreneur build a giving habit into the business model from day one instead of treating it as an afterthought. It is the kind of small, steady community investment that adds character to a neighborhood, the same way a good coffee shop or a favorite taco stand does. When I'm working with buyers who ask what makes a Las Vegas neighborhood feel like home, it's often these small touches, a food truck people know by name, a park where the community actually gathers, that tip the scale toward one area over another.

A twenty three year old building toward her own storefront also says something about the small business energy still moving through this valley. Buyers and sellers ask me all the time whether Las Vegas still has room for new, independent businesses next to the bigger chains, and stories like this one are a good, concrete answer. A mobile trailer today, hopefully a storefront tomorrow, is exactly the kind of growth path that keeps neighborhood commercial corridors interesting instead of predictable.

What You Can Do

If you want to catch Pups and Cups, follow along for the current schedule since the trailer is mobile and moves between valley locations, including recent stops at Bruce Trent Park. Because the location changes, check the schedule before you head out rather than assuming last week's spot. A drink is $6 for 16 ounces or $7 for 24 ounces, and the Funky Puppy is the one to try if you want to taste the crowd favorite.

If you cannot make it to a stop, most valley shelters and rescues post their own Amazon wish lists directly, so you can send food, blankets, or beds without leaving your house. Every item off that list is one less thing a shelter has to buy out of pocket, and it costs nothing extra beyond what you would already pay for the item.

Fostering is another option worth considering if a permanent adoption is not realistic right now. Shelters and rescues in the valley regularly need short term foster homes to free up kennel space, and a foster commitment can last anywhere from a few days to a few months depending on the group.

And if you have room in your home, adopting is still the biggest thing anyone can do. A coffee and a photo outside a trailer window is often how that connection starts, and a small business like Pups and Cups is proof that it does not take a big gesture to make that first introduction happen.
