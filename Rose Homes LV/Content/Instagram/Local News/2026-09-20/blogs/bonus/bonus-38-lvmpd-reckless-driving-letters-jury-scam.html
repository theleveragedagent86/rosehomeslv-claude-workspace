Metro Says That Reckless Driving Letter Is Real, the Jury Duty Call Is Not

Las Vegas police say a warning letter that many valley residents have found in their mailbox about reckless driving is real. It is not a ticket. It carries no points, and it never gets reported to the DMV. At the same time, Metro is warning residents about a fake phone call where someone claims you missed jury duty, says you have an active warrant, and tells you to stay on the phone while you go withdraw cash at a bank. That call is a scam, every time.

Both of these are landing on Las Vegas Valley residents right now, one through the mail and one through the phone, and mixing them up costs people money and peace of mind. LVMPD Officer Robert Wicks laid out exactly what the reckless driving letters are, how they get sent, and why the jury duty call is never legitimate no matter how official it sounds.

What Happened

According to Officer Robert Wicks with the Las Vegas Metropolitan Police Department, Metro has been mailing reckless driving warning letters to registered vehicle owners for about a year and a half. Wicks confirmed the letters are real, but he was clear about what they are not. They are not citations. They do not carry any points against a driver's license, and Metro never reports them to the Nevada DMV.

The letters start with a phone call, not a traffic stop. When someone in the community calls 911 to report a vehicle driving recklessly, that report gets routed to the area command that covers the location where the driving happened. From there, investigators use the information from the 911 call, often a license plate, to identify the registered owner of the vehicle. Metro then mails that registered owner a letter describing the reported behavior.

The letter is meant to put a driver on notice. It tells the registered owner that a complaint came in about the way their vehicle was being driven. Because it is not a citation, it does not require a court date, a fine, or a response back to Metro. Officer Wicks said the goal is simple: let people know their driving on a public street got someone's attention, without putting points on a license over a report that an officer did not personally witness.

Separately, and unrelated to the mailed letters, Metro issued a public warning about a phone scam making the rounds in the valley. In this scam, a caller claims to be with a court or law enforcement agency and tells the person on the line that they missed jury duty. The caller says there is now an active warrant out for the resident's arrest. To make the warrant go away, the caller instructs the victim to stay on the phone and drive to a bank to withdraw cash, which the caller then collects through a wire transfer, a gift card purchase, or by directing the victim to hand it to a courier. Metro was direct about this one: it is fake, and no legitimate court or police agency operates this way.

Officer Wicks noted that the two issues surfaced publicly around the same time, which is part of why Metro chose to address them together. Residents were calling in with questions about the mailed letters right as reports of the jury duty calls started coming into dispatch, and department leadership decided a single, clear public statement covering both would cut down on the confusion faster than handling them separately.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

These two stories are landing in Clark County households at the same time, and that timing is exactly why people get confused. A resident who gets a reckless driving letter in the mail might panic and assume it is the start of a legal process, when it is not. A resident who gets a phone call about a missed jury summons might assume it is connected to something real they cannot quite remember, and panic in the other direction by paying to make it go away.

Both reactions come from the same place. Most people do not deal with law enforcement paperwork often enough to know what a real notice looks like versus a fake one. Scammers count on that gap. They also count on urgency. A caller who says "stay on the phone, you have a warrant, go to the bank right now" is trying to keep a victim from stopping to think, calling a family member, or hanging up to check.

For homeowners and renters across the valley, from Summerlin to Henderson to North Las Vegas, this is not a small inconvenience. Jury duty scams that end in a bank withdrawal have cost victims thousands of dollars in a single call. Nevada has an older population and a large number of newer residents who may not know Clark County's court and law enforcement systems well, and both groups are common targets.

The reckless driving letters carry a different kind of stake. They do not cost money, but they can cause needless stress if a driver assumes a citation or license points are coming. Understanding that the letter is a notice, not a legal action, takes the fear out of opening that envelope and lets a driver treat it as what it is: a heads up that someone in their neighborhood noticed their driving and reported it.

There is also a family safety angle worth thinking about. Teen drivers and new drivers are common subjects of these reports, since neighbors are often quick to call in a car that is speeding through a residential street or cutting through a school zone. Parents who understand that a letter like this is not the same as a ticket can use it as a teaching moment with a young driver instead of a source of panic in the household.

Renters and homeowners alike should also remember that the registered owner of a vehicle receives the letter, not necessarily the person who was driving at the time. If a family member, roommate, or adult child was behind the wheel when a report came in, the letter still goes to whoever the car is registered to. That is worth knowing before assuming the letter reflects your own driving.

Background and History

Metro's reckless driving letter program has been running quietly for about a year and a half, according to Officer Wicks. It grew out of a simple problem. Officers cannot be everywhere, and a lot of dangerous driving gets reported after the fact by a witness who calls 911 with a description and a license plate, not during a traffic stop where an officer can issue a citation on the spot.

Rather than let those reports disappear once a call ends, Metro built a process to route them to the area command covering that stretch of road. Investigators there pull the registered owner information tied to the plate and send a letter. It is a way to close the loop on citizen reports without requiring an officer to have witnessed the driving directly, which the law generally requires before a citation can be issued.

Jury duty scams, on the other hand, are not new to Nevada or to the country. Variations of the "you missed jury duty and have a warrant" call have circulated in different states for years, and the script barely changes: claim an official identity, invent an urgent legal consequence, and instruct the victim to convert cash into something the scammer can collect before anyone has time to verify the story. What changes are the local details scammers use to sound believable, like naming a real Nevada courthouse or referencing Clark County by name.

What makes this scam durable is that jury duty is a real, if uncommon, part of civic life. Most adults in Clark County know a jury summons exists somewhere in the legal system even if they have never personally received one, and that small bit of familiarity is exactly what a scammer leans on. It is close enough to something real that the story does not sound made up on its face, which is why hanging up and verifying independently matters more than trying to spot the scam through gut instinct alone.

What Happens Next

Metro is continuing to send reckless driving letters through its area commands as 911 reports come in, so residents across the valley should expect this program to keep running. There is no indication it is changing into a system that issues citations or points, and Officer Wicks was clear that the letters remain a notice-only tool.

On the scam side, expect the jury duty call, and calls like it, to keep showing up on residents' phones. Metro's warning is part of an ongoing public safety effort, not a one-time alert tied to a single wave of calls. Scammers rotate their scripts and their caller ID information, so a warning about one exact version of the call does not mean the tactic disappears. It usually just gets a new coat of paint.

Residents who want to stay ahead of both issues should watch for any official Metro communication that clarifies how the letters are formatted, since scammers sometimes try to mimic real government mail once a program becomes public knowledge. If Metro or another Clark County agency ever changes how these letters look or what they contain, it is worth checking LVMPD's official channels directly rather than trusting a letter's appearance alone.

It is also reasonable to expect Metro to keep using its public information channels, including local news outlets like News 3 Las Vegas, to get ahead of new scam variations as they appear. Public safety messaging works best when it reaches people before a scammer calls them, not after, so residents who follow local news coverage on this topic will likely see updates as new versions of these calls surface across the valley.

This also matters for anyone thinking about the valley as a place to put down roots. A police department that is willing to publicly explain its own internal process, and just as publicly call out a scam targeting its residents, is a good sign for a community. What You Can Do

If you get a reckless driving letter from Metro in the mail, know that it is not a citation, it carries no points, and it will not be reported to the DMV. There is generally no requirement to respond to it. If you have questions about a letter you received, you can call LVMPD's non-emergency line to confirm it is legitimate rather than guessing.

If you get a phone call claiming you missed jury duty and have a warrant, hang up. A real court or law enforcement agency will not ask you to stay on the phone while you go to a bank, and it will never ask you to withdraw cash, buy gift cards, or hand money to a courier to resolve a warrant. If the caller says they have a badge number, ask for it, then hang up and look up the agency's actual phone number yourself instead of calling any number the caller gave you. Call that independently verified number to ask whether the claim is real.

It also helps to talk about this with family members who may be more likely to fall for the urgency of a scam call, including older relatives. A quick conversation now, letting them know this specific scam exists and that hanging up and calling back on a number you looked up yourself is always the safe move, can prevent a very expensive phone call later.

Keep in mind the same rule covers more than just this one scam. No legitimate agency, whether it is a court, the IRS, a utility company, or the police, will ever ask you to withdraw cash, buy gift cards, or send money through a wire transfer to resolve a legal problem over the phone. If a call ever includes that instruction, treat it as a red flag regardless of what story the caller tells you, and end the call.

Finally, if you do encounter one of these calls, consider reporting it. Even if you did not lose any money, letting Metro know the details of the call, including the phone number that showed up on your caller ID and what the caller claimed, helps investigators track the pattern and warn other residents faster.
