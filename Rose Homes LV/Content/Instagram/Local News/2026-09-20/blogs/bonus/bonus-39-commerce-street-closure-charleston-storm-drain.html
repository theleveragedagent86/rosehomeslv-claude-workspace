Commerce Street Closes South of Charleston for Four Weeks Starting Monday

Starting Monday, September 22, southbound Commerce Street on the south side of Charleston Boulevard will be closed for about four weeks. Crews need the closure to install underground box culverts for the Charleston Boulevard Storm Drain Project.

This is not a small detour. Charleston Boulevard itself drops to one lane in each direction during work hours, and Commerce Street will temporarily run two-way in a stretch where it is normally one-way. If you live, work, or drive through downtown Las Vegas or the Arts District, this closure is going to change your daily route for the next month.

What Happened

Beginning Monday, crews close southbound Commerce Street on the south side of Charleston Boulevard for roughly four weeks. The closure is tied directly to the Charleston Boulevard Storm Drain Project, and the specific task at hand is installing underground box culverts, the large concrete channels that carry stormwater beneath the road. Box culverts cannot be installed without digging up and closing the roadway above them, which is why the closure runs for a full month rather than a weekend.

The work limits southbound travel on Commerce Street daily from 7 a.m. to 6 p.m. That is an eleven-hour window every day, covering the entire morning and afternoon commute for downtown workers and Arts District residents. Outside those hours, the restrictions may ease, but drivers should not count on that changing day to day since construction schedules shift.

Charleston Boulevard itself is affected too. During the closure, Charleston drops to one lane in each direction. Charleston is one of the busiest east-west streets in the entire downtown core, so losing a lane in both directions is going to slow traffic well beyond the immediate work zone. Drivers heading east or west through downtown should expect backups, especially during peak commute hours.

To keep some north-south movement available near the closure, Commerce Street will temporarily convert to two-way between California Avenue and the closure point. Commerce Street is typically a one-way street in that stretch, so this is a genuine, if temporary, change to how the road functions. Drivers who know Commerce Street well should pay close attention to new signage, because the normal flow of traffic will not apply for the next several weeks.

Box culvert installation is not a quick patch job. Crews first have to excavate a trench deep enough and wide enough to fit large precast concrete sections, then lower those sections into place, connect them, and backfill the trench before the roadway surface can be repaired and reopened. Each of those steps takes time, and weather, underground utility conflicts, or equipment delivery schedules can all push a project like this a few days in either direction. That is part of why officials are framing the closure as "about four weeks" rather than committing to an exact reopening date.

Drivers should also expect some variability in how strictly the 7 a.m. to 6 p.m. window is enforced day to day. Early mornings and evenings may see lighter restrictions as crews wrap up for the day, but the safest assumption for anyone planning a trip through the area is that the closure is active during standard business hours, every weekday, for the full four weeks.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Downtown Las Vegas and the Arts District do not have a lot of north-south options to begin with. Commerce Street is one of the connectors that lets residents, delivery drivers, and commuters move between Charleston Boulevard and the streets south of it without detouring all the way around downtown. Taking that connector away for a month, even temporarily, forces a lot of trips onto other streets that were not built to absorb the extra volume.

Arts District businesses depend on customers being able to get to them easily. A four-week stretch of construction noise, lane reductions, and confusing detour signage right next to a retail and gallery district can quietly cut into foot traffic and drive-up visits. If you own or manage a business near Commerce and Charleston, it is worth telling regular customers about the closure directly, since a surprise detour is often what keeps people from stopping by at all.

Commuters who drive Charleston Boulevard to get through downtown, whether heading to the Medical District, the Fremont Street area, or points further east, are going to feel this too. One lane in each direction on a major arterial road means slower trips and more brake lights during the 7 a.m. to 6 p.m. work window every single day for a month. Building in extra travel time now, before the closure starts, is a lot easier than discovering the delay on a morning you cannot afford to be late.

Renters and homeowners near the closure zone should also expect some added noise and dust during work hours, which is typical for underground utility installation. Box culvert work involves heavy equipment and open trenches, so this is not the kind of project that finishes quietly in the background.

Parents dropping kids at schools near the closure zone should plan a backup route too. A single lane in each direction on Charleston Boulevard, combined with the temporary two-way switch on Commerce Street, can create confusing intersections for a few weeks, and confusion at intersections is exactly when fender benders and near misses happen. Give yourself extra time and expect to see more traffic control officers and cones than usual near the work zone.

Anyone who relies on rideshare or delivery services in the affected area should also expect longer pickup and drop-off times during the closure window. Drivers unfamiliar with the temporary detour pattern may need extra guidance to find an address near Commerce and Charleston, so it is worth mentioning the closure when you place an order or request a ride from that part of downtown.

Background and History

The Commerce Street closure is one piece of a much larger effort called the Charleston Boulevard Storm Drain Project. The full project runs from Maryland Parkway on the east end to Commerce Street on the west, covering a significant stretch of one of downtown's most heavily traveled corridors.

Storm drain projects like this one exist because older parts of Las Vegas, including much of the downtown core, were built before the valley saw the kind of intense, sudden rainfall events that have become more common in recent years. Flash flooding has long been a real risk in low-lying parts of the city, and undersized or aging storm drain infrastructure has made certain intersections and roadways prone to flooding during heavy storms.

Box culverts are a standard fix for this problem. By installing larger underground channels that can move more water more quickly away from the street, the project is designed to reduce the flooding risk along the entire Charleston corridor, not just at the Commerce Street intersection. That is a meaningful long-term benefit for a part of town that has dealt with flash flood problems for decades, even if it comes with real short-term disruption while the work is happening.

Projects of this scale are typically broken into segments, with different intersections and blocks closed at different times as the underground work moves along the corridor. The Commerce Street closure appears to be one segment in that sequence, meaning residents in other parts of the Charleston corridor may see similar, temporary closures as the broader project continues toward its 2027 completion date.

Downtown Las Vegas has invested heavily in revitalization over the past decade, from the Arts District's gallery and restaurant scene to new residential development near Fremont Street. Aging infrastructure underneath those same streets does not always keep pace with that above-ground growth. A storm drain system designed decades ago for a smaller, less developed downtown can struggle to handle the runoff from today's denser mix of buildings, parking lots, and paved surfaces. Projects like the Charleston Boulevard Storm Drain Project are part of the unglamorous but necessary work of catching underground infrastructure up to what is happening above it.

Clark County and the City of Las Vegas have flagged several corridors in and around downtown as flood-prone in past assessments, and Charleston Boulevard has historically been one of them. Heavy summer monsoon storms in past years have caused street flooding in low-lying stretches of the corridor, sometimes stranding vehicles and closing lanes temporarily until water could drain. The box culvert work happening now is aimed directly at reducing those kinds of closures in the future, even though it requires a planned closure today to get there.

What Happens Next

The southbound Commerce Street closure is expected to last about four weeks from its Monday start date. Drivers should plan for the closure to run through most of October, though construction timelines can shift based on weather, equipment availability, and what crews find once they start digging.

During the closure, official detour routes point drivers to Oakey Boulevard or Bonneville Avenue for east-west travel, and to Casino Center Boulevard or Las Vegas Boulevard for north-south travel. These are the routes local officials are recommending as the best alternatives while Commerce Street and Charleston Boulevard are constrained.

Beyond this specific closure, the larger Charleston Boulevard Storm Drain Project is slated for completion by late 2027. That means more construction activity, and likely more temporary closures at other points along the Maryland Parkway to Commerce Street corridor, over the next year or so. Downtown residents and commuters should expect this to be an ongoing story rather than a one-time event.

Once the Commerce Street segment wraps up, crews are expected to move on to other sections of the Charleston corridor as the project works its way toward completion. Each segment will likely bring its own version of the same tradeoff residents are facing now, short-term lane reductions and detours in exchange for a permanent upgrade to the area's flood control capacity. Keeping an eye on city and county construction announcements over the coming months is the best way to stay ahead of the next phase before it affects your commute.

What I would do is ask about drainage history on any specific property near the Charleston corridor, since a storm drain project this size tells you the city has identified real flooding risk in that area, and that history is worth understanding before you buy.

What You Can Do

If your regular commute or errands take you through Commerce Street or Charleston Boulevard near downtown, build extra time into your schedule for the next four weeks, especially between 7 a.m. and 6 p.m. when the restrictions are in effect. The detour routes, Oakey Boulevard or Bonneville Avenue for east-west travel and Casino Center Boulevard or Las Vegas Boulevard for north-south travel, are your best options while the closure is active.

Because construction schedules shift, it is worth checking current road conditions before you head out, especially in the first few days after the closure starts and again as the four-week window nears its end. The Nevada Department of Transportation and City of Las Vegas typically post updates when timelines change.

If you run a business in the Arts District near this closure, consider letting customers know about the detour routes directly through social media or signage, so a temporary traffic pattern does not turn into a temporary drop in visits.

Residents who rely on public transit through the area should also check the RTC's website for any temporary route or stop changes tied to the closure. Bus routes that run along Commerce Street or through the affected stretch of Charleston Boulevard may see stops relocated or schedules adjusted for the duration of the work, and it is easier to plan around that ahead of time than to discover a moved bus stop on your morning commute.

Finally, if you notice drainage or ponding issues near your own property during this construction period, especially if a storm hits while the work zone is open, document it and report it to the city. Active construction zones can sometimes create temporary drainage complications even while they are working to fix the underlying problem long term.
