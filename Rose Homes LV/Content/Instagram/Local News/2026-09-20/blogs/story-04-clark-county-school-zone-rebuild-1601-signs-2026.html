Clark County Rebuilt Its School Zones After More Than 50 Students Were Hit Since August 10

Clark County spent the first 30 days of the school year physically rebuilding the streets around its campuses. Crews replaced 1,601 traffic signs, installed 229 brand new ones, repainted or rebuilt 108 crosswalks, and trimmed 269 trees that were blocking drivers from seeing kids. The county also extended school zone flashers to run 40 minutes before and 40 minutes after school, which is a longer window than most valley drivers are used to.

The reason for the rush is hard to read. More than 50 students have been struck by vehicles since August 10, the first day of class. That follows more than 400 students struck during the 2025-2026 school year. County traffic officials laid all of this out at the September 15, 2026 Board of Commissioners meeting, along with 75 recommendations from a working group built specifically to fix the problem.

If you drive anywhere near a school in Las Vegas, Henderson, North Las Vegas, or unincorporated Clark County, your morning and afternoon commute changed this fall. Here is what is different, why it happened, and what is still coming.

What Happened

On September 15, 2026, Clark County traffic officials presented a progress report to the Board of Commissioners. The report covered the work of the School Traffic Safety Working Group, a 90-member panel that pulled in staff from every city in the valley plus the Clark County School District, the Regional Transportation Commission of Southern Nevada, the Nevada Department of Transportation, and the Southern Nevada Health District. That is a wide table for a local safety group, and it was built that way on purpose. School zones do not stop at city lines, and neither do the drivers cutting through them.

The working group produced 75 recommendations. Some are long-term policy items. Others were things crews could go do immediately with paint, poles, and a bucket truck. The county went after the immediate ones first, and the numbers from the first 30 days of school are the headline.

Crews replaced 1,601 traffic signs. Think about that number for a second. That is not 1,601 signs across a decade of maintenance. That is 1,601 signs swapped out in roughly one month, because faded, bent, blocked, or out-of-date signs were everywhere. On top of the replacements, crews installed 229 completely new signs in spots that did not have adequate warning before.

Crews also repainted or fully replaced 108 crosswalks. In the Las Vegas sun, crosswalk paint fades fast, and a washed out crosswalk at 6:45 in the morning with the sun low in the east is close to invisible. Another 269 trees were trimmed. That one sounds minor until you picture a mature tree that has grown out over a sidewalk and hidden a school zone sign, a flashing beacon, or a kid standing at the curb.

The biggest behavioral change is the flasher timing. School zone flashers now run for 40 minutes before school starts and 40 minutes after school ends. The old windows were tighter. Kids do not arrive and leave on a perfect schedule, and the extra minutes cover the early drop-offs and the slow walkers who are still in the crosswalk after the bell.

Enforcement moved too. CCSD Police citations rose 33.7 percent and vehicle stops rose 38.9 percent compared to the prior period. That is a real shift in how these zones are being patrolled, and it is showing up in people's mail.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Start with the obvious one. If you have a kid in a Clark County school, this is about whether they get home safely. More than 400 students struck in a single school year is not a statistic you can explain away with bad luck. It is a pattern, and the valley's street design is part of it.

But this also hits people who do not have kids at all. Clark County schools sit inside neighborhoods. Elementary campuses in particular are tucked into residential pockets, surrounded by single-family homes, and fed by streets that were never designed to absorb 300 cars in a 20 minute window. If you live within a few blocks of a school, that flasher window change means your street now runs at 15 miles per hour for a longer stretch of the morning and afternoon.

For commuters, the practical effect is time. Forty minutes on each side of the school day is a wide net. A driver who used to clear a school zone at full speed at 7:05 may now be hitting an active flasher. The same goes for the afternoon. If your route to work or to a job site runs past two or three campuses, you are adding minutes, and you are doing it on a stretch where CCSD Police stops are up almost 39 percent.

There is also a cost angle. Nevada school zone violations are not cheap, and a citation can follow you into your insurance rate. A 33.7 percent jump in citations means a lot of valley drivers are finding that out the hard way this fall. The cheapest response is simply knowing where the zones are and when they are live.

For homeowners, street safety is quietly part of what your neighborhood is worth. Buyers with young kids ask about walkability to the school. They ask whether there is a crosswalk, whether there is a crossing guard, and whether the street in front feels safe. Freshly painted crosswalks, clear sightlines, and working beacons are small things that show up in how a street feels on a Saturday showing.

Renters feel it too, and in Clark County that is a large share of households. A family renting a home near a campus deals with the same drop-off traffic, the same crossing, and the same crowded curb every morning. School zone conditions are one of the few neighborhood factors that affect owners and renters identically, which is part of why the working group pulled in every city rather than leaving it to one jurisdiction.

Background and History

This did not start in August. The 2025-2026 school year ended with more than 400 students struck by vehicles across Clark County. That number forced the issue into the open, and it is what led to the formation of the School Traffic Safety Working Group in the first place.

The structure of the group tells you how the county sees the problem. Ninety members drawn from every valley city, CCSD, the RTC, NDOT, and the Southern Nevada Health District is not a committee you build for a paperwork exercise. Each agency owns a different piece of the street. The county and the cities own most of the local roads and the signs. NDOT owns the state highways that many campuses sit near. The RTC handles regional transportation planning and bus routes. CCSD owns the campuses, the bus fleet, and its own police department. The health district tracks injuries. Before the working group, those pieces did not always talk to each other about the same intersection.

The valley's growth pattern is the other half of the story. Clark County built out fast, and it built out wide. Many neighborhoods are laid out with a school in the interior and a major arterial road with a 45 mile per hour posted speed running along one edge. Kids walking to campus from the other side of that arterial have to cross it. That design is common across Spring Valley, the southwest, the northeast, and North Las Vegas, and it puts children and fast-moving traffic in the same place twice a day.

Sun angle matters here more than most people think. In late summer and fall, the morning sun sits low in the east right at drop-off time and low in the west at pickup. Drivers heading into the glare on an east-west street genuinely cannot see well. That is part of why the tree trimming and the sign replacement work is not cosmetic. Anything that improves how quickly a driver registers a crosswalk in bad light is worth doing.

It is worth saying plainly that the county is not starting from zero on this. School zones, crossing guards, and reduced speed limits have been standard in Clark County for decades. What changed is the volume of traffic and the pace of it. More cars, more delivery vehicles, more rideshare drop-offs, and more devices on the road have pushed an old system past what it was built to handle. The 2025-2026 injury count is what made that gap impossible to ignore, and the 75 recommendations are the first serious attempt to close it valley-wide instead of one campus at a time.

What Happens Next

The county worked through the fastest recommendations first. The remaining items on the list of 75 are longer builds, and those take design time, funding, and coordination with whichever agency owns the road. Expect that work to roll out over the rest of the school year rather than in another 30 day sprint.

Commissioners flagged the next fight at the September 15 meeting, and it is e-bikes and e-scooters. Those devices have changed how a lot of Clark County middle school and high school students get to campus. They move at car-adjacent speeds, they do not always stay in the bike lane, they do not always stop at the stop sign, and helmet use is inconsistent. A kid on an e-bike moving 20 miles per hour into a crosswalk is a different physics problem than a kid on foot, and the county does not have a settled answer yet.

Watch for that topic to come back to the Board of Commissioners. Any real rule on e-bikes and e-scooters near campuses would likely involve CCSD policy, county code, and enforcement all at once, so it is not a one-meeting decision. Parents who have bought their kid an e-bike for the school commute should follow it closely.

The other thing to watch is whether the injury numbers move. The whole point of 1,601 signs and 108 crosswalks is to bring the student crash count down. The county now has a clear baseline and a clear intervention date, so the next progress report should say something meaningful about whether it worked.

One more thing to keep an eye on is funding. Paint, poles, and bucket trucks cost money, and so does the crew time behind 1,601 sign swaps in a month. The items still left on the list of 75 are the more expensive kind, things like new beacons, signal changes, and street redesign near specific campuses. Those compete with everything else in county and city road budgets, so the pace of the rest of the rollout will depend on what gets funded and when.

Ryan's Take

I drive these neighborhoods every day for work, and the difference this fall is noticeable. You see the fresh paint. You see signs that are actually bright instead of sun-bleached to a pale yellow. In a valley where people complain constantly that nothing gets fixed, 1,601 signs in 30 days is a genuinely fast turnaround, and the county deserves credit for it.

From a housing standpoint, this is one of those quiet quality-of-life items that buyers feel even when they cannot name it. When I show a home in a neighborhood where the crosswalk is clean, the school zone beacon works, and the sightlines are open, families relax. When I show a home on a street where the crossing is faded and cars are doing 40 past the school, parents notice and it costs the seller something. Street conditions do not appraise, but they absolutely affect how long a home sits.

My honest read is that the signs and paint are the easy part. The hard part is driver behavior, and 50 students struck in 30 days says we are not there yet. The 40 minute flasher window only works if people actually slow down for it. If you take one thing from this story, let it be that: the fix only counts if we drive like it matters.

What You Can Do

Start with your own route. Map the schools you pass on your normal drive and note the start and end times. With the flashers now running 40 minutes on each side, the live window is wider than you think. Leaving five minutes earlier is usually enough to keep you from feeling rushed through a zone where stops are up almost 39 percent.

If there is a spot near your home that still needs work, report it. Clark County takes traffic and road maintenance requests through its public works channels, and each valley city has its own request system. A faded crosswalk, a sign hidden behind a tree, a beacon that is not flashing, or a missing school zone marker are all fixable items, and the working group's whole reason for existing is to chase down exactly these. Be specific about the cross streets and the school when you report it.

Parents can help on the walking side. Walk the route with your kid once and pick the crossing you want them to use every time, ideally one with a marked crosswalk and a crossing guard. If your child rides an e-bike or e-scooter to school, set the rule now that they dismount and walk it across any crosswalk. That single habit removes the speed difference that makes these crashes so bad.

Finally, pay attention to Board of Commissioners agendas this fall. The e-bike and e-scooter discussion is coming, and the people who show up for public comment tend to shape what the final rule looks like. Meetings are public and agendas are posted in advance.

And if you are house hunting right now, drive the street in front of the school at 7:30 on a weekday before you write an offer. A neighborhood looks very different at drop-off time than it does at a Sunday open house. That ten minute detour tells you more about daily life on that block than any listing photo will.
