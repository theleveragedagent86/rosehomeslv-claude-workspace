The Henderson Girl Scout House, Standing Since 1943, Just Closed for Demolition

The Girl Scout house in downtown Henderson closed for good on Thursday, September 17, 2026, and the building is set to be torn down. It has stood behind the Eagle Club at Pacific Avenue and Van Wagenen Street since 1943, and the Girl Scouts have used it rent free since 1979.

The closure is tied to the city's plan to reconfigure Downtown Park and hand a piece of that land to the Henderson redevelopment agency. More than 100 girls across 13 troops met in that building on a regular basis. There is no replacement building and no plan to move the house somewhere else.

That last part is what has people talking. This is not a remodel or a relocation. It is a building coming down, and a group of Henderson families now looking for somewhere else to meet. It is also the clearest picture yet of how far the Water Street redevelopment footprint has grown.

What Happened

The building sits behind the Eagle Club at the corner of Pacific Avenue and Van Wagenen Street, just off the Water Street corridor in downtown Henderson. It first opened in 1943, back when the Henderson townsite was brand new. The Girl Scouts of Southern Nevada took it over in September 1979 under an arrangement with the city that gave them rent free exclusive use of the space.

That arrangement ran for 47 years. Then, on August 17, 2026, the city sent the Girl Scouts a letter saying the lease would not be renewed. The lease expired on September 17, 2026. The Girl Scouts held a farewell on that Thursday and closed the doors.

Carolyn Wheeler of Girl Scouts of Southern Nevada told News 3 Las Vegas that more than 100 girls in 13 troops used the building regularly. She also confirmed the piece that stings the most for those families. The house is not being relocated and it is not being rebuilt anywhere else. When it comes down, it is simply gone.

The city's reason is a land move. Henderson is reconfiguring Downtown Park, and a portion of that park is being remapped so it can be transferred to the city's redevelopment agency. Once land sits with a redevelopment agency, it can be packaged, marketed, and turned over for a private project in a way that ordinary park land cannot. The Girl Scout house sits inside that footprint.

FOX5 Vegas reported the closing on September 16, 2026, a day before the lease ran out, and News 3 Las Vegas covered the farewell on September 19. Between the two reports, the timeline is tight. Thirty one days passed between the letter and the last day. For a group that had been in the building since the Carter administration, that is a fast goodbye.

It is worth being precise about what the city did and did not say. The city confirmed the terms of the original 1979 agreement, confirmed the August 17 notice, confirmed the September 17 expiration, and confirmed that part of Downtown Park is being remapped for transfer to the redevelopment agency. What has not been laid out publicly, at least not in the coverage available, is what goes on the parcel afterward. That gap is the reason this story spread the way it did. People can accept a plan they disagree with. What they struggle with is a building coming down before anyone has said what replaces it.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Start with the families. More than 100 girls and their leaders now need a new place to meet. Troop meetings need a room, storage for supplies, a bathroom, parking that parents can handle at 6 p.m. on a weeknight, and a cost of zero or close to it. That combination is hard to find anywhere in Clark County, and it is especially hard in a downtown where commercial rent has been climbing.

Most troops end up in a church hall, a school cafeteria, a library meeting room, or somebody's living room. Those work, but they come with schedules, sign in sheets, and the constant risk of losing the room to a higher priority booking. A building your group controls outright is a different thing. Losing it is not just an inconvenience. It is a loss of the one place that belonged to those kids.

Then there is the neighborhood question, and this is the part that reaches past Girl Scout families. Downtown Henderson has been in slow transformation for years. Water Street picked up restaurants, breweries, a public plaza, and the arena that the Henderson Silver Knights call home. That growth has been good for property values and good for the people who wanted a real downtown instead of a strip of vacant storefronts.

But redevelopment always has a footprint, and the footprint always lands on something. Usually it lands on an empty lot or a tired building nobody misses. This time it landed on a building that 13 troops used every week. That is a useful reminder for anyone who owns a home or a small building near Water Street. The map is still being redrawn, and the lines are moving outward from the main corridor into the side streets.

For homeowners in the older Henderson townsite neighborhoods, this matters in a practical way. Redevelopment agency land usually means a project is coming. Projects change traffic, parking, noise, and eventually pricing. Some of those changes are welcome. Some are not. Either way, it is better to know early than to find out when the fencing goes up across the street.

There is also a plain dollars and cents angle for renters and young families in Henderson. Free community space is part of what makes a neighborhood affordable to live in, even though it never shows up in a rent number or a mortgage payment. When the free room disappears, the cost of participating in a scout troop, a youth club, or a neighborhood group goes up, because somebody has to rent a space or drive farther to one. Those small costs stack up, and they hit the households that are already stretched. A city that wants young families in its downtown has to keep some of that free space in the plan.

Background and History

Henderson exists because of World War II. The Basic Magnesium plant went up in the early 1940s to produce magnesium for the war effort, and a townsite went up alongside it to house the workers. Homes, a hospital, a school, and community buildings all appeared in a very short window. The Girl Scout house dates to 1943, which puts it squarely in that first wave of construction.

That is why buildings from this era carry weight in Henderson in a way they do not in newer parts of the valley. Summerlin and Inspirada are beautiful, but nothing there is 83 years old. Downtown Henderson has the oldest continuous residential fabric in the valley outside of the original Las Vegas townsite. Every original building that comes down takes a piece of that with it.

The Girl Scouts moved in during September 1979. The city gave them exclusive use of the building at no rent, which is the kind of arrangement that used to be common for civic groups and is increasingly rare now. Cities across the country have spent the last two decades reviewing legacy leases like this one, usually when the land underneath becomes valuable enough to matter.

Water Street is the piece that changed. For a long stretch, downtown Henderson was quiet, and the city spent years and public money trying to bring it back. The Water Street District got streetscape work, a public plaza, small business incentives, and eventually an arena. Henderson also built new civic buildings nearby. Each step made the surrounding land more valuable and more attractive for private development.

Downtown Park sits in that zone. Reconfiguring the park and remapping part of it for the redevelopment agency is a normal municipal step, and on paper it is procedural. On the ground, it is the mechanism that decided the fate of an 83 year old building.

Henderson is not unusual here. Every older city in the country has faced the same choice, and most have landed in the same place. The buildings that survive are the ones that got a historic designation, a preservation group behind them, or a private owner willing to pay to keep them standing. The buildings that come down are usually the ones sitting on public land with no formal protection, used by a group that has no lease leverage. The Girl Scout house fit that second description exactly, and that is the lesson other Clark County community groups should take from it.

What Happens Next

The immediate step is demolition. The lease has expired, the building is closed, and the city has said the structure is coming down as part of the Downtown Park reconfiguration. A specific demolition date has not been publicly stated in the reporting available, so treat any date you see circulating on social media as unconfirmed until the city posts it. [NOT VERIFIED]

After that, watch the remapping. When a portion of park land is transferred to a redevelopment agency, the paperwork goes through public meetings. That is where the actual plan for the parcel shows up, including what gets built, how tall it is, how much parking it needs, and whether any public space comes back in return. Henderson City Council and the redevelopment agency both post agendas in advance, and those agendas are the only reliable place to see the real proposal.

The other thing to watch is what happens to the 13 troops. Girl Scouts of Southern Nevada now has to place more than 100 girls somewhere. Expect the troops to split across several host sites rather than land in one building together. If a Henderson church, school, community center, or business steps up with free space, that is the outcome those families are hoping for. Nothing in the reporting suggests the city has offered a replacement facility.

Longer term, this is a preview. Downtown Henderson is not finished changing. More parcels near Water Street will be repositioned in the next few years, and each one will come with a version of this same conversation about what is gained and what is given up.

If you want a simple checklist for following it, here is mine. Watch for the remapping item on a council or redevelopment agency agenda. Watch for a demolition permit. Watch for a request for proposals on the parcel, because that is the step where the city tells developers what it wants there. And watch the park plan itself, since a reconfigured Downtown Park may end up better than the old one. All four of those move at different speeds, and all four are public.

Ryan's Take

I work with buyers in Henderson constantly, and the Water Street area has been one of the best turnaround stories in the valley. A downtown that people actually walk around is worth real money to the homes near it. The arena, the restaurants, and the plaza did what they were supposed to do. I am not going to pretend otherwise just because this particular story has a sad ending.

What I would push back on is the speed. A letter on August 17 and a locked door on September 17 is a hard way to end a 47 year relationship with a community group. A longer runway costs the city almost nothing and would have given Girl Scouts of Southern Nevada a real chance to line up a new home before the old one closed. That is the part worth remembering the next time a legacy lease comes up for review anywhere in Clark County.

For homeowners, my honest read is this. If you own near Water Street, your property is probably worth more today than it was five years ago, and redevelopment is a big reason why. But value comes with change you do not control. Read the agendas, know which agency holds the land around you, and do not assume the parcel across the street will stay what it is today.

What You Can Do

If you have space that could host a troop, reach out to Girl Scouts of Southern Nevada directly. Troops need a room for roughly an hour and a half once a week, a place to lock up supplies, and a safe parking area. Churches, community rooms, HOA clubhouses, schools, and even small businesses with a back room have all worked as host sites before. A single offer can cover an entire troop.

If you want a say in what replaces the building, go to the source. Henderson City Council and the Henderson redevelopment agency post their agendas ahead of meetings on the city website, and public comment is open at those meetings. Land transfers, remapping, and project approvals all have to move through that process, so that is where decisions actually get made. Emails to your ward representative also land, especially before a vote rather than after.

If you simply want to remember the place, this is the moment. If your family ran through that building at any point in the last 47 years, dig up the photos and share them. Henderson has a small but active group of residents who document townsite era buildings, and photos of a 1943 structure are worth keeping once the structure is gone.

And if you are buying or selling anywhere near downtown Henderson, ask about the redevelopment map before you write an offer. It takes one conversation and it can change how you think about a street.

One more practical note for anyone who leads a community group in Clark County. Go look at your own agreement. If your troop, club, league, or nonprofit uses a city, county, or district building, find out what your lease actually says about renewal and notice. A handshake that has held for decades is not the same thing as a term you can count on. Knowing your renewal date and your notice window gives you months to plan instead of weeks, and months is the difference between a smooth move and a scramble.
