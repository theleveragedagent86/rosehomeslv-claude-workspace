Boulder City's Wurst Fest Turns 30 This Saturday, and It Is Still Free to Walk In

Wurst Fest returns to Bicentennial Park in Boulder City on Saturday, September 26, 2026, from 10 a.m. to 6 p.m., and it is free to attend. This is the 30th year for the event, which the Boulder City Sunrise Rotary Club runs to raise money for Boulder City High School Grad Night.

Put simply, you can drive over, park, walk in, and spend the entire day without buying a ticket. You pay only for what you eat, drink, or bid on. For a family of four, that is the difference between a $200 Saturday and a $40 Saturday.

The day includes a car show, live music, antique vendors, a basket raffle, a kids area, bratwursts, beer, and food trucks. A live auction runs from 4 p.m. to 6 p.m. to close things out. The headline item this year is a 1929 Ford Model A pickup with a story attached to it that is very Boulder City, and we will get to that below.

What Happened

The Boulder City Sunrise Rotary Club confirmed that Wurst Fest will mark its 30th anniversary on Saturday, September 26, 2026. The festival runs a full eight hours, 10 a.m. to 6 p.m., at Bicentennial Park in Boulder City. There is no admission charge. That has been true for the life of the event, and it is the single biggest reason the thing keeps growing.

The lineup is the classic Wurst Fest mix. There is a car show, so people bring out the vehicles they spend all year working on. There is live music through the day. Antique vendors set up so you can dig through old signs, tools, glassware, and the kind of stuff that ends up on a shelf in somebody's garage. There is a basket raffle, which is the quiet moneymaker at almost every small town fundraiser in America. There is a kids area, so parents are not standing around apologizing to a bored six year old by noon.

Then there is the food, which is the whole point of the name. Bratwursts and beer are the anchor, and food trucks fill in the rest. This is not a gourmet festival and nobody is pretending it is. It is a park full of people eating sausages in the September sun, which is exactly what you want on the last weekend of the month.

The live auction runs from 4 p.m. to 6 p.m. Longtime Rotarian Doug Scheppmann told the Boulder City Review about the item that is drawing the most attention this year. A 1929 Ford Model A pickup, originally donated to the club by Vern and Darlene Burk, was bought at a past Wurst Fest auction by Boulder City residents Tony and Carolyn Veirup. The Veirups have now donated the truck back to the club, so it goes up for auction again this year.

Read that one more time, because it is the part people are repeating. The same truck is coming back around. It was given, it was sold, it raised money once, and now it is going to raise money again. That is not a marketing stunt. That is a small town where the same families keep showing up for the same cause across decades.

Every dollar the festival raises goes to Grad Night at Boulder City High School. Grad Night is the supervised, all night, alcohol free party that seniors go to after graduation. According to the club, more than 90 percent of graduating seniors attend. That is a participation rate most schools would love to have, and it is paid for by a park full of people buying bratwursts in September.

Why It Matters to Clark County Residents

Free is the headline, and it should be. Anyone who has taken a family out in Clark County in the last two years knows what a normal Saturday costs now. Tickets, parking, a wristband for the kids, two drinks, and you are into it for real money before anybody has eaten. Wurst Fest removes the gate entirely. You control your spend from the first minute.

It also matters because of where the money goes. This is not a fundraiser for something abstract. It funds one night for one graduating class at one high school, and it keeps a few hundred teenagers in a supervised building on the single riskiest night of their year. Parents in Boulder City know exactly what they are buying when they hand over cash for a raffle basket. That clarity is why the event has lasted 30 years.

For people who live in the rest of the valley, this is also a low effort day trip. Boulder City sits about 25 miles southeast of Las Vegas, straight down US 93 and 95. From Henderson, Anthem, Inspirada, or Cadence, you are looking at a short drive. From Summerlin or the northwest, it is longer, but it is still a Saturday drive and not a road trip. There is no Strip traffic involved, which in a week where F1 construction is chewing up lanes near Koval and Harmon is worth something all by itself.

There is a practical angle too. Free, long running community events are one of the few reliable signs that a place has an active volunteer base. A festival does not reach year 30 on its own. It takes a club that recruits, a school that partners, vendors who keep coming back, and a few hundred residents who treat one Saturday in September as a standing appointment. When people ask me what makes a neighborhood feel stable, that kind of quiet infrastructure tells me more than any amenity list does.

And it matters because events like this are how people actually get to know a town. If you have ever wondered what living in Boulder City feels like, a festival in Bicentennial Park tells you more in four hours than any listing photo will. You see who shows up, how people talk to each other, what the downtown looks like on a busy day, and whether the pace suits you. Plenty of buyers I have worked with figured out what they wanted by walking around a place on a normal weekend, not by looking at a map.

Background and History

Boulder City exists because of Hoover Dam. The federal government built the town in the early 1930s to house dam workers, and it was run directly by the federal government for years before it incorporated as a city in 1960. That origin shaped everything about it. The street grid, the compact historic downtown, the scale of the buildings, and the culture of a town where people know their neighbors all trace back to that planned beginning.

The other defining decision came later. Boulder City adopted a growth control ordinance that limits how many new residential building permits can be issued each year. That one policy is the reason Boulder City looks different than every other city in Clark County. While Henderson, North Las Vegas, and the unincorporated county added subdivision after subdivision, Boulder City stayed small, with a population in the neighborhood of 15,000 people. Gambling is also prohibited inside city limits, which is a remarkable thing to say about a town in Southern Nevada.

That is the backdrop for an event like Wurst Fest. In a town of that size, a single Saturday festival can genuinely be the biggest community day of the year. Everybody knows somebody working a booth. The car show is full of cars that belong to people you recognize. The auction is neighbors bidding against neighbors, in good humor, for a cause that touches almost every family in town at some point.

Thirty years of that adds up. The Sunrise Rotary Club has built a fundraising machine that runs on one day a year, and it has done it without a gate fee, without a headline act, and without turning the event into something that only tourists would attend. The 1929 Ford Model A coming back for a second trip through the auction is the perfect illustration of how the whole thing works. Nothing leaves the circle.

What Happens Next

The immediate answer is Saturday. Gates, or more accurately the park entrances, open at 10 a.m. and the event runs until 6 p.m. If you only want the car show and the food, go in the late morning or early afternoon. If you want the auction, plan to be there by 4 p.m., because that block runs right up against the 6 p.m. close.

Parking is the thing to think about ahead of time. Bicentennial Park sits close to the Boulder City historic downtown, and street parking around a park fills up fast on an event day in a town this size. Getting there earlier in the day is the simple fix. So is being willing to walk two or three blocks, which is not much of an ask in September weather.

After Saturday, the money goes to work. Grad Night happens at the end of the school year, so the funds raised this weekend fund a night that is still months away for the current senior class at Boulder City High School. If you want to track how the club uses it, the Sunrise Rotary Club is the group to follow, and the Boulder City Review is the paper that covers the town closely enough to report on it.

Worth noting: this is the last weekend of September, and the valley's outdoor event calendar starts to get crowded from here. October and November are the months when Southern Nevada finally cashes in on the weather, and Boulder City in particular runs a full slate of outdoor events in the fall. If Wurst Fest is your first trip out there, it will not be your last chance this season.

One more logistical note for anyone bringing a car to the show or planning to bid. Arrive with a plan for how you are getting an auction item home. A truck is not something you fold into a tote bag. The club handles this every year, but knowing your own arrangements before 4 p.m. makes the end of the day a lot smoother, and it keeps the auction moving for everyone else in the crowd.

Ryan's Take

I get asked about Boulder City more often than people would guess, usually by buyers who are tired of the pace in the valley and want to know if there is anywhere close that still feels like a town. Boulder City is the honest answer, and Wurst Fest is a good way to test whether the answer fits you.

Here is the real estate reality, said plainly. The growth control ordinance means Boulder City does not build much new inventory, and limited supply in a place people want to live does what limited supply always does. Listings there tend to be older homes on larger lots, they come to market in smaller numbers than you would see in a comparable Henderson neighborhood, and the good ones do not sit around. If you are waiting for a wave of new construction to bring prices down, that is not the market you are shopping in. You have to be ready when something comes up.

The trade you are making is space and commute for character and quiet. You give up the five minute drive to a dozen big box stores. You give up the newest floor plans. What you get is a walkable historic downtown, a town where the high school graduation is a community event, and a Saturday in September where the whole place shows up in one park. Some people hear that and know immediately. Others realize they want Anthem or Cadence instead, and that is a perfectly good outcome too. Going out and standing in the middle of it is how you find out.

What You Can Do

Go. That is the first and easiest thing. Saturday, September 26, 10 a.m. to 6 p.m., Bicentennial Park in Boulder City, no admission charge. Bring cash, because small vendors and raffle tables at community events still move faster on cash than on a card reader, and bring a hat. September in Southern Nevada is pleasant by our standards and still bright by everyone else's.

If you want your money to do the most good, spend it at the Rotary tables rather than only at the food trucks. The basket raffle and the live auction are the two places where the dollars go straight to Grad Night. You do not have to be in the market for a 1929 Ford Model A to participate. Raffle tickets work just fine, and there are always smaller auction items alongside the headliner.

Bring people. The car show and the antique vendors are the easiest sell for a parent or a grandparent who normally says no to a festival, and the kids area covers the younger end. This is one of the rare Clark County events that genuinely works for three generations at once, mostly because nobody is locked into a schedule. You can show up at 11, leave at 1, come back for the auction, or just stay in one shady spot all afternoon.

If you cannot make Saturday, you can still help. The Boulder City Sunrise Rotary Club takes donations and volunteers year round, and Grad Night programs at any Clark County high school need the same two things: money and adults willing to show up. If Boulder City is not your town, ask your own school's parent group what their Grad Night budget looks like. The answer is usually eye opening.

And if you are the kind of person who reads about a small town festival and starts wondering what a house out there costs, that is a normal reaction and a fair question. Go walk the downtown after the festival. Look at the streets, the lot sizes, and how far you would actually be driving for work and groceries. Then let's talk numbers.
