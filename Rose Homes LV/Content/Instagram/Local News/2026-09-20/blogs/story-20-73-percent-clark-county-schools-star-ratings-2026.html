73 Percent of Clark County Schools Held or Improved Their Star Rating

Nevada released its new school star ratings on September 15, 2026, and 73 percent of Clark County School District campuses either held their rating or moved up. Out of 353 CCSD schools with comparable year over year numbers, 49 climbed at least one star and 209 stayed the same.

That is the headline number, and it is a real one. But it is only part of the picture. Statewide, reading proficiency slipped, the number of five star schools dropped, and the number of one star schools went up. If you are shopping for a home in Clark County and school quality is on your list, you need both halves of that story before you decide anything.

Here is the good news up front: every single one of these ratings is public. You do not have to take a headline's word for it, and you do not have to take mine. The Nevada Report Card website lets you pull up any school in the state, look at its star rating, and read the numbers underneath it. That is the source you should be using.

What Happened

The Nevada Department of Education published its annual school performance results in mid September 2026. Clark County School District campuses were rated alongside every other public school in the state under the Nevada School Performance Framework, the system that assigns each school a rating from one star to five stars.

Of the 353 CCSD schools that had comparable figures from the prior year, 49 gained at least one star. Another 209 held the rating they already had. Add those together and you get 258 schools, or roughly 73 percent, that did not lose ground. The district also reported that 157 campuses now sit at three stars or higher.

Underneath the star counts are the academic numbers that drive them. High school English language arts proficiency rose 2.4 points to 51.3 percent. Middle school math proficiency climbed 2.5 points. High school science posted the largest single gain in the set, up 4.2 points to 27.3 percent. That science figure is worth pausing on twice, because it is both the biggest improvement and the lowest absolute number in the group.

Attendance moved in the right direction across every level. Chronic absenteeism, which measures students who miss roughly a tenth of the school year or more, fell 6.0 points at the elementary level, 5.4 points in middle school, and 4.4 points in high school. Chronic absenteeism has been one of the stubbornest problems in American education since 2020, and a drop of that size across all three grade bands in a single district is not a small thing. Kids who are in the building are the kids who can be taught.

Now the part that keeps this honest. On the same week, statewide data showed Nevada reading proficiency for grades three through eight slipping to 45.2 percent, down 0.4 points. That was the first drop in three years. Math went the other way, up 0.8 points to 36.2 percent. Five star schools statewide fell from about 17 percent of all campuses to about 14 percent, while one star schools rose from about 16 percent to nearly 19 percent. Nevada also debuted a new three level District Performance Framework, which rates whole districts rather than individual schools.

So the fair reading is this. Clark County held steadier than the state did, and posted real gains in several subject areas and in attendance. At the same time, the top of the rating scale got thinner statewide and the bottom got heavier. Holding steady in a year when the statewide distribution shifted down is a decent outcome. It is not the same as saying schools got great.

One more detail worth knowing about the 353 figure. That is the number of CCSD schools with usable year over year data, not the total number of campuses in the district. Schools that opened recently, schools that changed grade configurations, and schools with tested populations too small to report do not appear in a comparison set like this. So when you read 73 percent, read it as 73 percent of the schools that could be compared, which is the standard way these releases work and the same way the state handles it.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

School ratings are one of the first things families look up when they start thinking about where to live in the valley. It happens before the first showing, usually on a phone, usually late at night. A buyer types a neighborhood into a search bar, sees a number next to a school name, and forms an opinion in about four seconds.

That is exactly why the release matters here more than it would in a lot of other places. Clark County is one district covering an enormous area. Las Vegas, Henderson, North Las Vegas, Boulder City, and the unincorporated pockets in between all sit under the same school district umbrella. A family moving here from out of state cannot rely on district shopping the way they might in a metro with twenty separate districts. They are comparing individual campuses instead.

Zoning matters too, and it changes. CCSD adjusts attendance boundaries as new schools open and as neighborhoods fill in. A home that feeds one elementary today might feed a different one after a rezoning. That is not a reason to panic. It is a reason to verify the current zoning for the specific address you are considering rather than trusting a listing remark or a neighborhood reputation from five years ago.

For current homeowners, the picture is simpler. If your zoned schools held or improved, that is one more thing working quietly in your favor whenever you decide to sell. School quality is not the only driver of what a home is worth in Clark County, and it is usually not the biggest one. Location, condition, lot, age, and the monthly payment at the current interest rate all carry more weight. But ratings do show up in how fast a listing gets attention from families, and attention is what turns into offers.

Renters should care about this too. A lot of families in the valley rent for a year or two before they buy, and they pick that rental partly on which school their kid will walk into in August. Knowing how to read the actual report card, instead of a third party score of unclear origin, is a practical skill whether you are signing a lease or a loan.

There is also a timing angle that people miss. Ratings come out in September, right after the school year starts. Buyers who are touring homes in the fall are looking at fresh numbers. Buyers touring in May are looking at numbers that are eight months old and about to be replaced. Neither is wrong, but knowing which version you are reading keeps you from treating a rating as more permanent than it is. A school that moved up a star this month was the same building with the same staff last month.

Background and History

Nevada's star rating system comes out of the Nevada School Performance Framework, the state's accountability structure for public schools. Every rated school gets an index score built from several components, and that score maps to a rating of one through five stars. The exact mix varies by grade level. Elementary and middle schools lean heavily on proficiency in reading and math, growth over the prior year, English learner progress, and chronic absenteeism. High schools add graduation rate and college and career readiness measures.

Growth is the piece most people skip, and it is often the most useful piece. A school can serve students who arrive well behind grade level and still post strong growth, meaning those students are gaining more in a year than expected. A school can also post high raw proficiency with flat growth. Both of those schools can end up with similar star ratings for very different reasons. If you only look at the star, you miss that.

The ratings also went through a long interruption. Nevada paused star ratings during the pandemic years, then brought them back. That is why year over year comparisons have only recently become meaningful again, and why the 353 school figure matters. Not every campus has a clean prior year number to compare against. New schools, schools with small tested populations, and schools with data irregularities get left out of the comparison set.

CCSD is also one of the largest school districts in the country, with hundreds of campuses and a student population bigger than many entire states. That scale is why the district's numbers move slowly and why a 73 percent hold or improve figure represents a lot of individual buildings pulling in the same direction. It is also why any single district wide average hides enormous variation from campus to campus. The average tells you very little about the school three blocks from the house you are considering.

What Happens Next

The immediate next step is local. CCSD school communities will see these results discussed at School Organizational Team meetings and at school board meetings this fall. Schools that moved up will build on whatever they changed. Schools that slipped will be working on improvement plans, and in some cases the state gets involved in that planning.

The bigger question hanging over all of it is money. CCSD is working through roughly $51.6 million in cuts this year, with more reductions being discussed, while the state education stabilization account sits well above $800 million. Whatever you think about how that money should be handled, the practical point for families is that staffing, class sizes, and support programs are the levers that move the numbers in this report, and those levers all cost money. Next year's ratings will reflect decisions being made right now.

Watch the new District Performance Framework too. This is its first year, and it gives families a second measure to look at alongside individual school ratings. It will take a couple of cycles before anyone can say whether it is genuinely useful or just another number, but it is worth knowing it exists.

For buyers, the practical timeline is this. Ratings update once a year in the fall. If you are house hunting between now and next September, the numbers released in September 2026 are the current ones. Check them at the address level, and check them again if you are still looking a year from now.

There is one more thing worth watching, and it is the gap between the statewide trend and the Clark County trend. If reading proficiency keeps sliding at the state level while CCSD holds, the conversation in Carson City is going to shift toward what the rest of Nevada needs, not just what the biggest district needs. That affects funding formulas, and funding formulas eventually affect the school at the end of your street. It is a slow moving story, but it is the one behind the numbers.

Ryan's Take

I get asked about schools constantly, and my answer is always the same. I will tell you exactly where to look it up, and I will not tell you what to think about what you find. Star ratings are public data. The Nevada Report Card site is free, it is the official source, and it will give you more detail in ten minutes than any summary I could write.

What I will offer is this. Do not buy a house on a star rating alone, and do not rule one out on a star rating alone. I have watched families fall in love with a home and walk away over a number they never looked into, and I have watched other families pay a premium for a zoned school their kid ended up not attending anyway because of a magnet program, a charter, or a zone variance. Go tour the school. Talk to the principal. Ask about the programs your specific kid needs. A school is a building full of people, and no rating captures whether it is the right fit for your family.

The honest read on this year's data is a district that held its ground in a year when the state as a whole did not, with real gains in attendance and in a few subject areas, and with plenty of room left to improve. That is a fair thing to tell a client, and it is the version I give people.

What You Can Do

Start at the primary source. Go to Nevada Report Card, search the school by name, and open its report. You will see the star rating, the index score, proficiency rates by subject, growth data, chronic absenteeism, and enrollment. Compare a school to itself over several years before you compare it to anything else. A campus trending up over three years tells you something a single year's star cannot.

Next, confirm the zoning for the exact address. CCSD publishes a school zone lookup where you enter a street address and get the assigned elementary, middle, and high school. Do this before you write an offer, not after. Listing descriptions and neighborhood reputations get zoning wrong all the time, and boundaries do change.

Then go see the place in person. Call the front office and ask about a tour. Ask what changed this year, what programs they run, what the class sizes actually look like, and how they handle whatever your child specifically needs. Also look into magnet and Career and Technical Academy options, which operate on their own application timelines and can change the calculus entirely for a high school student.

Finally, show up. School board meetings, School Organizational Team meetings, and budget hearings are open to the public, and the fall budget conversation is the one that will shape next year's numbers. If your zoned school matters to your home and your kid, that meeting is worth an evening.
