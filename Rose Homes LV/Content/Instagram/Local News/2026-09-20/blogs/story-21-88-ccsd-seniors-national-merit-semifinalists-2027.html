88 Clark County Seniors Made National Merit Semifinalist, Up From 74 Last Year

Eighty-eight Clark County School District seniors were named 2027 National Merit Scholarship semifinalists, up from 74 a year ago. Those students come from 18 different high schools across the valley, and they now compete for about 6,800 scholarships worth close to $24 million nationwide.

Ed W. Clark High School and Coronado High School produced the largest groups of semifinalists this year. That is a 19 percent jump in semifinalists for the district in a single year, and it landed in the same week that CCSD was working through a round of budget cuts.

If you are a parent here, the number matters for a simple reason. It is one of the few public, outside measures of how the top of a Clark County graduating class stacks up against the rest of the country. And unlike a lot of school data, it is tied to specific campuses that you can look up, drive past, and ask questions about.

What Happened

FOX5 Vegas reported on September 14, 2026 that 88 CCSD high school seniors had been named semifinalists in the 2027 National Merit Scholarship Program. Last year the district's count was 74. That is 14 more students, a gain of roughly 19 percent, and it came without any change in how students qualify.

The 88 students represent 18 high schools. That spread is the part a lot of people skip past. The honor did not land at one or two magnet campuses and stop there. It showed up at schools on both sides of the valley, in Las Vegas and in Henderson, at comprehensive high schools and at selective programs alike.

Ed W. Clark High School and Coronado High School produced the largest groups this year. Clark sits near the center of the valley and runs a long-established Academy of Math, Science and Applied Technology. Coronado sits in Henderson and has been one of the district's larger, higher-performing comprehensive high schools for years. Both campuses have a track record on this list, so neither result is a fluke.

We are not publishing the students' names here. The district and the individual schools released them, and families are free to share them, but this article is about what the group number tells you about Clark County schools, not about any one kid.

Here is what being a semifinalist actually means. Students enter the competition by taking the PSAT/NMSQT during their junior year of high school. The highest scorers in each state move forward as semifinalists, which is generally described as roughly the top one percent of test takers in that state. [NOT VERIFIED] The cutoff is set state by state, not nationally, so a score that qualifies in Nevada may differ from the score needed in California or Texas.

From here, semifinalists have to complete an application, submit an essay, provide a high school record, and earn an endorsement from their school in order to advance to finalist standing. Most semifinalists nationally do advance. Then the awards get handed out in the spring of the senior year, which in this case means spring 2027.

The prize pool the district cited is about 6,800 scholarships worth nearly $24 million. Those awards come in a few different forms, including one-time national awards, corporate-sponsored awards tied to a parent's employer or a student's intended field, and college-sponsored awards paid out over four years by a specific university. The college-sponsored awards tend to be the largest by total dollar value, and they are a real factor in where some of these students end up enrolling.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Most school news in Clark County is about money, safety, or staffing. This one is about outcomes, and outcomes are what parents actually ask about when they are deciding where to live.

Think about how a family usually researches schools here. They look at a star rating. They look at a test score chart. They read a few comments online. None of that tells them much about what the top end of a school looks like, because star ratings are built around growth and proficiency across an entire campus. A semifinalist count is a different kind of signal. It tells you a school has students taking hard classes, taking the PSAT seriously in eleventh grade, and getting the kind of support that turns a strong junior year into a scholarship application.

It also matters because 18 schools made the list. For a district with dozens of high schools, that is a meaningful share of campuses with at least one student at the top of the state. A family looking at a home near a campus that is not Clark or Coronado still has a reasonable question to ask the front office, which is whether their school has had semifinalists recently and what the path looks like there.

There is a money angle for households too. National Merit standing can unlock scholarship offers that are worth tens of thousands of dollars over four years at certain universities. For a Clark County family staring down out-of-state tuition, that is not a trophy. That is a line item that changes what schools are realistic. It is the kind of thing that can decide whether a household refinances, takes on a parent loan, or stays put in the same house for another four years.

Renters feel this too, and they get left out of most school conversations. A family renting a house in Henderson or the southwest valley is zoned the same way an owner is. If you are signing a twelve month lease and you have a rising junior at home, the campus you are zoned to is the campus that will handle that student's PSAT sitting, their transcript, and their recommendation letters. That is worth five minutes of research before you sign the lease, not after.

And there is the plain civic value. CCSD spent the last few weeks in the news over a $51.6 million round of budget cuts while the state's education savings account moved past $800 million. In a stretch like that, it is easy for families to assume nothing good is happening in the buildings. Eighty-eight semifinalists at 18 campuses is a direct counterweight to that assumption. Both things are true at the same time, and residents deserve to see both.

Background and History

The National Merit Scholarship Program has been running since 1955, and the structure has stayed remarkably consistent. Juniors take the PSAT/NMSQT in the fall. Semifinalists are announced the following September, at the start of senior year. Finalists are named in the winter, and scholarship winners are announced across the spring.

Nevada has always been a smaller player in raw numbers simply because the state has fewer students than places like California, Texas, and Florida. That is also why the state-by-state cutoff matters. Because each state gets semifinalist slots roughly proportional to its share of graduating seniors, a Nevada student is competing against other Nevada students for that first cut, not against the entire country.

Clark County is the overwhelming majority of Nevada's public school enrollment, so the CCSD number drives the state number. When CCSD goes from 74 to 88, Nevada's overall picture moves with it. A single year is not a trend, and the count can swing on the strength of one graduating class. Still, a 19 percent jump is large enough that it is worth watching whether it holds in the 2028 cycle.

The campus pattern is not new either. Clark's Academy of Math, Science and Applied Technology and Coronado's programs in Henderson have both been producing semifinalists for years. What has shifted over the last decade is how many other campuses appear on the list alongside them. The spread across 18 schools this year is the detail that separates a concentrated result from a district-wide one.

This also arrives on top of a broader CCSD data week. Of 353 district schools with comparable year-over-year figures, 49 climbed at least one star and 209 held steady, meaning 73 percent of campuses maintained or improved their rating. High school English proficiency rose 2.4 points to 51.3 percent, and 157 campuses now rate three stars or higher. The semifinalist number is the top-of-the-class version of that same story.

It is also worth remembering how far back this test reaches into a student's schooling. A strong PSAT score in eleventh grade is built on the middle school math track a student was placed into years earlier, and on whether the reading load kept climbing through ninth and tenth grade. Nothing about this list is decided in senior year, which is exactly why the middle school feeding a high school belongs in the conversation too.

What Happens Next

The 88 semifinalists now have paperwork to do. To move from semifinalist to finalist, each student has to submit a detailed scholarship application, write an essay, document a strong academic record through senior year, and get an endorsement and recommendation from their high school. Their SAT or ACT score also has to confirm the PSAT performance that got them here.

Finalist notifications go out in February. Nationally, the large majority of semifinalists advance to finalist standing, so most of these 88 students should be finalists by late winter if they complete the steps on time. That February window is the one families should have on the calendar, because a missed deadline at this stage is the most common way a semifinalist falls out of the running.

Scholarship offers follow across spring 2027. Those come in waves rather than all at once, since national awards, corporate awards, and college-sponsored awards are announced on different schedules. Students will be making enrollment decisions at the same time, which is why the college-sponsored offers carry so much weight in this stretch.

The other thing to watch is the next PSAT cycle. Current juniors across Clark County take the PSAT/NMSQT this fall, and that class becomes the 2028 semifinalist pool announced next September. If CCSD's number holds in the mid-to-high 80s or climbs again, that starts to look like a real shift rather than one strong class. If it falls back toward the low 70s, this year reads as a good year rather than a new baseline.

Watch the budget side as well. The district is working through $51.6 million in cuts this year, and the places that usually get squeezed first are the things that feed a result like this. Advanced coursework sections, counseling caseloads, and test proctoring all cost money and staff time. None of those line items were named in this announcement, so nothing here is a prediction. It is simply the pressure point to keep an eye on when the district publishes what the cuts actually touch.

Ryan's Take

I get asked about schools on almost every buyer call, and the question is usually some version of "is that a good school." What people really want is a way to compare campuses without wading through a spreadsheet. A semifinalist count is one clean, public, outside-verified data point, and I like pointing families to it for that reason.

I would not buy a house based on this list alone, and I would not tell anyone to. A school that produced four semifinalists is not automatically the right fit for your kid, and a school that produced none is not automatically the wrong one. Class size, commute, program offerings, the specific teachers in the department your kid cares about, and whether your student will actually be happy walking in the door all matter more day to day. What this number does is give you a starting place and a question to ask.

The honest read on the housing side is that campus reputation in Clark County moves demand slowly and steadily rather than overnight. Nobody is repricing a Henderson listing because Coronado had a strong year. But over time, the schools that keep showing up on lists like this build a reputation that keeps families in the area longer, and long-tenure neighborhoods tend to hold their value well. That is the version of the school-quality effect I actually see in the data, and it is available to anyone willing to look the numbers up for themselves.

What You Can Do

If you have a sophomore or junior, the single most useful step is making sure they actually sit for the PSAT/NMSQT in the fall of eleventh grade. That is the only test that puts a student into this competition, and a junior who skips it is out for good. Call the counseling office at your student's high school now and confirm the test date, the registration process, and any fee.

If you have a senior who made semifinalist, the priority is the application timeline. Ask the counseling office for the school endorsement process and the internal deadlines, because the school's steps often come earlier than the program's. Get the essay drafted early rather than over winter break, and make sure the confirming SAT or ACT score is submitted on time.

If you are house hunting and trying to compare campuses, do the research yourself and use more than one source. Nevada Report Card publishes star ratings, proficiency, and enrollment data for every CCSD school, and CCSD posts school-level performance plans. Call the school and ask what advanced coursework it offers, how many students sit for the PSAT, and whether it has had semifinalists in recent years. These are public schools, the information is public, and front offices answer this question all the time.

One more thing worth doing, whether or not you have a student in the system. Board of Trustees meetings are public, agendas are posted ahead of time, and public comment is open to any resident. If academic programs come up as the district works through this year's cuts, that meeting is where residents get to say something about it before the vote rather than after. You do not need a kid in a CCSD seat to have a stake in how Clark County high schools perform, because every homeowner in the valley lives with the result.
