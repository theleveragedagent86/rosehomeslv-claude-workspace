Mark Stone Says He Plans to Finish His Career in Las Vegas

Vegas Golden Knights captain Mark Stone stood in front of reporters at training camp on September 18 and said something this city had been waiting to hear. He told them he expects to play more than three more years, and that his plan is to "retire a Golden Knight." That is a captain telling a ten-year-old franchise that he is not going anywhere.

Stone, 34, signed a two-year extension earlier this month that runs through the 2028-29 season at $9 million per year. The signing itself was the news last week. What he said at camp this week is different and, honestly, bigger. He did not just sign a piece of paper. He said out loud where he wants the end of his career to happen, and that answer was Las Vegas.

For a town that spent most of its history watching stars pass through on their way to somewhere else, that sentence matters more than the dollar figure attached to it.

What Happened at Camp

Stone spoke to the media at Golden Knights training camp, which is being held at City National Arena in Summerlin. The team opened camp on September 17 with 66 players on the roster, and every on-ice session at that building is free and open to the public. Stone's comments came the following day, on September 18, and FOX5 Vegas reported them.

The first thing he said was practical. He was glad the extension got finished before camp started, because it meant he would not spend the entire season answering the same contract question in every locker room in the league. Anyone who has watched a player try to have a normal year while his future is the only thing anybody asks about understands why that matters. He wanted the noise gone before the first practice, and he got what he wanted.

Then he pushed back on the retirement talk. There has been plenty of it. Stone has dealt with real injuries over his time in Vegas, and a two-year extension for a 34-year-old naturally gets read as a countdown clock. Stone's response was that he thinks he has more than three years left, and that the plan is to finish here. He did not put a hard number on it. He just made it clear that the two-year term on the paper is not the same thing as a two-year plan in his head.

The contract details fill in the rest of the picture. Stone is in the final year of a deal paying him $9.5 million this season. The extension takes over after that at $9 million per year through 2028-29. So the new money is slightly less than the old money, on a shorter term, from a player who is still producing at a high level. That is not the shape of a deal a player signs when he is trying to squeeze out a last payday somewhere else.

His teammates noticed. Jack Eichel called the extension great news for the organization, which is the kind of comment that sounds routine until you consider what the alternative headline would have been. A captain hitting the open market in the tenth season of a franchise's life is a very different story, and everyone in that room knew it.

The numbers behind Stone's case are strong. He has 396 points in 400 regular-season games in a Vegas sweater. He is the franchise leader in playoff goals with 43 and playoff points with 84. Last season he tied his career high with 73 points, and he did it in 60 games. Whatever the injury history says, the production has not slipped.

Why It Matters to Las Vegas Residents

Las Vegas has a complicated relationship with people who leave. Entertainers do residencies and go home. Executives rotate in and out. Athletes in every sport treat this town as a stop, not a destination. So when a captain says the last chapter of his career is going to be written here, it lands differently than it would in a city with a hundred years of sports history behind it.

The practical version of this shows up in the fall calendar. Families plan around a team that has a known face. Season ticket decisions, youth league signups, watch party habits, and the general rhythm of hockey season in a desert city all get easier when there is continuity at the top of the roster. A captain who is staying gives people something steady to hang the season on, especially in a year with a first-time head coach.

It matters more in the two neighborhoods where hockey actually lives here. Summerlin has City National Arena, which is the team's practice facility and also the rink where an enormous number of valley kids learn to skate. Henderson has America First Center at 222 S. Water St., home of the Henderson Silver Knights, in the middle of the Water Street district the city keeps investing in. Those two buildings are where Las Vegas hockey stops being a television product and becomes a thing local families do on a Tuesday night.

A stable, committed captain feeds both of those places. Kids at City National Arena camps are skating on the same ice their captain uses. Families in Henderson watch prospects come through America First Center knowing there is a veteran core waiting at the other end of the pipeline. That connection is not sentimental. It is the reason youth programs in both cities have grown the way they have since 2017.

There is a civic piece too. Every time a major figure in this city publicly plants a flag here, it chips away at the old idea that Las Vegas is a place people pass through. Ryan Reaves, who retired this month after 16 NHL seasons, still lives here. Now the captain says he intends to finish here. Those are small signals individually and a real pattern together.

Background and History

Stone became captain in 2021 and has been the face of this franchise through the best stretch of its short life. The team is now entering its tenth season. That is old enough to have real traditions and young enough that the people who built them are still in the building. Stone is the last obvious link between the early years and whatever comes next.

This offseason gave the organization plenty of turnover to manage. Vegas lost Game 6 of the Stanley Cup Final to Carolina in June. Three days later, on June 17, the team hired Ryan Craig, 44, as head coach. It is his first NHL head coaching job. Craig has said the team is starting over, and his camp plan includes playing both goalies, putting a puck-moving defenseman on each pair, getting defensemen more involved in the offense, and making Eichel more of a shooter on the power play.

A new coach installing a new system in a shortened camp is a lot of change at once. Keeping the captain through it is how a team absorbs that change without it feeling like a teardown. The front office clearly saw it the same way, because it moved on Stone's extension before camp rather than letting the question hang over the season.

Stone was not the only long-term commitment this month. The team also signed Braeden Bowman, 23, to a six-year extension through 2032-33 at $3.4 million per year. Bowman came up through Henderson, where he put together 62 points in 88 AHL games and was named AHL Player of the Month for April 2026. He made his NHL debut last season with 26 points in 54 games and tied franchise rookie records with a goal in three straight games and a point in five straight.

Put the two deals next to each other and the strategy is obvious. The captain stays through the back half of his career. The homegrown 23-year-old is locked up into the next decade. That is a bridge, not a rebuild, and it runs directly through the Henderson development pipeline the organization built America First Center to feed.

It is worth remembering how new all of this still is. Before 2017 there was no NHL team in Nevada, and youth hockey here was a small, determined community that had to explain itself to everyone. The valley now has rinks running programs year round, a team in Summerlin, a team in Henderson, and a generation of kids who have never known a Las Vegas without hockey. The oldest of those kids are in high school now.

That is the real context for what Stone said. He is not just a player choosing a city. He is the person those kids have watched wear the C for most of the time they have been paying attention. Continuity at that spot is how a nine-year-old tradition turns into a twenty-year one.

What Happens Next

Camp keeps running in Summerlin, and the practices at City National Arena stay free and open to the public. This is the stretch of the year where you can actually watch the roster sort itself out in person. Names to watch as the young group pushes for jobs include Trevor Connelly and Juho Piiparinen, both on the camp roster.

Down in Henderson, the Silver Knights open their own camp on Monday, September 21, with a first skate at noon Pacific at America First Center. Every one of those practices is free to watch as well, and this is the first camp under new Henderson head coach Joel Ward. Henderson closes camp with two exhibition games at the Southwest Showdown in Tucson on September 26 and 27, then opens the regular season October 3 at Lee's Family Forum against the Bakersfield Condors.

The real thing to watch with Stone is not a date on a calendar. It is games played. He said he expects more than three more years, and the extension covers two after this one. If he stays healthy and productive through 2028-29, the conversation about a third contract starts on his terms. If the injuries return, the same words get read very differently a year from now. Nobody has to guess. The season will answer it.

The other thing to watch is what the organization does around him. A captain locked in through 2028-29 and a 23-year-old signed through 2032-33 give the front office a clear window to build inside. Expect the next round of decisions, on the goalie rotation, on the defense pairs, and on which Henderson prospects get real NHL minutes, to be made with that window in mind rather than year to year. That is a different way of operating than a team patching holes each summer, and it usually shows up first in who gets a chance in October.

Ryan's Take

I sell real estate in this valley, so I pay attention to the things that make people decide to stay somewhere instead of just live somewhere. A captain saying he wants to finish his career here is one of those things. It is not a market statistic and I am not going to pretend it moves prices. But it is part of the slow, real change in how Las Vegas sees itself.

Watch what happens around the two rinks. Summerlin already draws families who want walkable parks, good schools, and something to do on a weekend that is not the Strip. City National Arena is part of that pitch now in a way it was not ten years ago. In Henderson, America First Center sits in the Water Street district, and free camp practices put foot traffic on a street the city has been trying to revive for years. Amenities like that do not show up on a listing sheet. They show up when a buyer from out of state asks what there is to do here in November and you have a real answer.

The Golden Knights arriving in 2017 gave this city a shared thing that had nothing to do with tourism. Nine years later the captain is saying he plans to stay. If you have been here long enough to remember when Las Vegas had no team at all, you already understand why that is worth more than a headline.

What You Can Do

Go watch a practice. Golden Knights camp sessions at City National Arena in Summerlin are free and open to the public, and you can walk in without a ticket. It is one of the few no-cost family outings in the valley where you get to see professional athletes up close, and most people who live five minutes away have never done it.

If you are on the Henderson side, the Silver Knights camp at America First Center, 222 S. Water St., is also free to watch, starting Monday, September 21, at noon. Make a morning of it in the Water Street district. The local businesses down there benefit when people come for the rink and stay for lunch, and that is exactly the kind of small, repeatable thing that makes a downtown work.

If you have kids who have never been on skates, this is the week to test whether they are interested. Watching a camp practice costs nothing, and it is a much easier first step than signing up for a program you are not sure they will like. Check the team schedules before you drive over, since camp practice times shift and preseason games move the ice availability around.
