Nevada Put New Strings on Data Center Tax Breaks After Schools Lost $357 Million

For years the data center argument in this valley has been about water and power. Now it has a school funding number attached to it.

Gov. Joe Lombardo signed an executive order on September 18 requiring data center developers to sign a "Nevada Community Support Commitment" before any tax abatement application can move forward.

Under the order, applicants must pay the local school support sales tax in full.

The Clark County Education Association estimates those abatements have cost K-12 schools $357 million since 2015.

The Governor's Office of Economic Development has to finalize the commitment terms no later than November 6.

Those terms cover water, ratepayers, grid reliability, siting, workforce, and transparency.

That last list is the whole fight in one sentence.

Data centers use water and electricity that Clark County households also need, in a valley already watching Lake Mead. And the tax breaks that brought them here pulled money out of the same school budget CCSD has now cut twice.

You can support the jobs and the tax base and still think $357 million is a lot to have given away without conditions.

What changes now is the pipeline. Every project still waiting on an abatement has a new document to sign and a new standard to meet. Siting language matters most to neighborhoods, because it decides how close one of these can go to your street.

The transparency piece matters almost as much. A lot of the frustration in Boulder City and the northwest came from residents finding out about a project after the approvals were already moving.

This is not the end of data centers in Nevada. It is the state admitting the old deal was too cheap.

Should Nevada keep offering tax breaks to data centers at all, or is the school funding cost too high? Tell me where you land.

Nearly 43 Percent of Clark County Homes Are Not Lived In by Their Owner

Everybody in this valley has a theory about who owns the house next door. Now there is a county number, and it is bigger than most people guessed.

County tax filing data through 2025, obtained by the Las Vegas Review-Journal, shows 332,040 of Clark County's 775,199 residential parcels are not occupied by their owner.

That is about 43 percent.

Second homes, vacation homes, most rentals, and vacant land all land in that group.

And here is the piece almost nobody checks. Owner-occupied homes qualify for a maximum 3 percent property tax abatement. Everything else can be taxed up to 8 percent.

A February study by Arbor ranked Las Vegas sixth in the country for share of renters, at 44.9 percent.

This is not really a story about investors. It is a story about a tax cap.

Three percent versus eight percent does not sound dramatic until you run it out over a decade of compounding on a property you plan to keep. That gap is the single largest property tax variable most Clark County owners never look at, and 332,040 parcels sit on the expensive side of it.

Some of those are genuinely investor-owned. Plenty are not. They are homes where somebody moved, kept the property, and never updated the filing. They are inherited houses. They are second homes people do live in part of the year.

If you own a home here and you actually live in it, it is worth confirming with the Clark County Assessor that your abatement is set correctly. People find out they were on the wrong cap years after the fact.

The bigger picture is what a 43 percent number does to a neighborhood. Owner-occupied streets and heavily rented streets behave differently at the HOA meeting and on the maintenance calendar.

Is a valley where 43 percent of homes are not owner-occupied a problem, or just what a growing city looks like?

The Boulder City Data Center Fight Just Produced a Federal Bill

Boulder City residents have been fighting a data center project all year. That fight just turned into proposed federal law.

Reusing a solar project's environmental review for a data center is where residents lost patience. Solar panels and a facility drawing water and power constantly are not the same land use, and treating a permit as transferable between them is how you end up with a project nobody in town saw coming.

The water assessment requirement is the piece with the longest reach. In a basin already asking Congress for drought money, requiring a regional water review before approval is not a small procedural tweak.

The bill has no vote scheduled.
