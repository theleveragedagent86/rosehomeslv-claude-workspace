### BODY SECTION 1

[GFX: chapter — 1 "Price and what's included"]
Let's start with money, because that's where most people start.

[GFX: compare — New home vs Resale home | median price]
The median new home in the valley is over five hundred thousand dollars right now. The median existing single-family home is around four hundred seventy-five thousand. So new costs more up front, plain and simple.

[GFX: stat — $258 | resale price per square foot, down 5% YoY | src: Redfin 2026]
On resale, you're paying around two hundred fifty-eight dollars a square foot, and in most neighborhoods that lands between one hundred eighty and two hundred twenty. New construction sits above that. You're paying for the newness.

But here's the part that trips people up. A new home's sticker price is rarely the real price.

[GFX: stat — $150 to $300 | per window for blinds, often not included | src: LV Window Coverings Center 2026]
Builders here often don't include window coverings. Blinds run about a hundred fifty to three hundred dollars a window, and a whole house adds up fast.

[GFX: stat — $3,900 to $26,000 | typical backyard finishing | src: Homeyou 2026]
And the backyard is usually just dirt. A real yard with a patio and some landscaping commonly runs anywhere from about thirty-nine hundred to twenty-six thousand dollars.

So picture this. You buy new, you move in, and you're still writing checks for blinds and a backyard. A resale home almost always comes with all of that already done.

Quick gut check. Do you have cash set aside after the down payment, or are you tapped out at closing? Hold that thought, because it points you toward one of these paths.

### BODY SECTION 2

[GFX: chapter — 2 "Location, lot, and your timeline"]
Now, where do these homes actually sit? This one surprises people.

[GFX: map — Las Vegas valley: rim vs core]
New construction lives on the edges of the valley. Think Skye Canyon up north, which only started going in around 2016, or newer parts of Henderson. Resale fills the built-out core, places like Green Valley and Spring Valley.

[GFX: compare — Rim new build vs Core resale | landscaping and services]
That location difference is bigger than it sounds. A new build gives you modern wiring, a fresh floor plan, and a young street with skinny trees and a lot of sun. A resale home in an established area gives you grown trees, real shade, and shops and schools that have been there for years.

Timeline matters too. If you buy a home that's still being built, you might wait months to move in. A resale home, you could be in within weeks.

[GFX: broll — calendar pages flipping, then a moving truck pulling up]
So ask yourself, am I in a hurry, or can I wait for something built just for me?

### BODY SECTION 3

[GFX: chapter — 3 "Repairs, warranties, and peace of mind"]
Okay, this next part is the one people skip and later wish they hadn't. Repairs.

[GFX: list — New: builder warranty; Resale: rely on the inspection]
A new home usually comes with a builder warranty. If something fails early, like the workmanship or a major system, you've got coverage and a builder to call. There's a process for it, and there's a key inspection late in that first year that's your last chance to flag warranty items.

A resale home has no builder warranty. What protects you there is a good home inspection before you buy, and you can also purchase a separate home warranty if you want.

[GFX: broll — Las Vegas summer heat shimmer over a rooftop, AC unit running]
And remember where we live. Our summer heat is brutal on air conditioners, water heaters, and plumbing. On a new home, that coverage gives you breathing room. On an older home, that's exactly why the inspection earns its money.

One thing I always tell buyers. A builder warranty is not the same as an appliance warranty. Appliances are usually handled separately and for a short window, so don't assume the fridge is covered for years. That kind of detail is what I dig into in my other video on what builders don't always tell you up front.

Now let's talk money again, because the monthly cost is where this whole decision can flip.

### ACTION / TAKEAWAYS

[GFX: chapter — 4 "Monthly costs and how to decide"]
[GFX: stat — $29 to $3,800 | Las Vegas HOA range per month | src: Julia Grambo 2026]
HOA fees in the valley run anywhere from twenty-nine dollars to thirty-eight hundred dollars a month. That's a huge spread, and it's mostly tied to amenities.

[GFX: compare — New master plan vs older neighborhood | HOA structure]
Newer master plans tend to stack fees. Skye Canyon runs around eighty-three dollars a month. Summerlin's master fee is roughly sixty-nine to seventy-six dollars depending on where you are, plus about thirty-seven dollars for the council. In Henderson, Cadence runs about forty to a hundred. Older, lower-density neighborhoods often have a light HOA, or none at all.

So new often means more amenities and a bigger monthly bill. Resale in an older area can mean fewer perks and more money in your pocket each month.

[GFX: list — Add up the real cost; Match it to your timeline; Decide who handles repairs]
So here's how to actually decide. One, add up the real cost, not the sticker. On new, include blinds and a backyard. On resale, include any repairs the inspection turns up. Two, match it to your timeline. Need to move fast, resale wins. Want it built your way and can wait, new wins. Three, decide who you want handling repairs. A builder on the phone, or your own inspector and your own budget.
