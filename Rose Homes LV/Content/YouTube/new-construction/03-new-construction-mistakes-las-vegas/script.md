KEY FACTS:
- The on-site sales rep legally represents the builder, not the buyer, and cannot disclose info that hurts the builder (Hank Miller Team; Felix Homes)
- The buyer's agent commission comes from the builder, so your own representation costs you nothing, but the agent must be registered on the FIRST model visit (Felix Homes; homesforsale.vegas; hendersonredevelopment.com)
- Las Vegas builder flooring and upgrades are commonly marked up 50% to 100% over doing the work after closing (rosehomeslv.com, 2025)
- Builders prefer to fund rate buydowns instead of cutting price, to protect appraisals on remaining inventory (Bankrate, 2025-11-25)
- A builder cannot require a specific lender under RESPA, but can legally offer an incentive for using its affiliated lender with disclosure (CFPB 12 CFR 1024.15; BuilderOnline; Silberman Law Firm)
- Builder contracts usually have no appraisal contingency and weaker inspection and financing protections, and often a binding arbitration clause (Geraghty Law Office; Homes.com)

[GFX: chapter — 1 "Who Actually Represents You"]
[GFX: transition — medium]
Let's start with the big one. The sales rep in the model home.

They are warm, they are helpful, and they are licensed. But legally, they represent the builder, not you.

[GFX: stat — Builder | who the on-site rep represents | src: Hank Miller Team]
That means they cannot tell you things that would hurt the builder. They cannot tell you the price is too high, that homes have not been appraising, or that a price cut is coming next month.

So here is how you protect yourself. Bring your own agent on your very first visit.

[GFX: stat — $0 | what your own agent costs the buyer | src: Felix Homes]
And here is the part nobody mentions. Your agent's pay comes from the builder, not from you. It costs you nothing. The builder already set that money aside.

[GFX: list — Register your agent on the FIRST visit; They work for you, not the builder; It costs you nothing]
But there's a catch that's specific to Vegas. Your agent has to be registered on that first model-home visit. Walk in alone, and the builder can cut your agent out of the deal. So the move is simple. Don't tour solo.

[GFX: chapter — 2 "Lot Premiums and Upgrade Markups"]
[GFX: transition — medium]
Okay, now let's talk money. Two places builders quietly pad your price.

First, lot premiums. A lot premium is an extra charge for a better homesite. A view, a corner, a cul-de-sac, no neighbor behind you. Those all cost more.

[GFX: list — View; Corner lot; Cul-de-sac; No back neighbor]
And it's not a line item they advertise. It's baked right into the price. So before you fall in love, ask flat out. How much of this number is the lot premium? Then decide if that view is worth it to you.

Second, the design center. This is where it gets wild.

[GFX: stat — 50% to 100% | Las Vegas builder upgrade markup vs. doing it after closing | src: rosehomeslv.com]
Here in Las Vegas, builder flooring and upgrades are commonly marked up fifty to a hundred percent over what the same work costs after you close. Some finishes get marked up even more than that.

So here's my rule, and it's easy to remember. Do the behind-the-wall stuff now. Anything that needs open walls, plumbing, or framing. Do the cosmetic stuff later. Flooring, blinds, paint, light fixtures. You can shop those for a lot less once you own the home.

Quick question for you. Drop a comment. Are you planning a new build, or leaning resale? I read these.

[GFX: chapter — 3 "The Rate Buydown Trap"]
[GFX: transition — high]
Now the one that fools smart people. The builder's amazing interest rate.

You'll see it everywhere right now. A super low rate through the builder's own lender. And it can be a real deal. But understand why they do it.

[GFX: stat — protects appraisals | why builders fund buydowns instead of cutting price | src: Bankrate]
Builders would rather pay down your rate than cut the price. Because a lower price creates a low comparable sale, and that drags down the appraisals on every other home they still have to sell.

[GFX: broll — split screen, "lower rate" on one side, "same high price" on the other]
Here's the risk. That buydown can be baked into a price that's held artificially high. You get a nice rate, but you finance a bigger balance. If values dip, you could owe more than the home is worth.

[GFX: quote — "Can a builder make me use their lender?" — common buyer question]
And about the lender. A builder cannot force you to use theirs. That's federal law. But they can legally offer an incentive for using their in-house lender, as long as it's disclosed.

So protect yourself. Take the builder lender's full loan estimate, with the APR and all the fees, and put it next to at least one outside lender. Shopping your loan can save you real money. Let the numbers tell you if that incentive is actually worth it.

[GFX: chapter — 4 "The Fine Print That Costs You"]
[GFX: transition — medium]
Last one, and this is the one I promised. The contract.

[GFX: compare — Resale contract vs Builder contract | buyer protections]
A normal resale contract protects you. If the home appraises low, you can renegotiate or walk. Builder contracts usually do not give you that.

[GFX: list — No appraisal contingency; Weaker inspection terms; Sometimes binding arbitration]
Many also have weaker inspection and financing protections. And a lot include a binding arbitration clause, which means you give up your right to sue in court if something goes wrong later. You usually don't have to sign that part, but you have to know it's in there.

So read the contract. Better yet, read it with your agent. This is exactly why having your own person matters.

[GFX: transition — calm]
Let's pull it together. Three things to do.

[GFX: list — Bring your own agent on visit one; Skip the design-center markup on cosmetics; Compare the builder's loan to an outside lender]
