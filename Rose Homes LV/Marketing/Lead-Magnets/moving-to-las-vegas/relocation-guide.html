Why people move here Start here

Most people move here for two or three reasons.

These are the ones that come up over and over when I sit down with someone who is thinking about the move. None of them is the Strip.

What is the money reason?

Nevada has no state income tax.

Nevada is one of a handful of states that does not tax personal income. If you are coming from a state that does, that shows up in your take home pay the first month, not years later.

Nevada Department of Taxation, verified July 2026

Is it really sunny all the time?

The valley sees sun on most days of the year.

Winters are mild, so hiking, biking, and youth sports run in months that are frozen elsewhere. Summers are hot and dry, and locals plan around them. The cost of that is on page 7, in dollars.

What is daily life actually like?

Suburban, and quieter than you expect.

Almost nobody who lives here spends their week on the Strip. Most of the valley is master planned suburbs with parks, trails, golf, and good food. You get a big city underneath, without living in the middle of it.

And the entertainment is there when you want it.

Shows, concerts, dining, the Raiders, and the Golden Knights are all a short drive away. It is a good town for visitors, which matters when family comes.

How far is the coast?

About four hours to Southern California.

Close enough for a long weekend at the beach or in the mountains, without paying coastal prices every month of the year.

What do people underestimate?

July and August, honestly.

The heat is dry, which helps, but it is real from June through September. Locals shift outdoors to early morning and evening, run errands at night, and stop apologizing for it by the second summer. Shade, a good HVAC system, and a garage you can park in matter more here than square footage does.

The flip side is that October through May is close to perfect, which is eight months of the year.

What surprises people the most?

How quickly you get everywhere.

The valley is laid out on a grid with a beltway around it, so most trips inside your side of town are ten or fifteen minutes and crossing the whole valley is usually under forty. People arriving from Southern California or Phoenix notice it in the first week.

Pick your side of town, and the rest gets easy.

One thing to know before you pick a spot

The valley grew fast, and neighborhoods ten minutes apart can feel nothing alike. The right area depends on your commute, your stage of life, and the pace you want. That is exactly what the next three pages are for.

Where to live  ·  The valley at a glance

Las Vegas is really seven or eight towns.

Pick the feel first. Pick the street later.

Each part of the valley has its own pace, its own drive times, and its own price range. Start with the two or three that sound like your life, then look at homes inside them.

Henderson, Summerlin, and the northwest Where to live

Four areas people ask about first.

Southeast valley

Henderson

Safe, suburban, family first. Henderson is its own city on the southeast side, and it runs like one. Clean streets, a lot of parks, a long trail system, and services that work. It feels separate from Las Vegas while still being twenty minutes from most of it.

Good fit if you want a calm suburb and you care more about day to day life than about being near the action.

West valley

Summerlin

Master planned, active, up against Red Rock. Summerlin is large enough to have its own downtown, and it was laid out around parks, trails, and golf rather than around a highway. The hiking on that side of town is the best in the valley.

Good fit if you want somewhere planned, walkable in pieces, and close to the outdoors.

Northwest

Centennial Hills

Newer, roomier, easy to get out of. The northwest is where a lot of the newer inventory sits, and where the same budget usually buys more square footage than it does further in. The 215 Beltway makes the rest of the valley reachable.

Good fit if you want a newer home and more space without paying for a central address.

Far northwest

Skye Canyon

Outdoorsy, newer, built around amenities. A newer master plan with a fitness center, a resort style pool, parks, and a large trail network. It sits near US 95, and Mount Charleston is a short drive up for cooler air in the summer.

Good fit for active families and first time buyers who want new construction with things to do inside the community.

What actually separates these four?

Age of the housing, and how far out you are.

Henderson and Green Valley are the mature side of that trade. Skye Canyon and the far northwest are the new side. Summerlin manages to be both, which is part of why it costs what it costs.

Every one of these has homes at several price points. The area sets the feel and the drive. The street and the builder set the number.

Worth doing before you commit to an area

  • Drive the commute at the hour you would actually drive it.
  • Walk it in the evening. A street reads completely differently at 7pm than at noon.
  • Ask what the HOA covers and what it costs, in writing.
  • Find the grocery store you would use every week, and time the drive.

North Las Vegas, Green Valley, Inspirada Where to live

North valley

North Las Vegas

Growing, and usually the best value. Its own city on the north end, with a mix of established neighborhoods and a lot of brand new construction. It is often where the same budget goes furthest.

Good fit for first time buyers and anyone who wants more home for the money.

Inside Henderson

Green Valley

Established, leafy, convenient. One of the older parts of Henderson, so the trees are grown in and the shopping, dining, and parks are already there rather than coming later.

Good fit if you would rather have a mature neighborhood than a brand new one.

South Henderson

Inspirada

Newer, parks first, social. A newer master plan laid out around a web of parks, sports fields, and trails, with a younger feel and a real community calendar.

Good fit for young families who want a newer home and easy ways to meet neighbors.

What it costs to live here The practical part

Four numbers that actually move your budget.

These are valley wide figures for 2026, each one from a named public source with the date it was checked. Your real costs depend on the area, the home, and how you use it.

Housing

About $490,000

The median price of an existing single family home in the valley. It is the biggest line in almost every budget, and it moves month to month, so check a fresh number before you plan around it.

Las Vegas REALTORS, June 2026

State income tax

None

Nevada does not tax personal income. Coming from a state that does, this is the one number that quietly offsets several of the others, and it shows up every paycheck rather than at tax time.

Nevada Department of Taxation, verified July 2026

Summer cooling

Around $135 a month

A typical valley power bill, closer to $80 in the mild months and higher at the peak of summer. Nevada averaged about $114 a month across 2025. Cooling is the utility to plan for here, not heating.

U.S. Energy Information Administration, 2025 average

Everyday utilities

Water about $32 to $45

Per month, on steep tiered rates that reward lower outdoor water use, which is part of why desert landscaping is everywhere. Power, gas, water, and internet together run about $325 to $345 a month.

Published utility rates and 2026 valley averages

What do these four leave out?

Four more lines, and they are all address specific.

  1. 1Property taxes. Set per parcel, so a valley wide figure is Not found here on purpose. The Clark County Assessor publishes the actual number for any address.
  2. 2HOA dues. Common across the valley and they vary widely by community. Always ask for the amount in writing before you are under contract.
  1. 3Homeowners insurance. Priced off the home, not the zip code. Get a real quote once you have an address in mind, not before.
  2. 4Vehicle registration. A new resident cost that catches people from states with cheap registration. Budget for it in month one.

Schools and family life The practical part

If schools drive your move, look at them before the house.

The home you pick usually decides the school your child is zoned for, so doing it in the other order is the most common thing I watch families undo.

How does the public system work here?

One district covers the whole valley.

Public schools across Las Vegas, Henderson, and North Las Vegas are run by the Clark County School District. Wherever you land, you are inside CCSD, and the school your child attends normally comes from your home address.

Is my zoned school my only option?

No. There are three other paths.

The valley also has magnet programs, public charter schools, and private schools. Each has its own application, its own calendar, and its own deadlines. Seats can be competitive, so this is a thing to start early rather than in August.

Do this in your first week of searching

Five steps, in order.

  • 1. Look up the zoning for any address you are serious about, before you tour it.
  • 2. Check the calendar. Magnet and charter applications open long before the school year.
  • 3. Visit if you can. Two schools with the same rating can feel completely different.
  • 4. Ask about the bus. Routes and eligibility vary by address and by grade.
  • 5. Then pick the neighborhood. Not the other way around.

What about the rest of family life?

Youth sports run almost year round.

Mild winters mean leagues and practices keep going through months that shut down elsewhere, and most of the master planned communities were built with fields, pools, and rec centers inside them. In summer, everything shifts to early morning or after dark.

Anything that trips up new families?

The school calendar, and the bus.

Start dates, break weeks, and application windows do not match what you are used to, and bus eligibility depends on the address and the grade. Both are easy to look up and expensive to assume.

Here is where I stop

I do not rate schools, and I will not steer you.

Fair housing rules exist for good reason, and a rating I repeated secondhand would be worth nothing to you anyway. Ratings also change year to year.

What I will do is point you at the district's own address lookup and the application calendars, and tell you what families in each area actually say.

You make the call. I will make sure you have the real sources to make it with.

Costs people forget, and your first 30 days Tear this page out

Two lists. Keep this page.

Nothing on the left is a surprise if you plan for it, and nothing on the right takes long if you do it in the first two weeks while you still have momentum.

Before you close

Seven costs that catch people out.

  • Earnest money. A good faith deposit when your offer is accepted. It goes toward the purchase, but you need it ready up front.
  • Home inspection. You pay for it during the process, and it is the cheapest peace of mind in the whole transaction.
  • Appraisal. Ordered by your lender to confirm value. Usually a separate cost you cover along the way.
  • HOA dues. Common across the valley, and an ongoing monthly cost, not a one time one. Ask for the number in writing.
  • Moving costs. Movers, truck, packing, maybe storage. Long distance costs more, so get quotes early.
  • Utility deposits. Some providers ask for one when you are new to the area.
  • DMV and registration. Becoming a Nevada resident means registering the vehicle and updating the license. Plan for the time and the fees.

After you get the keys

Your first 30 days.

  • Turn on utilities. Power, water, trash, gas, internet. Do it before move in day so you arrive to a working house.
  • Update your license. Check the document list and book the appointment. Walking in cold costs you a morning.
  • Register the vehicle. There is an inspection step, so look up the order of operations before you go.
  • Register to vote at the new address once you are a resident.
  • Line up everyday services. Grocery, pharmacy, doctor, dentist, vet, bank. Life feels normal much faster once these exist.
  • Drive your routes. The commute, the school run, the nearest hospital. Do it once on a calm day.
  • Explore. Parks, a few restaurants, your coffee spot. This is the part that turns an address into home.

Before you leave the old city

Five things easier to do there than here.

  • Collect records. Medical, dental, vet, and school transcripts. Requesting them from 2,000 miles away is slow.
  • Forward the mail and update the address on anything that bills you.
  • Close out local accounts that do not operate in Nevada.
  • Photograph anything valuable before it goes on the truck.
  • Keep the paperwork with you, not in the moving truck.

What is the single most common mistake?

Booking movers off a date nobody has confirmed.

Closing dates move, and on a new build they move more than most people expect. Book a mover you can reschedule, and do not hand back the keys to your old place until the new one has actually funded and recorded.

A week of overlap is cheaper than a week in a hotel.

The other one is arriving with no utilities on. It is a fifteen minute job weeks ahead, and a miserable first night if you skip it.
