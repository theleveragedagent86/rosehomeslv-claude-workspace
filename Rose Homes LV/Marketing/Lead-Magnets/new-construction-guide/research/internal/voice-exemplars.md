# Voice Exemplars: Plain-Spoken Protective Insider

**Prepared:** 2026-07-25
**For:** Las Vegas New Construction Buyer Guide (lead magnet)

**Target register:** the person who tells the reader what nobody at the model home will tell them. Direct. Specific. Names the traps plainly. Never hypes. Always on the buyer's side. Warm, not folksy. Confident, not superior.

## The 14 exemplars

### 1. The whole thesis, in three sentences

> "Buying new construction is one of the biggest financial decisions you will make. The on-site sales rep works for the builder. I work for you."

*Source: Ironwood buyer guide, closing CTA.*

**Why it works:** this is the register in its purest form. Three short declaratives, no adjectives, no hedge. The middle sentence is a fact about the reader's situation that the reader has probably never had said to them out loud. The third sentence earns the right to sell by first admitting who is on whose side. **Use this as the calibration standard for the whole guide.** If a paragraph could not sit next to these three sentences, rewrite it.

---

### 2. Refusing to print a stale number

> "I left the figures above blank on purpose. I don't want to hand you a stale number that sends your budget the wrong way. Ask me and I'll share current, area-specific costs so you can plan with confidence."

*Source: relocation guide, cost of living section.*

**Why it works:** it turns a gap into a credibility move. Most guides invent a number rather than admit they do not have one. This one says out loud that a wrong number is worse than no number, and it names the consequence to the reader, not to the author. **This passage is directly load-bearing for the new construction guide**, where a large share of internal source data is five months stale and cannot be cited. It is the model for every `NOT FOUND` in the finished piece.

---

### 3. Naming the missing disclosure without accusing anyone

> "Century Communities has not published an HOA fee schedule for Ironwood online. Because Ironwood is a small infill community, the HOA is likely modest, but you need confirmed numbers in writing before you sign. Call the sales office at 702-605-1504 or pull a title commitment to get the exact monthly amount and what it covers."

*Source: Ironwood buyer guide, fees section.*

**Why it works:** it states a factual absence, gives an honest read on it, then refuses to let the reader rely on that read. "In writing before you sign" is the protective core. And it hands over two specific ways to go get the answer, so the reader is not left holding a worry with nothing to do about it. **Every warning in the guide should end with an action like this.**

---

### 4. Explaining a fee by what it does to the reader's bill

> "A SID is a tax assessment that pays for public infrastructure like roads, sewers, and parks. SIDs show up as a separate line item on your property tax bill. The 89113 zip code is generally outside the SID-heavy zones, but you should still verify with the builder. If a SID exists, it can add $100 to $300+ per month and run for 20 years or more."

*Source: Ironwood buyer guide, fees section.*

**Why it works:** defines the jargon in one plain sentence, then immediately says where the reader will physically see it. The "but you should still verify" beat refuses to let a favorable general rule become a promise. The number arrives last, as a consequence, and it carries a duration, because a monthly figure without a term length is a meaningless figure.

---

### 5. The disclaimer that actually helps

> "These numbers are estimates only. Your actual payment will depend on your loan terms, credit score, down payment, and the final HOA and SID assessment. Always get a real quote from a lender before you sign."

*Source: Ironwood buyer guide, monthly cost section.*

**Why it works:** a normal disclaimer protects the author. This one lists the four specific variables that will move the reader's number, which turns boilerplate into instruction. "A real quote" is the plainest possible way to say "do not budget off my math."

---

### 6. Handing the reader the leverage

> "Pro tip: The $50K incentive is tied to using Inspire Home Loans on a quick move-in home. Even if you plan to shop other lenders, get a quote from Inspire first. You can use it as leverage and the bundled credits often beat rate-only competition."

*Source: Ironwood buyer guide, incentives section.*

**Why it works:** insider knowledge with a use for it. It does not tell the reader the preferred lender is bad or good, it tells them how to hold both offers at once. "Rate-only competition" is a real distinction most buyers do not know exists. This is the exact move the new construction guide should make over and over: explain the mechanism, then tell the reader what to do with it.

---

### 7. Saying out loud where the thing is weak

> "Where it trails: no internal amenities like a clubhouse or pool. If amenities matter to you, Triad Springs or another master-planned community may be a better fit."

*Source: Ironwood buyer guide, comparison section.*

**Why it works:** it names a competitor and sends the reader there. Nothing buys trust faster than a recommendation against your own interest. **The guide needs at least two moments like this**, where it tells a reader that new construction may not be right for them, or that a different community fits better.

---

### 8. Answering the commission question flatly

> "Yes, and it costs you nothing. The builder pays the agent's commission. The on-site sales rep works for Century Communities, not for you. Having your own agent gives you someone whose only job is to look out for your interests during contract review, inspections, and negotiations on extras."

*Source: Ironwood buyer guide, FAQ.*

**Why it works:** answers in the first two words, then explains. "Whose only job is" is the load-bearing phrase, because it draws the line by role rather than by character. Nobody is called a villain.

**Caution for the guide:** the flat "it costs you nothing" claim is a compensation claim that must be re-verified against current builder registration and buyer-agent compensation practice before it is repeated. Keep the sentence structure, verify the substance. See `blog-corpus-map.md`, section 2H.

---

### 9. Explaining the builder's motive, not just the builder's offer

> "Builders use deeper incentives on standing inventory because those homes have already been built and they want to move them. Quick move-in homes give Century certainty on the closing date, so they pass more savings along to the buyer."

*Source: Ironwood buyer guide, FAQ.*

**Why it works:** the protective insider's signature move. Once the reader understands *why* the incentive exists, they can predict where the next one will be. This is teaching, not telling. **Do this wherever the guide describes a builder behavior.**

---

### 10. Telling the reader what is actually negotiable

> "Builders rarely lower the base price because it affects comps for the rest of the community. You can usually negotiate for closing cost credits, design center upgrades, or rate buy-downs through the preferred lender."

*Source: Ironwood buyer guide, FAQ.*

**Why it works:** it kills a false hope and replaces it with a real one in the same breath. The reason given ("it affects comps") is the kind of thing the sales office knows and the buyer does not. Two sentences, no wasted words, and the reader walks away able to ask for the right thing.

---

### 11. Short imperative on a step people skip

> "A third-party final inspection runs $310 to $730 and is worth every penny. Do not skip this step. It catches issues the builder may miss."

*Source: Ironwood buyer guide, buying process step 08.*

**Why it works:** the three-beat rhythm of cost, command, reason. "Do not skip this step" is four words and is the only place in the piece where the voice raises itself. Save that register for the two or three moments that genuinely warrant it, or it stops working.

---

### 12. Sorting the reader's decisions for them

> "The upgrades that still make sense are anything structural (extra room, expanded primary bath, garage cabinets) and items that are hard to add later. Cosmetic items like paint and lighting can be done after closing."

*Source: Ironwood buyer guide, FAQ.*

**Why it works:** gives the reader a rule, not a list. "Hard to add later" is a principle they can carry into a design center appointment the guide will never see. Principle first, examples in parentheses, is the right shape for teaching in a lead magnet.

---

### 13. Normalizing the reader's confusion before offering help

> "Not sure which fits? That's normal. Tell me how you'll commute, what your budget is, and what matters most to you, and I'll send a short list of areas worth a look. No pressure, just helpful."

*Source: relocation guide, neighborhoods section.*

**Why it works:** the softest CTA in either document, and the most effective one. "That's normal" removes embarrassment. The ask is three specific inputs, so the reader knows exactly what a reply costs them. The offer is a short list, which is small and concrete, not a consultation. **This is the CTA template for the guide.**

---

### 14. Warning framed as a schedule, not a scare

> "Moving has a few costs that sneak up on people, especially when you're crossing state lines. None of these are surprises if you plan for them. Here's the short list so your budget stays on track."

*Source: relocation guide, hidden costs section.*

**Why it works:** "sneak up on people" is plain and slightly conspiratorial in the reader's favor. Then the second sentence immediately defuses the alarm it just raised. A protective voice raises a risk and lowers the reader's blood pressure in the same paragraph, because the goal is action, not anxiety.

---

### Bonus, for structure rather than sentence craft

> "Good to do early: If schools drive your move, look at them before you settle on a neighborhood. The home you choose can decide your zoned school, so it's worth lining up first."

*Source: relocation guide, schools section.*

**Why it works:** a labeled aside that reorders the reader's process. The guide should use this device (a short bolded label plus two sentences) as a recurring callout format for sequencing advice: *Do this first. Ask this before you sign. Get this in writing.*

## House style

### Sentence length

Measured across the prose in both source documents:

| | Relocation guide | Ironwood buyer guide |
|---|---|---|
| Mean words per sentence | 11.8 | 13.8 |
| Median | 12 | 13 |
| 90th percentile | 20 | 24 |
| Longest | 33 | 40 |
| Sentences under 10 words | 34% | 29% |

**Targets for the new construction guide:** mean 12 to 14 words. Median 13. Nothing over 25 words except a genuinely unavoidable list sentence. **At least 30% of sentences under 10 words.** That last one is the setting that actually produces the voice. The short sentences are what make the long ones land.

Paragraphs run 2 to 4 sentences. Never 5. If a paragraph reaches 5, there are two ideas in it.

Rhythm to imitate: a short declarative, then a longer explanatory sentence, then a short one that tells the reader what to do. "The on-site sales rep works for the builder. I work for you." is that pattern compressed to two beats.

### How numbers are introduced

1. **The number comes after the reason, not before it.** "A pre-drywall inspection runs about $200 to $400 and is highly recommended. It catches framing, plumbing, and electrical issues while everything is still visible." The reader learns what they are buying, then what it costs.
2. **Ranges over precision.** "$310 to $730", "$100 to $300+ per month", "6 to 9 months". A single hard number reads as a promise. A range reads as an honest estimate, and it is almost always more truthful.
3. **A recurring cost always carries its duration.** Never "$200 per month" alone. Always "$200 per month, and it runs 20 years or more." A monthly figure without a term is not information.
4. **Every estimate says what would move it.** "Your actual payment will depend on your loan terms, credit score, down payment, and the final HOA and SID assessment."
5. **Assumptions are stated before the math.** "Based on a $520,000 Ironwood home with 20% down and a 6.5% rate over 30 years."
6. **When the number is unknown, say so in the reader's interest.** "TBD", "Contact Ryan for current details", "[NOT FOUND - current figure]". The relocation guide even explains why the blank is there. **For this guide, use the literal string `NOT FOUND` and, where it matters, one sentence on why.**
7. **Never round a stale number to make it look current.** If a figure is from 2025, the sentence says 2025.

### How CTAs are phrased

The pattern is: **normalize, ask for something small and specific, promise something small and specific, release the pressure.**

Verified phrasings from the sources:

- "No pressure, just real answers."
- "No pressure, just helpful."
- "No pressure, no hard sell, just real help when you want it."
- "Ask me and I'll..."
- "Tell me where you are in the process and what you're hoping for, and I'll point you in the right direction."
- "Want me to put together a short list of neighborhoods for your move?"
- "Reach out and I will help you tour the model homes, compare quick move-ins, and review the contract before you sign."
- "I'm a text away."
- "I'll keep it factual and let you make the call."

Rules that fall out of those:

- The offer is always **a small concrete artifact** (a short list, a review, a walkthrough), never "a consultation" or "a strategy session".
- The ask is always **information the reader already has** (budget, commute, timeline), never a commitment.
- The release valve is always present and always plain: "no pressure", "let you make the call", "when you want it".
- Never a deadline, never scarcity, never "before it's gone".
- First person singular. "I'll help you", not "Ryan Rose can help you". **The blog corpus uses third-person self-reference ("As a Las Vegas real estate specialist, Ryan Rose...") because of a blog-specific convention. The guide must not.** A guide is a person talking to a person.

### Transitions used

Short, spoken, and functional. Verified from the sources:

- "One thing to know:"
- "Here's the short list so..."
- "The trade-off is..." / "Where it wins:" / "Where it trails:"
- "That said,"
- "A simple way to think about it:"
- "How to stay ahead of it:"
- "Good to do early:"
- "Pro tip:"
- "Not sure which fits? That's normal."
- "The critical detail is when..."

Note the pattern: **many transitions are labeled callouts rather than connective clauses.** That is what keeps sentences short. Instead of a subordinate clause carrying the pivot, a two-word label carries it and the next sentence starts clean.

Avoid: "Furthermore", "Moreover", "In addition", "It is important to note that", "That being said", "At the end of the day", "When it comes to".

### Banned words and phrases

**Hard bans, no exceptions.**

| Banned | Use instead |
|---|---|
| Em-dash (U+2014) | comma, period, or "and" |
| Exclusive, exclusive opportunity | great, standout, or nothing |
| Premier | top, best |
| Luxury (as a hype adjective) | high-end, upscale, or a price |
| Opportunity | option, chance |
| Nestled | located, sits near |
| Boasts | has, offers, includes |
| Stunning | beautiful, sharp |
| Pursuant | about, for |
| Utilize | use |
| Residence (for a home) | home, house |
| Amenity-rich | lots of amenities |
| Dream home | home |
| Must-see, don't miss out, act now, limited time | delete entirely |
| Once in a lifetime, unparalleled, unbeatable | delete entirely |
| Coveted, sought-after, prestigious | delete entirely |
| Oasis, gem, hidden gem, jewel | delete entirely |
| World-class | name the actual thing |
| State-of-the-art, cutting-edge | modern, new, or the actual spec |
| Curated, bespoke, elevated | delete entirely |
| Seamless, effortless, turnkey | say what actually happens |
| Game-changer, transform, revolutionize | delete entirely |
| Leverage (as a verb), synergy, robust | plain equivalents |
| Nightmare, disaster, horror story | describe the actual outcome and cost |
| Trust me, believe me | delete; earn it with a fact |
| Perfect, flawless | delete |
| "Reach out to schedule a consultation" | "Ask me and I'll send you..." |

**One source inconsistency to correct going forward:** the relocation guide uses "World-class shows, concerts, dining, and sports are minutes away." That is the one hype phrase in either document and it should not be carried into this guide. Name the venues or drop the sentence.

**Also banned in this guide specifically:**

- Third-person self-reference. Use "I", not "Ryan Rose".
- Any unsourced superlative about a named builder ("the best builder", "the highest quality"). If it cannot carry a citation, it does not go in.
- Any unsourced negative claim about a named builder. See `blog-corpus-map.md` section 2K.
- Any rate, incentive, or loan limit stated without its date.
- Blanket promises about representation being free. Describe how compensation is documented instead.

### The one-line test

Before any paragraph ships, read it and ask: **would a builder's sales manager be mildly annoyed that this is in print?** If the answer is no, the paragraph is probably marketing, not a guide. If the answer is yes, check that every fact in it carries a citation, then keep it.
