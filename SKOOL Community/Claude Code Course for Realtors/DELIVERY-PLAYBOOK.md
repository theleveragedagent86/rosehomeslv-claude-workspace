# Delivery Playbook

How every video in this course gets opened, taught, and closed. This is the on-camera half of the system. The description template is the other half.

The whole value of a fixed delivery pattern is that after three lessons the student stops noticing the structure and starts trusting it, and Ryan stops re-inventing the first ninety seconds of every recording.

---

## The first 30 seconds: five beats, same order, every level

**1. Fixed greeting.** Same words every single video. Never explained, never varied. It becomes a brand asset around lesson three.

Jack uses "Hey there, beautiful AI automator." Ryan needs his own. Candidates, pick one and never change it:

- "What's going on, leveraged agent."
- "Hey there, leveraged agent."
- "Alright, leveraged agents."

Recommendation: **"What's going on, leveraged agent."** It names the identity he is selling, it is natural to say a hundred times, and it does not sound borrowed.

**2. Name the level and the subject in one clause.**

> "...and welcome to Level 4, where we're talking about memory."

The student always knows exactly where they are in the ladder without opening anything.

**3. Spoken agenda, as a run-on list.**

> "I'm gonna show you how to pull your voice out of your own past emails, I'm gonna show you how to get your farm data in there, I'm gonna show you how to build a wiki that actually remembers your past clients, and then I'm gonna show you the one command that saves all of it."

Deliberately breathless. Not organized, not a slide read-off. Five to ten "I'm gonna show you" items in about 40 seconds. The effect is density, not clarity.

**4. The promise, as an end-state capability.**

Never "you will learn about memory." Always:

> "By the end of this one, you're gonna be able to ask Claude about a client you talked to eight months ago and get the right answer back."

**5. A physical ritual instruction.**

A state-change command. Same one every time. This is the most consistent device in Jack's entire course and it costs nothing.

Candidates:
- "Phone face down, close the MLS tab, let's go."
- "Grab your coffee, put the phone on do not disturb, lock in."
- "Shut the door, silence the phone, this one's worth your full attention."

Recommendation: **"Phone face down, close the MLS tab, let's go."** It is specific to the audience, it is short, and it is funny the fourth time.

**No cold open. No results montage. No "in this video" cutaway. No music bed.** Talking within the first second.

## Framing the promise

Two moves, used per level:

**Status delta.** Not "you'll know how to do X" but "you'll be ahead of the agents who don't."

> "There are agents in your office who have been licensed for fifteen years who cannot do what you're about to do in the next thirty minutes."

**Name the enemy in the first two minutes.** Every level has one. Say it out loud.

| Level | The enemy |
|---|---|
| 1 | Re-explaining your business to the AI every single time you open it |
| 2 | Paying a web guy $2,000 and waiting three weeks for a listing page |
| 3 | Doing the same 20-minute task 40 times a year by hand |
| 4 | AI writing that sounds like AI writing |
| 5 | The listing launch that takes a day and a half and still misses half the channels |
| 6 | The deadline you missed because it lived in your head |
| 7 | Waiting for someone to build you a workflow for your exact problem |
| 8 | Marketing that looks like it came from a template, because it did |
| 9 | Running client data through tools you have never once thought about |
| 10 | Buying more tools when tools were never the constraint |

## The teaching loop

The unit of a lesson is **roughly 90 seconds**, and it repeats:

**analogy → concept → live demo → the actual prompt, read out loud → confirm it worked**

### Analogy first, always

Every technical concept gets a physical, non-technical comparison **before** any explanation. The audience is non-technical and anxious about looking stupid. The analogy is what buys permission to keep listening.

Real estate analogies worth using:

| Concept | Analogy |
|---|---|
| Context window | A brilliant new assistant with no memory of yesterday |
| `CLAUDE.md` | The onboarding packet you hand a new team member |
| Business Brain folder | Your file cabinet, if the file cabinet could read |
| Skills | A saved search in the MLS, but for work instead of listings |
| Sub-agents | Sending three showing agents out at once instead of driving to all three yourself |
| Memory vs context | What your TC remembers vs what is on today's whiteboard |
| Model choice | You do not send your top producer to install a lockbox |
| Permission tiers | Who on your team has the lockbox code, and who does not |
| Compliance | Your E&O policy, but for the AI |
| Theory of constraints | Adding more leads when you cannot answer the phone |

### Never wait in silence

When a generation is running, start the next concept. Verbal bridges to use:

- "While that's cooking, let me show you the other half of this."
- "Give that a second, and in the meantime, here's why this matters."
- "That's gonna take about a minute. Let's talk about what it's actually doing."

### Read the prompt out loud, word for word

The single highest-value move in the whole system. Do not paraphrase what to type. Say the literal prompt in a normal speaking voice, slowly enough to be transcribed, then say "and I'll put this down below."

### Leave the failures in

When something breaks on camera, keep it and fix it on camera. Jack's NotebookLM auth fails twice and he retries. A DNS record takes ten minutes and he says so.

This is not a stylistic choice. The audience is agents who are afraid of looking dumb in front of technology. Watching Ryan hit an error and calmly fix it is what converts. A course where nothing ever breaks teaches them that when it breaks for them, they did it wrong.

## Handling setup tedium

Four techniques, in order of how much they save.

**1. Delegate the tedium to the AI, on camera.** Do not narrate fourteen clicks. Paste the setup doc into Claude and say "walk me through this, configure whatever needs configuring, and tell me anything else I need to know." This kills the "my screen looks different than yours" support load permanently and teaches the meta-skill at the same time.

**2. Teach the move, not the steps.** When connecting a tool, say why:

> "I'm doing it this way instead of just handing you the setup, because I want you to be able to connect anything. Follow Up Boss, kvCORE, Lofty, whatever your brokerage makes you use. The process is the same and then you're never stuck."

**3. De-jargon on first use, every time, with a disclaimer.** Every technical word gets a one-sentence civilian definition the first time it appears, plus permission not to already know it.

> "The terminal. I know. It looks like the Matrix. All it is, is a place you type instructions instead of clicking buttons. That's genuinely it."

Also teach them to make the AI do this: "if I say a word you don't know, stop the video, ask Claude to explain it like you're twelve. It will."

**4. Label optional steps as optional and price them.** Wherever a student could stall on a purchase or a setup they do not need:

> "You do not need this. This is a very specific use case. If you don't have a Canva Pro account, skip it, nothing later in the course breaks."

## Pre-normalize failure before anything can break

Say this in Level 0, before a single install:

> "Something is going to not work. It happens to me weekly. Here is exactly what you do: screenshot it, paste the screenshot into Claude, tell it what you were trying to do, and ask for step by step. That fixes it about eight times out of ten. The other two, post it in the community and tag me."

That single paragraph is worth more than any troubleshooting document, because it arrives before the student has an emotional reason to quit.

---

## The close: four beats, in order

**1. Recap phrased as accomplishment, not summary.**

> "Take a step back and look at what you just did. You installed it, you built the folder, you wrote the file, and Claude now knows your farm, your price points and your brokerage. That's the foundation. It's done."

**2. Status conferral.**

> "Most agents will never do this. You did it on a Tuesday."

**3. Name the next level with a specific payoff, and tease it visually if you can.**

Not a topic list. An open question or a thing on screen.

> "Which brings up the obvious problem. You've got a brain now. But how do you get it to build something a client can actually see? That's Level 2, and by the end of it you're gonna have a live page on your own domain."

**4. Fixed sign-off.** Same words every time, paired with the ritual from the open.

> "Phone stays down. I'll see you in Level 2."

---

## Assign homework with a deliverable

Not every level, but every level where it is real. One sentence, at the close, with a specific output.

- Level 1: "Before you watch Level 2, build the folder. Just the folder and the file. Twenty minutes."
- Level 4: "Pull twenty of your own past emails and run the voice extraction today. Not this week. Today."
- Level 9: "Run the four permission tiers on your own setup and find the one thing you'd be embarrassed about."

## Production standard

From the existing Module 0 notes, still correct:

**Script quality > audio quality > lighting > camera.** Buy a good mic before anything else.

Screen-share walkthroughs with light editing. Cut dead air, add text callouts, clean audio. Not raw Looms, not studio production. Professional enough to feel like a real course, authentic enough to feel like a working agent showing you his screen.

**Show real listings and real workflows.** Ryan is a working agent. That is the entire differentiator against every AI course on the internet, and it only counts if it is on screen.
