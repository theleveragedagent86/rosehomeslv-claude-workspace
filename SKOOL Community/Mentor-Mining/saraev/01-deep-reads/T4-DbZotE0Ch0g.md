## Thesis
The constraint on a solo AI automation business is not technical skill or tool choice, it is how many sales calls you book, so the winning move is a boring daily outreach streak you never break.

## System demoed
No build. It is an operating cadence: a basic Airtable CRM, a ClickUp project management board, a simple website that converts to booked calls, Upwork as primary lead source, and Instantly running always-on cold email tests. The daily deliverable is three Upwork applications per day plus cold email, marked with a red X on a whiteboard calendar in his office ("don't break the chain"). Every interested reply gets routed to a calendar link rather than a price quote. Qualification happens at the end of the sales call, not upstream. Pricing is fixed fee, 50% up front and 50% on delivery, then upsold to a monthly retainer priced off a 20 to 25 hour block that he is trying to shift toward deliverables plus add-ons.

## Who it's for
Someone with a stable full-time job, a non-sales background, and no clients, who has been telling themselves they need more technical skill or a better tool before they can start. Justin's stated background is supply chain operations, "I'm not a software engineer. I'm not a developer."

## Economic argument
All figures are the guest's own. "from starting my business in 2024, my biggest month was February this year where I crossed $20,000. It was $20,354 to be exact." First real deal: "I closed like the 5k project in a retainer" after "applying to three Upwork apps a day, every single day. And I think I might have hit that after 20 days or it was like 20 or 25 days." Pricing: "usually most my projects are fixed fee and I'll do 50% up front, 50% on delivery." Targets: "I want to hit 25K a month. I backtrack that into like say I can comfortably service or I want to service like eight clients. Like, okay, that retainer is 3,25." Nick's math on the goal: "25 a month is uh assuming you work 160 hours a month like most people try to. It's uh about $150 uh an hour or so." Nick also estimates Justin's true output at "at least $30,000 of economic just in the lob value" once the day job is counted. The environment-design payoff is asserted, not measured: "you may literally like make $100,000 more because you have something as stupid as a calendar that you look at."
